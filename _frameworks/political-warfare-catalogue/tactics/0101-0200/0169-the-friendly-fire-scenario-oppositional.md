# 0169 — THE 'FRIENDLY FIRE' SCENARIO (OPPOSITIONAL)

> Manipulating information flow so that one resistant faction believes another internal group is the source of their problems, redirecting their energy away from the actual transformation leadership.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C50 — Divide and Conquer, Adversarial Collaboration & Coalition Disruption

**Cited principles:** Akerlof, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title's military metaphor and explicit reference to 'transformation leadership' anchor the tactic in a specific corporate change context, but the underlying mechanism — engineering misattribution so that resisters fight each other instead of the actual antagonist — is an ancient maneuver visible in everything from imperial divide-and-rule to street-level scapegoating.

**Specific Interpretation.** A transformation leader, facing a resistant faction (e.g., long-tenured operators or a regional power base), feeds them selectively curated information that makes a different internal group (HR, Finance, a rival department) look like the architect of their grievances, so resistance energy gets spent on a horizontal feud rather than on the vertical confrontation with leadership.

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents the universal influence pattern of scapegoat substitution — diverting hostile attention from the true beneficiary of a state of affairs onto a designated proxy, so the opponent's adversarial energy is consumed on a target that cannot threaten the actor.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The 'transformation leadership' framing is a contemporary skin on a tactic that predates corporate life by millennia. The mechanism — controlled information flow producing misattribution of agency — is fully portable across courts, factions, families, and states. The specific reading sharpens execution; the generic reading explains why it keeps working.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The resistant faction directs its adversarial energy laterally — toward the scapegoat group (e.g., HR, Finance, a rival operating unit) — and exhausts its political capital fighting them
- **Option Y:** The resistant faction correctly identifies transformation leadership as the source of its grievances and mounts coordinated upward resistance
- **Context:** A transformation leader is implementing a controversial program and faces a resistant faction with enough informal authority to derail it. The leader controls or shapes the information channels through which that faction interprets the cause of new policies, role losses, and budget squeezes, and uses that control to attribute the pain to a sibling internal group rather than to the transformation office itself.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make the transformation agenda itself more attractive to the resistant faction — they remain unhappy. It works by misdirecting their unhappiness, not by converting it. This absence is diagnostic: friendly fire is a defensive misdirection play, not a sales pitch.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

By rerouting the faction's adversarial energy toward a horizontal target, the political and operational cost of executing the transformation (X) drops sharply for leadership — they no longer have to absorb the resistance fire themselves, defend their decisions in heated forums, or burn capital neutralizing the faction directly.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Continued resistance to transformation (Y) loses its instrumental value when the faction is misaimed — even successful 'wins' against the scapegoat group produce no concession from the actual transformation office. The resistance becomes performative noise rather than effective opposition.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Effective upward resistance (Y) now requires the faction to first untangle the manufactured grievance, reconcile with the maligned sibling group, rebuild a coordinated coalition, and only then redirect attention upward — an enormous coordination cost on top of fighting the original transformation. Energy already spent on the lateral feud is sunk and unrecoverable.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** Transformation leaders rarely need to make their agenda lovable to a hostile faction — they need the faction's resistance to fail. Friendly fire achieves this without promoting X (which would expose the leader to direct counter-fire) by quietly cheapening X's path forward, draining Y of effective benefit, and loading Y with the coordination cost of repairing the very alliances the tactic poisoned. The promotion lever stays cold on purpose: visible promotion would re-attract attention to the true source.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires informational chokepoints the leader can shape (one-on-one briefings, selective document distribution, controlled all-hands narrative); a plausible second target whose recent actions can be made to look causal for the faction's pain (HR rolling out new policies, Finance tightening budgets, IT changing access); enough cultural distance or pre-existing friction between the faction and the scapegoat that misattribution feels confirmatory rather than absurd; and the discipline to never appear as the originator of the framing. Fails when the resistant faction has direct unfiltered access to the scapegoat group (allowing comparison of notes), when an internal investigator or skeptical journalist reconstructs the information trail, or when the scapegoat group counter-attacks credibly and exposes the absence of motive.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Scapegoat Substitution (Girard's mimetic-violence redirection) operating through Hostile Attribution Bias (Dodge & Crick) and the Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross). Information asymmetry (Akerlof) is the enabling structural condition.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not change what the target wants or feels — they remain aggrieved and hostile. It changes the target's causal model of who produced the grievance. Because humans under threat default to attributing harm to identifiable agents (and to whichever agent is most salient and behaviorally available), feeding curated information that elevates a chosen proxy as the salient cause reroutes the entire downstream adversarial response. The target's hostility is genuine and energetic; only its vector is engineered.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where (a) the actor controls information channels between target and possible causal agents, (b) there is at least one plausible alternative agent whose recent actions can be made to look causal, (c) the target and the scapegoat have low-bandwidth or already-strained relations so cross-checking is unlikely, and (d) the target is under emotional load — fear, frustration, status threat — which suppresses careful causal reasoning and amplifies reliance on the most salient explanation.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An automotive manufacturer's strategy office wants to consolidate its dealer network from 1,200 to 800 stores. Rather than absorb direct dealer fury, the strategy office briefs the captive finance subsidiary to enforce stricter credit standards using language that makes the finance arm appear to be acting on its own risk mandate. Dealers organize against the finance arm; the strategy office watches from outside the line of fire.

**Sample X:** A regional dealer association mobilizes against the parent company's new financing arm, believing it has unilaterally tightened credit terms

**Sample Y:** The dealer association recognizes that corporate strategy leadership directed the credit tightening as part of a deliberate dealer-consolidation program

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active in this configuration — the strategy office does not try to make the consolidation appealing to dealers, only to prevent them from aiming at it

- C(X)↓ ✓ — the consolidation proceeds with the strategy office paying near-zero political cost, as the dealer network's litigation threats and lobbying letters are routed at finance instead

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — dealer organizing against the finance arm yields at most marginal credit relief; the underlying consolidation strategy is untouched, making the resistance effort substantively worthless

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — the dealer association has spent its mobilization budget, exhausted its bargaining chips with finance, and damaged its working relationship with the credit team — making any pivot to attack strategy leadership both expensive and operationally crippling

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** In a wartime theater, a commanding general is starving one sector of materiel to lavish another for political reasons. He routes all supply communications through the quartermaster's office, using language and signed orders that make the quartermaster appear to be the discretionary decision-maker. Junior officers, already culturally primed to despise rear-echelon logisticians, mobilize complaints, file formal grievances against the quartermaster, and never look up the chain.

**Sample X:** A junior officer corps directs its anger at the quartermaster general for supply shortages

**Sample Y:** The junior officer corps recognizes that the campaign commander deliberately deprioritized their sector to free resources for a politically preferred front

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active — the commander does not need to make the deprioritization sound noble, only to prevent his officers from blaming him

- C(X)↓ ✓ — the commander pays no morale or political cost for his sectoral favoritism; the heat lands on the quartermaster's office

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — grievances filed against the quartermaster produce paperwork, not redistribution; the officers' resistance is structurally incapable of changing what they actually want changed

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — having loudly accused the quartermaster, the junior officers cannot now credibly pivot to accuse the commander without first retracting and explaining their earlier campaign — a reputational humiliation few will accept

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Effective in any system where (a) one actor sits above two or more subordinate groups and controls communication between them, (b) the subordinate groups have means and motive to fight each other, and (c) the actor's true role can be plausibly masked behind a procedural or technical intermediary. This makes it applicable in corporations, militaries, royal courts, political parties, religious hierarchies, organized crime, and family systems with controlling matriarchs/patriarchs. Less effective in flat networks with high lateral transparency (open-source projects, small partnerships) where cross-checking is cheap and constant.

### Application Examples

- A regional sales VP leaks 'concerns from Legal' to a frustrated sales team so that anger over a new commission structure is aimed at the General Counsel rather than at the VP, who actually authored the change.
- A government minister briefs newspapers via cabinet intermediaries to direct public anger over an unpopular regulation at a regulatory agency he himself instructed to issue it.
- A university provost circulates information through department chairs that makes a new tenure-clock policy appear to originate from the Faculty Senate, redirecting junior faculty fury onto senior faculty colleagues rather than the provost's office.
- A lobbying firm coordinates with industry trade associations to ensure that activist groups attack a rival trade association — over policies the lobbying firm's clients quietly drafted.
- An NGO executive director allows board frustration with restricted donor reporting requirements to land on the development director, masking that the executive director personally negotiated those restrictions to maintain a key funding relationship.
- A family business matriarch lets resentment over an unequal inheritance plan settle onto the family lawyer, who is portrayed as the cold technician imposing the structure, rather than onto her as the actual decision-maker.
- A military commander makes punitive supply rationing appear to be the quartermaster's discretionary decision, redirecting junior officer grievances horizontally.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific and generic analyses converge on Strategy 14 (Resistance Triple): C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑. Both readings share the same diagnostic absence — the promotion lever (B(X)↑) stays deliberately cold. This convergence is itself the signature of the tactic: friendly fire is fundamentally a tactic of *not* promoting the preferred outcome, because visible promotion would re-identify the actor as the source of the disturbance. The actor's invisibility is the operating principle.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: Use the specific lens when diagnosing or designing an actual corporate transformation playbook — where information channel architecture, plausible scapegoat candidates, and the timing of grievance manifestation are the practical levers to manipulate or defend against. The specific reading is also more useful when training the resistant faction's leaders to detect that they are being misdirected — they need the concrete cues (who briefed me, where did this document originate, has the scapegoat ever benefited from the policy they're being blamed for?). When to use the generic lens: Use the generic lens when teaching the underlying influence pattern across domains (politics, military, family systems, diplomacy), when comparing this tactic to historical analogues (the scapegoating of grand viziers, the use of secret police chiefs as deniability layers), or when assessing whether a non-corporate system is vulnerable to it. The generic reading also reveals the tactic's signature countermeasure: any environment that builds direct, unfiltered communication channels between potential scapegoats and potential targets defangs the tactic structurally.

### Resolution Approach

The hardest thing for resisters to perceive is the absence of a promotion lever. When an actor seems to *not* be selling something, observers assume that actor is not the interested party. Friendly fire weaponizes that assumption — the transformation leader's deliberate silence is read as disinterest, which is exactly the cognitive cover the tactic needs. The countermeasure is therefore to always ask 'who benefits from this fight being fought between us?' before asking 'who is wrong here?'

## Friendly Fire Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Information asymmetry plus a plausible proxy plus pre-existing lateral friction — the three structural conditions that must all be present. Remove any one and the tactic fails.

**Escalation Pattern.** Phase 1 — leader seeds initial grievance with selective attribution. Phase 2 — resistant faction self-amplifies the grievance through internal venting, locking in commitment to the misattribution (Festinger's cognitive dissonance now defends the false target). Phase 3 — overt action against scapegoat (formal complaints, public criticism, withdrawal of cooperation). Phase 4 — scapegoat counter-attacks or withdraws, deepening the feud. Phase 5 — resistant faction's bandwidth is depleted; transformation completes in the background.

**Counter Measures.** (a) Triangulate every grievance against at least one source outside the suspected actor's information channel before acting on it. (b) Audit the provenance of inflammatory documents — who actually authored, who actually decided, who actually benefits. (c) Establish a back-channel with the suspected scapegoat group early; a five-minute conversation often reveals they are as confused as you are. (d) Apply the 'cui bono' test before mobilizing — if attacking the visible target produces no concession on the actual grievance, the visible target is not the actual antagonist.

**Exit Criteria.** The tactic ends successfully (from the actor's perspective) when the resistant faction is too exhausted, too publicly committed to the false target, or too operationally damaged to redirect upward. It ends unsuccessfully when a credible third party (auditor, journalist, board member, ombudsperson) reconstructs the information trail and exposes the misdirection, at which point the actor faces both the original resistance and a credibility crisis.
