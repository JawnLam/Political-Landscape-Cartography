# 0106 — SHOWCASE PILOTS

> Selecting pilot locations or teams guaranteed to succeed (often by providing them with disproportionate resources and support) and presenting these curated successes as proof of concept for a broader, riskier rollout.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#12 Cost-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C33 — Showcase Pilots, Engineered Wins & Pilot Strangulation

**Cited principles:** Kahneman/Tversky, Kahneman, Tversky, Cialdini

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a recognizable organizational artifact (the rollout pilot or proof-of-concept), but the underlying mechanism — using selectively engineered success cases as 'evidence' for a broader claim — is a generic problem of induction-from-stacked-samples that appears wherever decisions are justified by reference to demonstration cases.

**Specific Interpretation.** A change sponsor stages a small number of pilots inside the organization, hand-picks the locations, teams, and conditions most likely to yield clean wins, over-resources them, and then presents the resulting metrics as 'the data' justifying enterprise-wide rollout — bypassing skepticism about whether the program will survive outside the curated conditions.

**Generic Interpretation.** A broadly applicable persuasion pattern in which an advocate engineers a small, visible set of favorable cases — selected non-randomly and resourced disproportionately — and presents that set as a representative sample, exploiting the audience's tendency to generalize from vivid concrete cases rather than interrogate the sampling frame.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The pilot artifact is industry-agnostic and is itself just one expression of the deeper mechanism: manufacturing a non-representative sample and then trading on the audience's failure to question representativeness. The same move recurs in clinical trials, electoral demonstrations, foreign-aid showcase villages, and product beta programs. The transformation-program framing is the most common organizational manifestation but is not load-bearing on the mechanism.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Approve enterprise-wide rollout of the program on the basis of pilot results
- **Option Y:** Hold rollout pending broader evidence, randomized pilots, or independent replication
- **Context:** A transformation sponsor — typically a divisional VP or program leader — needs steering committee or board approval to scale a program (lean cell, new operating model, digital platform, performance system) across the enterprise, and has run two or three pilots that produced strongly favorable headline metrics.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The whole tactic is engineered to produce a single, vivid datum that supercharges the benefit case for rollout: 'the pilot delivered X% improvement.' Because the pilot is a concrete, narratable case rather than a forecast, it bypasses the discount that decision-makers apply to projections and lands as proof.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The pilot's apparent success directly lowers the perceived implementation risk of rollout — 'we already did it, we know it works.' Disproportionate resourcing of the pilot is concealed, so the audience prices the rollout cost against the pilot's apparent ease, not its actual subsidy. This is the tactic's signature cost-illusion move.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not directly degrade the benefit of waiting or running broader pilots. It does not argue that caution is worthless. It simply makes caution look unnecessary by virtue of the apparent evidence — an indirect effect rather than an active lever.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Once a pilot is paraded as a success, holding rollout becomes politically expensive: the dissenter is positioned as ignoring 'what worked,' second-guessing the team that delivered, or stalling the transformation. The cost of dissent is paid not in evidence-debate but in alignment-cost — opposing the showcased win signals lack of belief.

### Strategy

**#12 Cost-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The pilot operates simultaneously as evidence (lifting B(X)), as risk reduction (lowering perceived C(X) by exhibiting a working instance), and as political shield (raising C(Y) by making dissent look anti-data). The tactic deliberately avoids attacking the benefit of caution — that would invite a methodological argument the sponsor would lose. Instead it lets the curated win do the work, and lets opponents feel the political cost of standing against 'the results.'

**Effectiveness factors.** Highly effective when the audience is metrics-deferent, when the pilot's selection criteria and resource subsidy are not disclosed, and when no independent party owns replication. Fails when a skeptical board member asks 'what did we spend on the pilot per unit of outcome?' or 'were the pilot sites comparable to the rollout population?' Also fails when a prior pilot in the same organization has already produced the post-rollout collapse pattern — institutional memory of one strangled rollout immunizes the next audience.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Representativeness Heuristic and Survivorship Bias (Kahneman/Tversky), reinforced by Cialdini's Social Proof. Audiences judge whether a program will work by how well a vivid demonstrated instance resembles the target outcome, rather than by interrogating whether the demonstrated instance was drawn from a representative sample. The advocate's selection and resourcing decisions are pre-decisional invisible to the audience, who sees only the result.

**Decision Alteration.** The audience's decision shifts from 'should we believe this will work?' to 'why would we override the evidence that it has worked?' This is not a rebuttal of skepticism — it is a reframing of the burden of proof. Skeptics now have to argue from absence (the unstaged pilots that didn't run) against presence (the staged pilot that did), which is cognitively and rhetorically uphill.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when the decision-makers are several layers removed from the pilot site, when 'data-driven' is a cultural mandate, when there is no institutional discipline around sample construction, and when the advocate controls both the pilot design and the narrative of its results. Weakens sharply in cultures with strong replication norms (clinical trials with pre-registration; engineering with statistical process control) or where an adversarial review function actually examines the sampling frame.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A digital health vendor and an internal sponsor jointly select three flagship hospitals with strong IT teams, young clinicians, and recently refreshed EHR contracts. The vendor's customer success team is on-site daily for the pilot. Documentation time drops 40%. The number is taken to the joint executive committee as proof.

**Sample X:** Greenlight a national pharmaceutical's enterprise-wide deployment of an AI-assisted documentation tool

**Sample Y:** Mandate a randomized multi-site trial before deployment, including sites with weaker IT support and older clinician populations

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The 40% number becomes the single quoted statistic in the deck. It is not a forecast, it is described as 'realized,' which carries a different cognitive weight even though it is unrepresentative.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The on-site vendor support — a transient subsidy not available at scale — is folded silently into the pilot baseline, so rollout cost appears lower than it can be.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The tactic does not argue that randomized trials are bad. It just makes them look slow and redundant in the face of 'what already worked.'

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — A committee member proposing a randomized multi-site trial is positioned as delaying patient benefit and second-guessing colleagues who 'already showed this works.'

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A grant program selects three demonstration counties with low unemployment, engaged local employers, and existing community-college partnerships. Each receives roughly four times the per-capita support of standard counties. Reported job-placement gains are presented to congressional appropriators as the policy's evidence base for national expansion.

**Sample X:** A federal department adopts a workforce-development model nationally based on three demonstration counties

**Sample Y:** Maintain the existing block-grant approach pending controlled evaluation across heterogeneous labor markets

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The placement statistics from the three counties become the program's signature outcome metric, used in testimony, op-eds, and budget documents.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Because the demonstration counties were over-resourced, the cost-per-placement appears favorable; the budget projection for national rollout uses a per-county cost that under-prices the actual support model.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The status quo block-grant model is not attacked on its merits. It is simply allowed to look unproven next to the photogenic demonstration.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — An appropriator who calls for randomized evaluation is positioned as obstructing a 'proven' program — politically costly in a constituent-facing setting where the local newspaper has already profiled the demonstration county.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** This pattern recurs anywhere a population-level decision is justified by reference to a small set of demonstration cases that the advocate selected and resourced: corporate transformation rollouts, government program expansions, foreign-aid showcase villages, vendor reference-customer programs, pharmaceutical early-access cohorts, charter-school flagship campuses, and political party 'model districts.' Less effective in domains with mandatory pre-registration, blinded evaluation, or hostile-review processes — but those domains remain a minority of decision contexts.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A consulting firm uses three flagship clients — each given partner-level attention and customized analytics — as the case-study reference base for an enterprise practice it is trying to scale.
- Government: A federal job-training program funds three pilot states with above-average labor-market conditions and additional implementation funding, then cites their placement rates in budget testimony for national expansion.
- Foreign aid / NGO: A development agency builds two 'model villages' with concentrated investment in roads, schools, and clinics, then brings donors and journalists to them as evidence the wider program approach works.
- Sales / SaaS: A vendor builds a reference-customer roster from three early customers who received free implementation services and dedicated engineering support, and presents their outcomes as the typical customer ROI in prospect decks.
- Academic: A pedagogical innovation is piloted in three honors sections taught by its inventor, then proposed for the full undergraduate curriculum on the basis of the honors-section outcomes.
- Military / political: A counterinsurgency program runs intensively in two showcase districts with extra forces and reconstruction funds, and the resulting stability metrics are cited as evidence the broader strategy is working.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific organizational reading and the generic persuasion reading converge on a single insight: the tactic's force comes not from the pilot's results but from the audience's failure to interrogate the sampling frame. In both readings, the advocate's most consequential decisions — which sites, which teams, what level of subsidy — happen before any data is collected, and remain invisible at the point of decision. The pilot is not a test; it is a manufactured exhibit.

### Strategic Implications

For the practitioner deploying the tactic, the operational discipline is to keep the selection criteria and subsidy structure out of the headline narrative and to time the rollout decision before the pilot can be 'broken' by an unfavorable site. For the practitioner defending against it, the single most diagnostic question is not about the pilot's outcome — which will be strong by construction — but about the counterfactual: 'What sites were considered and rejected, and what made the chosen ones different from the rollout population?' Boards that institutionalize that question lose the ability to be moved by showcase pilots; boards that treat the curated number as data remain vulnerable. This sits adjacent to PERFORMANCE METRIC MANIPULATION (which redefines the measure) but is upstream of it: SHOWCASE PILOTS does not corrupt the metric, it corrupts the sample, which is harder to detect because the metric itself is honestly reported.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when the decision is a rollout/scale question inside a known organization with named pilot sites — there the analysis points to interrogating subsidy, selection, and timing. Use the generic lens when the question is broader (national policy, vendor evaluation, NGO program approval) — there the analysis points to the underlying representativeness failure and the need for randomized or adversarial replication before scale.
