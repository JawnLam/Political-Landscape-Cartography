# 0137 — THE 'TEMPORARY' SOLUTION TRAP

> Implementing a controversial change under the guise of a temporary fix for a crisis, knowing that organizational inertia will make it permanent once the immediate pressure subsides.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C20 — Incrementalism, Normalization & Boiling-Frog Drift

**Cited principles:** Kahneman, Cialdini, Thaler, Samuelson & Zeckhauser, Samuelson, Zeckhauser

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a concrete situational frame ('a temporary fix for a crisis') but the engine of the tactic — the asymmetry between the political cost of installing a measure and the political cost of removing it once installed — is a perfectly general property of status quo bias and the endowment effect. The crisis frame is interchangeable; the inertia trap is not.

**Specific Interpretation.** A change agent uses an active crisis (security breach, regulatory scare, financial shock, pandemic, executive scandal) as cover to install a controversial measure that would have been blocked under normal scrutiny, then relies on organizational inertia, sunk-cost reasoning, and the friction of explicit reversal to make 'temporary' permanent.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any decision system in which the bar for installing a provisional change is lower than the bar for actively removing it can be exploited by initially framing a desired permanent change as provisional. The mechanism does not require a crisis — only that 'do nothing' becomes the path of least resistance once the measure is in place.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The crisis-cover framing is one powerful instantiation, but the underlying lever — exploiting the asymmetry between activation energy and de-activation energy of policy — generalizes to any context where defaults stick. Status quo bias and the endowment effect are why this tactic works, not the specific crisis; the crisis just lowers the activation energy enough to get the measure installed.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The controversial measure (surveillance system, expanded executive authority, restricted approval process, austerity policy, hiring freeze) is installed under emergency framing and then quietly persists past the crisis as the new operating baseline
- **Option Y:** Decision-makers sunset the measure on schedule, returning the organization to its pre-crisis state, and require a full deliberative process if the measure is to be re-adopted
- **Context:** A crisis (real or amplified) creates time pressure and emotional urgency that compresses normal deliberation; the actor proposes a 'temporary' measure framed as proportionate to the immediate threat, with the implicit or explicit promise of review or expiration that will, in practice, not happen because no one will be incentivized to fight to remove it later

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not argue that the measure is intrinsically good — it deliberately avoids that argument because the measure is controversial and would lose on merits. The 'temporary' framing is a way to bypass the benefit debate entirely, not to win it.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the entire engine of the installation phase. The 'temporary' label collapses the political, procedural, and reputational cost of approving the measure — review boards waive normal scrutiny, opposition stands down because the change is 'just for now,' sunset clauses substitute for real debate, and decision-makers preserve face by treating their approval as a crisis-pragmatic exception rather than an endorsement. Almost every defense the tactic relies on routes through this lever.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not attack the merits of returning to the pre-crisis state. In fact, it implicitly concedes those merits by promising the measure is temporary. The trick is to never let Y get formally evaluated — not to argue against it.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Operates strongly but only in the post-installation phase. Once the measure is in place, removal requires: (a) someone to actively spend political capital to reopen the file, (b) operational disruption to undo the now-embedded process, (c) implicit admission that the crisis-era leaders were wrong to install it, (d) reabsorbing whatever risk the measure was nominally guarding against. Each of these is a tax on Y that did not exist before installation.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic wins by exploiting a temporal asymmetry in the two costs. During the crisis, C(X) is artificially suppressed by the urgency frame; after the crisis, C(Y) is naturally inflated by the inertia of the installed measure. The actor never has to argue the measure is good — they only have to bridge the moment when scrutiny is lowest, after which the burden of action flips to anyone wanting to remove it.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires a plausible crisis frame, an organization that under-staffs sunset enforcement (review committees that never meet, expiration dates that no one tracks), and a measure whose operational footprint quickly becomes load-bearing (workflows route through it, dependencies form, junior staff onboard not knowing it was temporary). Fails when an opposing actor formally calendars the sunset review, treats the expiration as a default action requiring no decision, or when the measure is too visibly disruptive to fade into the operational background.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Status Quo Bias (Samuelson & Zeckhauser, 1988) compounded by the Endowment Effect (Thaler/Kahneman) and reinforced by Cialdini's Commitment & Consistency Principle. Once a measure is in place, the cognitive default shifts to keeping it; reversing requires an explicit decision, while drift requires none.

**Decision Alteration.** Reframes the temporal locus of the controversial choice. Rather than presenting the target with 'install permanently, yes or no?' (where 'no' is easy), the tactic presents 'install temporarily, yes or no?' (where 'yes' is easy) and then, later, 'actively remove the existing measure, yes or no?' (where 'no' — meaning keep it — is again easy). The actor wins both votes without ever winning the actual question.

**Optimal Conditions.** Decision systems with asymmetric procedural costs for action vs. inaction — bureaucracies where new policies need full review but existing ones renew silently, legislatures where sunset clauses lapse without re-debate, organizations where 'who is responsible for un-doing this?' has no clear owner. Most effective when the proposing actor controls operational implementation, so the measure can quickly accumulate dependencies that make removal expensive.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** Post-9/11 PATRIOT Act-style legislation passed in days under crisis framing, with sunset clauses that have been rolled forward through successive reauthorizations because each renewal vote is framed as 'continuing existing protections' rather than 'enacting controversial surveillance powers'

**Sample X:** A national legislature renews 'temporary' anti-terror surveillance powers indefinitely, twenty years past the crisis that prompted them

**Sample Y:** The sunset clause is treated as a hard default; powers expire automatically and must be re-justified from scratch through normal lawmaking process

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Legislators are not asked to affirm that the surveillance is good policy on the merits — they are asked whether to disrupt 'existing' arrangements during a renewal vote, which is a different and lower-bar question

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Original passage framed as emergency response, suppressing normal cost of voting for controversial surveillance authority; renewal votes framed as administrative continuity, suppressing the cost again

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Tactic does not argue against the value of expiration and sunset review — it just engineers around them procedurally

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Letting the measure expire now requires affirmatively voting against 'national security tools the country has relied on for two decades,' a vote no incumbent wants to cast in an election cycle

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Academic department adopted remote-only admissions in 2020 as a 'temporary' COVID accommodation; faculty who preferred this change (saving travel costs, expanding applicant pool from regions previously underrepresented) never put reinstatement on the agenda, and by the time anyone proposed restoring campus visits, an entire admitted cohort had matriculated under the new process with no apparent harm

**Sample X:** A university's pandemic-era remote-only PhD admissions process becomes the permanent norm three years after the pandemic ends, eliminating campus visits that had been used to assess fit

**Sample Y:** The pre-pandemic in-person interview component is formally reinstated by departmental vote

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — No faculty meeting was ever held to argue remote-only admissions is superior on the merits — that argument would have been contested. The tactic worked precisely because it never had to be made.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Pandemic justification collapsed the cost of dropping in-person interviews to zero — opposing the change in 2020 would have looked tone-deaf to public health concerns

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The benefits of in-person assessment (gut-check fit, candidate seeing the lab, informal conversations) are not disputed — they are simply never re-invoked in a forum where a decision could be made

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Reinstating campus visits now requires someone to argue against an admissions process that 'works fine,' commit travel funds back into the budget, and implicitly criticize the cohort admitted under the new process

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies to any decision system with asymmetric action/inaction costs — national legislation, corporate policy, academic governance, military authorization, regulatory rulemaking, household budgeting. Less effective in systems with strong sunset discipline (e.g., zero-based budgeting cultures, regulations with statutorily-enforced expiration review, agile teams that audit and prune their own processes), or where the measure is so visibly costly that it cannot fade into operational background.

### Core Strategy

### Application Examples

- Corporate hiring freeze imposed during a quarterly revenue miss that quietly persists for three years, becoming the de facto headcount strategy without any board ever voting to make it permanent
- Government emergency war powers granted to the executive that remain on the books decades after the conflict ends, used for policy purposes the original authorization never contemplated
- Activist coalition agrees to a 'temporary' tactical concession during a fragile negotiation that becomes the new floor of demands in subsequent talks, ratcheting the movement's position permanently
- Family business installs the founder's son as 'interim' CEO during the founder's illness, and the lack of any formal succession search process means he remains CEO a decade later
- Diplomatic agreement establishes a 'provisional' demilitarized zone or refugee camp that becomes a permanent territorial fact because dismantling it would require an active diplomatic decision no party wants to make
- Religious institution adopts an 'emergency' lay-leadership arrangement during a clergy shortage, and parishioners come to prefer it, making restoration of traditional structures politically impossible later

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both analyses converge on Strategy 8 (Cost Contrast), but they illuminate different phases of its operation. The specific analysis emphasizes installation-phase Cost Contrast: the crisis suppresses C(X) sharply while C(Y) is not yet relevant because the measure does not yet exist. The generic analysis emphasizes the temporal trick: the tactic depends on the C(Y) lever activating later, as inertia and dependencies accrete, in a way the original decision-makers never explicitly authorized.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: When diagnosing a single controversial measure that was installed during an actual crisis and is now being treated as baseline — useful for naming what happened, surfacing the sunset clause that was supposed to fire, and producing a forcing function for re-review. Practitioners running counter-tactics (e.g., 'this expires on date X and reverting is the default') need the specific lens to make the original deal visible. When to use the generic lens: When designing decision systems prospectively to be resistant to the trap — sunset discipline, default-reversion rules, mandatory affirmative re-authorization, separation of installation authority from removal authority. Also useful when the 'crisis' is manufactured or amplified rather than real, because the generic frame exposes that the crisis was a vehicle for the asymmetry, not the actual mechanism.

### Resolution Approach

The tactic exploits a structural property of most decision systems that practitioners rarely notice until it is used against them: the activation energy required to install a policy is almost never equal to the activation energy required to remove it. The single most effective counter is not arguing the measure is bad — that argument was deliberately routed around — but installing the procedural rule that the measure's expiration requires no decision, while its continuation does. Flip the asymmetry and the tactic collapses.

## Temporary Solution Trap Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Two-phase asymmetric cost play. Phase 1 (installation): crisis frame artificially compresses C(X), enabling adoption that would fail on merits. Phase 2 (entrenchment): operational dependencies accrete, sunset review either fails to occur or occurs under 'continuity bias' framing, C(Y) silently inflates. The actor's only active work is at the seam between phases — ensuring the sunset never fires and the measure quietly absorbs into operational baseline.

**Escalation Pattern.** Each successful application teaches the actor that the tactic works, increasing willingness to use it again with thinner crisis pretexts. Organizations that fall for it once tend to fall for it repeatedly because the structural vulnerability (weak sunset enforcement, no clear owner for un-doing) was not fixed by the experience.

**Counter Measures.** (1) Calendarize the sunset review as an automatic agenda item with an assigned owner who is not the original implementer. (2) Default-revert rules: at expiration, the measure must be affirmatively re-authorized or it dies. (3) Decouple installation authority from removal authority — the body that installed the 'temporary' measure does not get to renew it. (4) Pre-commitment device: at installation, require the implementer to specify in writing what will trigger removal, with that text re-read at every renewal vote. (5) Periodic 'zero-based' policy audits where every existing measure must justify itself from scratch.

**Exit Criteria.** The trap is sprung once the measure has survived one sunset review cycle without explicit re-debate, or once operational dependencies are deep enough that removal requires more political capital than the original installation. After roughly 12-24 months of normal operation post-crisis, the tactic is effectively complete and the measure has become permanent.
