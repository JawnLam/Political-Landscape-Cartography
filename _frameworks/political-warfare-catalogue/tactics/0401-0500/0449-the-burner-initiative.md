# 0449 — THE 'BURNER' INITIATIVE

> A project designed to fail spectacularly, created for the express purpose of justifying the removal of the executive in charge of it.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C46 — Setting Up to Fail (Impossible Mandates, Glass Cliffs & Burner Initiatives)

**Cited principles:** Kahneman/Tversky, Kahneman, Tversky, Pfeffer, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description names a concrete organizational artifact — a project — but the artifact is a fiction whose only real purpose is producing a documented failure. The tactic thus sits between a specific HR/governance maneuver (engineering grounds for executive dismissal) and the abstract pattern of fabricating a pretext to legitimize a predetermined punitive action.

**Specific Interpretation.** A senior actor (CEO, board faction, dominant coalition) commissions a high-visibility initiative under a targeted executive, structured so failure is overdetermined — unrealistic scope, inadequate funding, hostile dependencies, moving acceptance criteria — so the executive's eventual ouster can be presented as a performance decision rather than a political one.

**Generic Interpretation.** The general strategic pattern of manufacturing observable failure to justify a removal, restriction, or restructuring that the actor already intends but cannot publicly defend on the real grounds. The 'project' is just the visible substrate; the pattern recurs anywhere a decision-maker needs cover for an unpopular elimination.

**Primary Classification.** specific

**Reasoning.** What makes this a Burner Initiative — not merely a tough assignment, a glass cliff, or an impossible mandate — is the prior intent: the project exists for the removal, not as a genuine bet that happens to be hard. Strip the executive-elimination purpose and the tactic dissolves into its siblings. The specific organizational context (a documented project owned by a target executive, evaluated by the same actor who scoped it) is constitutive, not incidental. The generic pretext-manufacturing pattern is real and reusable, but it is the abstraction of this tactic, not its core.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The board (or CEO, or senior coalition) terminates the target executive on documented performance grounds and reallocates their portfolio
- **Option Y:** The target executive remains in place with their current standing, mandate, and access to resources
- **Context:** A senior decision-maker has already decided the target executive must go but lacks defensible cause — the executive has allies, contractual protections, a clean record, or a constituency that would treat a naked political firing as a provocation. A new strategic-sounding initiative is launched under the executive's ownership, engineered with scope, timeline, dependencies, and success criteria the actor knows will produce a visible, documented failure within one to three review cycles.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not enhance the inherent appeal of removing the executive — it doesn't make their successor more attractive or paint a vision of the post-removal organization. The removal is sold as remedial, not aspirational. Trying to amplify benefits of X here would expose the predetermined intent.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The manufactured failure dramatically reduces the political and reputational cost of firing. Without the Burner, terminating the executive would require either fabricated cause (legally and politically dangerous) or open acknowledgment of the political motive (relationally and culturally expensive). The documented project failure provides the actor with a clean, performance-coded narrative — the cost of executing the removal is laundered through a paper trail.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the core operating lever. The executive's track record, judgment, and standing — all the things that make keeping them valuable — are systematically devalued by the visible failure. Prior wins are recontextualized as flukes; the executive's strategic credibility evaporates because they 'just shipped this disaster.' Availability bias does the work: the failure becomes the most recent, most vivid evidence about the executive, dominating any longer record.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Keeping the executive becomes actively expensive for everyone in the chain of accountability. Other board members, sponsors, or peers who advocate retention now must defend tolerating documented failure — they inherit the reputational stain. The decision-maker can credibly say 'this is what accountability looks like'; defenders are positioned as protecting underperformance. Refusal to remove looks like nepotism or political cowardice.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The actor does not need to sell the executive's removal as a positive vision; they need to make retention unsustainable while quietly providing the cover that makes removal executable. The manufactured failure simultaneously destroys the perceived value of keeping the executive (their judgment is suspect, their record contaminated), raises the political cost of any defender (allies must now eat the failure to keep them), and lowers the cost of executing the removal itself (the failure record launders what would otherwise be a politically explosive firing into a routine performance dismissal). The conspicuous absence of B(X)↑ is diagnostic — promoting the removal as aspirational would expose the predetermined intent. This is the signature of Strategy 14: quietly facilitate the preferred outcome, fully attack the alternative on both axes, never sell the upside.

**Effectiveness factors.** Success depends on five conditions: (1) the actor controls both the project's scoping and its evaluation, so failure can be both engineered and certified; (2) the project is plausible enough at launch that the executive cannot decline it without appearing risk-averse or insubordinate; (3) the executive lacks the political intelligence or capital to expose the setup before the verdict lands; (4) the organizational culture treats project outcomes as legitimate performance evidence rather than reading the scoping itself as the political act; and (5) the timeline runs long enough to look like a fair shot but short enough to deny the executive recovery. Common failure modes: the project accidentally succeeds (the executive turns out to be more capable than scoped for); the executive recognizes the trap early and publicly documents the impossibilities, converting their dismissal into a martyrdom narrative; or a third-party investigation surfaces the scoping decisions and attributes the failure to the architect rather than the operator.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer's organizational power & pretext manufacturing, reinforced by Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross) and Availability Bias (Kahneman/Tversky). Observers attribute project failure to the operator's character and competence rather than the architect's scoping choices, and the recent, vivid failure dominates their assessment of the operator's overall record.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic exploits the asymmetry between visible outcomes and invisible scoping. The audience evaluates the operator on the failure they can see, not on the structural impossibility they cannot. By manufacturing observable failure, the actor shifts the question from 'should this person be removed?' (which the audience would resist without cause) to 'why is this person still here after such a visible disaster?' (which the audience now feels obligated to answer). The decision frame is rewritten without any audience member noticing the rewriter.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in environments with three features: outcome-based evaluation cultures that don't audit scoping decisions; concentrated authority to assign and evaluate projects; and a target with insufficient political intelligence to detect setup or insufficient capital to expose it. Less effective where independent governance functions audit project-failure root causes, where the target has strong external constituencies that can reframe the narrative, or where organizational memory holds the scoper accountable for what they scoped.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A newly elected administration wants to remove a holdover agency director appointed by the prior administration but lacks legal cause. The administration tasks the director with implementing a flagship reform — assigned aggressive deadlines, mandated to coordinate with hostile sister agencies, and given budget authority that requires sign-off from a political opponent. The reform predictably stalls; the director is removed for 'failure to execute the President's priorities.'

**Sample X:** The governing board terminates the agency head on grounds of program failure

**Sample Y:** The agency head retains their post with credibility intact

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Removal is framed as accountability, not as opening space for a better leader — the administration deliberately avoids elevating the successor, since making the move look aspirational would reveal the political motive.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The documented programmatic failure converts what would be a politically explosive firing into a routine performance dismissal — the administration can point to the record.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The director's prior agency record becomes irrelevant once they own a high-visibility flagship failure; their professional standing is contaminated by the most recent visible outcome.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Anyone in Congress or the press who defends retention must now defend tolerating the failure itself — the political cost of advocacy for the director rises sharply.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A faction at a professional services firm wants to push out a partner whose origination has slipped but whose long-tenure equity stake and personal client relationships make a direct vote politically expensive. The managing committee 'recognizes the partner's capabilities' by handing them a complex pro-bono engagement with a difficult institutional client, no junior staffing, and a fixed deadline tied to the client's litigation calendar. When the engagement under-delivers, the committee cites the failure in the next compensation review and forces an equity buyout.

**Sample X:** The senior partners eject the targeted partner from the firm on capacity grounds

**Sample Y:** The targeted partner retains equity and client portfolio

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The faction does not need to promote the partner's exit as improving the firm; the failure-driven buyout is presented as routine performance management.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The documented engagement failure provides the compensation committee with cover for an action that would otherwise look like a politically motivated equity grab.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The partner's long-standing reputation as a steady operator is overwritten by the visible engagement failure — the most recent data point dominates partner assessment.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Allied partners who would normally defend the targeted partner's tenure must now defend tolerating the under-delivered engagement — the cost of loyalty becomes visible and personal.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable wherever an actor can assign and evaluate work on a target — corporate, government, academic, military, religious, NGO — and where outcome-based assessment cultures allow the architect's scoping to remain invisible. Most powerful in environments with concentrated authority and weak independent governance; less effective in environments with structural audit functions (independent inspectors general, external program reviews, peer-elected governance) that can interrogate scoping decisions retrospectively.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CEO assigns a rival division head a high-visibility digital transformation with an unrealistic 12-month timeline and matrixed dependencies on uncooperative business units, then uses the predictable stall as grounds for reorganizing the rival's division under loyalists.
- Government: An incoming administration tasks a career senior executive with implementing a controversial mandate the administration knows the bureaucracy will resist, then cites implementation failure to push for the executive's reassignment or retirement.
- Academic: A department chair assigns a tenured but uncooperative faculty member to lead a new interdisciplinary program with insufficient cross-departmental commitments, using the program's eventual collapse to argue for the faculty member's removal from administrative roles.
- Military: A senior commander assigns a politically inconvenient subordinate to lead a deployment with inadequate logistical support, using the deployment's documented underperformance to justify their early rotation or career-ending evaluation.
- NGO: A board faction commissions the executive director to lead a high-profile capital campaign with unrealistic targets and limited board fundraising support, then uses the campaign shortfall as grounds for terminating the director without admitting the underlying boardroom conflict.
- Family business: A patriarch assigns a non-favored child to launch a new venture with constrained capital and hostile family-network dependencies, using the venture's failure to justify excluding that child from succession in favor of a preferred sibling.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The Burner Initiative is the engineered-failure variant of the broader 'manufactured pretext' family. Where REPUTATION ASSASSINATION attacks the person directly through reputational moves, the Burner attacks the person obliquely through their work — making the work itself the indictment. It differs from its cluster siblings in a critical dimension: the IMPOSSIBLE MANDATE may be assigned without prior intent to remove (the target is set up to fail but the outcome is contingent on actual failure); the GLASS CLIFF puts a target into a real crisis they may or may not survive; the GHOST DELIVERABLE wastes the target's effort without necessarily ending their tenure. The Burner is distinguished by prior intent — the project exists for the removal, not as an opportunity that happens to be hard. This makes it the most calculated and most legible of the cluster: if the architect's scoping decisions are ever audited, the political motive is exposed.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's power comes from severing the audience's evaluation from the scoping decision. Audiences see outcomes (the project failed); they rarely audit inputs (the scope was unsurvivable). The architect harvests the asymmetry. But this same asymmetry is the tactic's structural vulnerability: any institution that develops the habit of asking 'who scoped this?' alongside 'who delivered this?' neutralizes the move. The tactic also produces a second-order cost the architect often underestimates — the manufactured failure consumes real organizational resources (budget, staff hours, opportunity cost on the displaced real initiative), and other actors in the system learn that being handed a 'strategic priority' may be a political marker, which over time degrades the legitimacy of all stretch assignments. The architect wins the removal but spends institutional credibility on the win.

### Resolution Approach

Read as specific when analyzing the executive-removal play within a defined governance structure — the concrete tools (project scoping authority, performance review cycles, board reporting cadence) and the bounded scope of the operation. Read as generic when extracting the pretext-manufacturing pattern for use across domains — the structural move of producing observable failure to legitimize a predetermined action. Both readings converge on Strategy 14 (Resistance Triple), and this convergence is itself diagnostic: the architect never sells the preferred outcome (B(X)↑ stays absent because openly promoting the removal would expose the political motive), but quietly facilitates it via the cover story (C(X)↓) while attacking the alternative on both axes (B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑). The missing lever is the tell — Resistance Triple is precisely the configuration of someone who needs the audience to think they're observing accountability rather than persuasion.
