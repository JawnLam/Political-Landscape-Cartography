# 0423 — ADVERSARIAL COLLABORATION

> Forcing rival departments to work together on a high-stakes project with deliberately misaligned incentives or contradictory goals, engineering conflict and failure.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C50 — Divide and Conquer, Adversarial Collaboration & Coalition Disruption

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Williamson

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The phrase 'adversarial collaboration' is borrowed from a known organizational design construct (rival teams jointly chartered for stress-testing), but the tactic inverts that prosocial frame: the orchestrator uses the joint mandate as a delivery mechanism for sabotage. The title therefore reads as both a named organizational pattern and a generic principle of engineered failure through structural contradiction.

**Specific Interpretation.** An executive or department head pairs two known rival units on a high-visibility deliverable while wiring the charter with conflicting KPIs, overlapping scope, or asymmetric resource grants, so that the inevitable collision discredits one or both rivals without the orchestrator appearing to act against them.

**Generic Interpretation.** A principal can defeat or weaken two threats simultaneously by binding them to a shared, contradictory mandate — the work itself becomes the weapon, since failure is structurally determined and blame attaches to the agents rather than to the architect.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The orchestrational core — yoking parties to a charter whose internal contradictions guarantee conflict — applies far beyond department politics. It operates in coalition governments, joint ventures, blended families, and franchise grants. The succession-style organizational case is the most legible application, but the mechanism does not need that context to function.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The two rival departments collide, miss the deadline, blame each other publicly, and both lose credibility — clearing the orchestrator's path
- **Option Y:** The rival departments recognize the trap, refuse the engineered conflict, escalate the contradictions back to the orchestrator, and force a charter rewrite
- **Context:** A senior executive with two ambitious rival department heads — each angling for the same upcoming SVP role — chartering them as co-leads on a flagship cross-functional program with deliberately overlapping authority, contradictory success metrics, and a budget that cannot fund both interpretations of the mandate

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The orchestrator does not need to enhance the appeal of mutual destruction to the participants — they are not the choosers here. The targets of the tactic are the rivals, not an audience being sold on the failure outcome. The lever does not engage.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The chartered structure pre-pays the orchestrator's political costs. Because the failure is owned by the rivals' apparent dysfunction, the orchestrator pays nothing to discredit them — no smear, no confrontation, no visible move. The structure is the move. This is the operating core of the tactic.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Refusing the trap (escalating contradictions) is recoded by the program's optics as 'inability to collaborate' or 'lack of executive maturity.' Rivals who try to surface the structural impossibility look weak or political themselves, draining the credibility benefit they would otherwise gain by naming the problem.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

To resist successfully, a rival must publicly diagnose the orchestrator's charter as deliberately rigged — a move that requires accusing one's principal of bad faith, with little proof, while a high-stakes deliverable is in flight. The political cost of correctly naming the trap is often higher than the cost of walking into it.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The orchestrator does not need to sell the failure outcome — they need only make failure cheap to deliver and resistance expensive to mount. By embedding contradictions in the charter, the cost of the desired outcome (rival self-destruction) is structurally subsidized, while the route out (publicly accusing the principal of sabotage-by-design) is loaded with cost and stripped of credibility. The tactic is signature-Resistance-Triple: it never has to celebrate the goal because the structure does the work.

**Effectiveness factors.** Works when (a) the orchestrator controls charter design and resource allocation, (b) the rivals already distrust each other enough that good-faith renegotiation is unlikely, (c) the deliverable is high-visibility enough that failure is costly to both rivals and (d) the orchestrator has plausible deniability — the contradictions look like complexity, not engineering. Fails when one rival is sophisticated enough to refuse the mandate, when an external auditor reviews the charter design, or when the rivals defect into open alliance against the orchestrator.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Schelling's mechanism design (the architect of the game beats the players of the game), with reinforcement from Williamson's transaction-cost analysis of incomplete contracts — contradictory mandates create asset specificity and bilateral lock-in that the parties cannot exit without loss

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic eliminates the target's effective choice set by changing the structure of the game itself rather than persuading them within it. Both parties are forced into a strategic situation whose Nash equilibrium is mutual destruction. They cannot cooperate (contradictory incentives), cannot exit (committed to a chartered deliverable), and cannot defect against the orchestrator (the contradiction is deniable as ordinary cross-functional complexity).

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when the orchestrator has design authority but limited direct authority to act against either target; when the targets have prior history that makes cooperation costly; when third parties can observe outcomes but not the design; and when the deliverable has enough complexity that engineered contradictions are indistinguishable from ordinary scope ambiguity.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A board, having reluctantly accepted a merger-of-equals with co-CEO structure, drafts an integration charter that splits revenue ownership but unifies cost-cutting authority, knowing each co-CEO will be measured against contradictory P&L definitions in the first year

**Sample X:** Two rival co-CEOs in a forced merger destroy each other in the integration phase, leaving the board free to install an outside successor

**Sample Y:** The two co-CEOs recognize the structural trap, share information, and present a unified front to the board demanding a clean role split

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The board doesn't need to make co-CEO self-destruction look good to anyone — it simply needs the structure to produce it

- C(X)↓ ✓ — By embedding the conflict in the integration charter at signing, the board pays zero political cost for the eventual destabilization — it was 'in the deal'

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Either co-CEO who tries to unify the role splits is framed as 'unable to share leadership,' undermining the legitimacy of even raising the structural problem

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Publicly accusing the board of designing the co-CEO structure to fail requires attacking the merger architecture itself — a move that destabilizes the executive's own legitimacy more than it threatens the board's

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A prime minister in a fragile three-party coalition assigns two junior partners overlapping authority over immigration policy — one party with a humanitarian platform, the other with a restrictionist platform — knowing that the first contested case will detonate the working relationship and provide grounds for early dissolution

**Sample X:** Two coalition partners in a unity government collapse the cabinet over irreconcilable portfolio mandates, allowing the prime minister to call a snap election from a position of strength

**Sample Y:** The coalition partners suspend their rivalry, jointly demand portfolio renegotiation, and force the PM into a public concession

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The PM doesn't sell the coalition collapse — the structure delivers it

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Because the contradictory mandates were negotiated as 'compromise' at coalition formation, the PM bears no visible cost for the eventual rupture — the partners destroyed themselves

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — A junior coalition partner who publicly names the trap is recast as 'incapable of governing in coalition,' diminishing electoral standing rather than the PM's

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Naming the trap requires accusing the head of government of bad-faith coalition design — a charge requiring evidence the junior partners do not have

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any context where (a) one party has architectural authority over the rules of engagement while another party plays inside those rules, and (b) the architect can disguise contradictions as ordinary complexity. Found across coalition politics, M&A integration, franchise systems, joint ventures, blended families, federated organizations, and military command-by-committee structures. Less effective in simple bilateral relationships (no third-party audience), in highly transparent governance regimes (charter design is audited), or when targets have low prior animosity (good-faith renegotiation defeats the trap).

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CEO charters two rival SVPs as co-leads on a digital-transformation program with contradictory KPIs (one rewarded for speed-to-market, the other for risk-controlled rollout), engineering the political collapse of both before the next succession decision
- Government: A coalition prime minister assigns overlapping authority on a controversial portfolio to two ideologically opposed junior partners, manufacturing the collapse pretext for a snap election
- Diplomacy: A major power brokers a peace agreement between two regional rivals with deliberately ambiguous border language, knowing the first incident will restart the conflict and justify continued external mediation presence
- Academic: A department chair appoints two rival faculty members as co-directors of a high-profile grant initiative with contradictory accountability lines to the dean and provost, ensuring neither builds the institutional base to challenge the chair's renewal
- Family business: A founder names two warring children as co-trustees of an estate with overlapping fiduciary authority, knowing the resulting litigation will neutralize both as successors and preserve the founder's external advisor as the de facto controller
- Military: A theater commander assigns overlapping operational responsibility for a contested zone to two competing service branches, ensuring inter-service friction consumes political bandwidth that would otherwise constrain the commander's discretion
- NGO: A donor conditions a major grant on joint program execution by two implementing partners with incompatible operating doctrines, generating the cancellation evidence needed to redirect funds to a preferred third party

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Adversarial Collaboration belongs to the cluster of divide-and-conquer tactics (alongside Conflict Entrepreneurship, Friction Engineering, and Divided Loyalty Engineering) but is distinguished by a single architectural signature: the orchestrator does not merely exploit existing rivalry, they bind the rivals to a contradictory mandate that mathematically forecloses cooperation. Where Divide and Conquer separates, Adversarial Collaboration forces destructive contact. Where Friction Engineering slows movement, this tactic guarantees collision. The 'collaboration' label is essential to its plausible deniability — the same yoking, named honestly as a conflict assignment, would attract immediate scrutiny.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic teaches that institutional design is a weapon, not just a container. A practitioner who can shape charters, KPIs, or governance documents has access to influence that no amount of direct persuasion or coalition-building can match. The defensive corollary: any cross-functional mandate, joint role, or co-leadership structure should be treated as potentially adversarial until its incentive geometry is audited. Sophisticated targets do not ask 'do my counterparts want to cooperate' but rather 'can the rules of this engagement support cooperation even if we wanted to.'

### Resolution Approach

Classify as specific when the analysis concerns a bounded organizational case — a particular co-lead appointment, a particular merged-entity charter — where the practitioner's question is whether to accept the role or how to renegotiate it. Classify as generic when extracting the design pattern for use elsewhere, or when teaching the diagnostic to spot it in unfamiliar contexts. The generic lens is more reusable; the specific lens is more actionable when one is the rival being chartered. The deepest practitioner takeaway is that the time to defeat this tactic is at the moment of charter drafting — once the structure is signed, the equilibrium is set and the rivals' degrees of freedom have already collapsed.
