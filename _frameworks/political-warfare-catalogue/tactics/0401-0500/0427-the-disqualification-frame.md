# 0427 — THE DISQUALIFICATION FRAME

> Defining the required competencies for newly created roles in a way that perfectly matches pre-selected allies while specifically excluding the expertise of resistant incumbents.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C68 — Talent Review, Performance Review & Calibration Weaponization

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Schelling

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic is anchored in a concrete organizational scene — newly created roles and a job-description authoring moment — but the underlying move (writing selection criteria to fit pre-chosen winners and exclude pre-chosen losers) is a domain-general method of laundering a predetermined outcome through ostensibly neutral procedure.

**Specific Interpretation.** Inside a reorganization, the actor controlling the role-design pen specifies competencies (certifications, years of platform experience, specific leadership rotations, particular degrees) that map onto allies' resumes while disqualifying incumbents who hold institutional knowledge but lack the now-mandatory credentials.

**Generic Interpretation.** A general influence pattern in which a decision-maker uses control over criteria-setting to produce a desired selection result while the selection itself appears merit-based and objective. The criteria do the political work that overt advocacy would expose.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The reorganization scene is vivid but incidental — the same mechanism appears in grant-writing, regulatory licensing, tender specifications, electoral district lines, and immigration policy. What is reusable is the principle: whoever defines the rubric controls the outcome while appearing to defer to it. The specific reading is one instance of a much larger family.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Appoint the pre-selected allies to the newly designed roles on the basis of 'objective' competency requirements
- **Option Y:** Retain or reassign resistant incumbents whose institutional expertise no longer matches the published requirements
- **Context:** A reorganization in which the architect of the new structure also controls the writing of role descriptions and required competencies, before any selection process begins. Common during post-merger integration, transformation programs, or new-CEO-imposed restructures where 'fresh eyes' justify wholesale role redesign.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not promote the allies' qualifications directly — that would invite scrutiny of the comparison. The allies' apparent fit emerges silently from the criteria themselves, which is precisely the move's signature: persuasion is replaced by definition. Direct enhancement of X would compromise the procedural neutrality the tactic depends on.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Allies' selection becomes structurally frictionless: they walk into the role as the only candidates who 'check every box.' The decision-maker need not defend a controversial appointment because the criteria — drafted upstream — have already done the defending. Political cost of appointing favorites is converted into procedural cost of meeting the rubric, which the favorites alone bear without effort.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The incumbents' deep institutional knowledge — previously their core asset — is rendered formally irrelevant by criteria that don't list it. Years of context, relationships, and tacit expertise become invisible in the rubric, so even sympathetic observers cannot point to what's being lost. The tactic does not attack incumbents' competence; it redefines competence so their competence no longer counts.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Resistant incumbents now face a public 'failure to qualify' rather than a political dismissal. Challenging their exclusion requires them to attack the criteria — which forces them to argue against ostensibly objective professional standards, an unflattering posture. The cost of resistance is the cost of appearing to demand a lower bar.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration's coherence comes from what the actor refuses to do. By declining to amplify the allies' merits directly, the actor avoids the rebuttable claim 'these people are better.' Instead, the criteria silently lower the friction for the favored outcome (C(X)↓), redefine incumbent value out of existence (B(Y)↓), and load the cost of objection onto anyone who would challenge the rubric (C(Y)↑). The selection that follows is barely a decision — it is the arithmetic consequence of definitions set earlier and elsewhere.

**Effectiveness factors.** Depends on (1) the actor controlling criteria authorship before selection begins, (2) plausible domain reasoning for each requirement so the rubric reads as professional rather than gerrymandered, (3) sufficient time elapsed between criteria-setting and selection to obscure the link, and (4) absence of an independent reviewer empowered to interrogate the criteria themselves. Fails when an incumbent has a credible champion at a higher level willing to ask 'why these requirements?' before the selection meeting, or when an outside auditor (general counsel, board committee, regulator) is given visibility into criteria drafting.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Goffman's Frame Analysis combined with Schelling's manipulation of focal points — the actor controls the definitional frame within which a 'fair' decision is then made, so the focal-point outcome is engineered upstream of the choice itself. Adjacent mechanism: procedural legitimacy laundering, in which a predetermined result is washed through an ostensibly neutral process to acquire the authority of having been 'selected' rather than 'imposed.'

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not change the target's preferences or supply new information about candidates — it changes the question being asked. By the time selectors deliberate, the criteria have already eliminated the field. Each individual evaluative judgment looks rigorous, but the rigor is applied within boundaries that have been drawn to produce one answer. The decision-maker experiences themselves as objective; the outcome was nonetheless determined.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where (a) criteria-setting is procedurally separated from selection and treated as a technical exercise rather than a political one, (b) the domain has enough complexity that almost any criterion can be defended on professional grounds, and (c) the audience values procedural legitimacy enough that overturning a 'fair process' carries higher cost than accepting an unwelcome outcome.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A federal procurement officer drafts a Request for Proposals whose mandatory technical specifications — particular software stacks, specific cleared-personnel headcounts, certifications held by very few firms — describe the favored vendor's catalog almost verbatim. Competing bidders cannot meet the spec without retooling, and the incumbent's substantively superior generalist capability is non-responsive to the rubric.

**Sample X:** Award a multi-year federal contract to a favored vendor whose existing capabilities precisely match the published requirements

**Sample Y:** Award the contract to incumbent or competing vendors with broader but differently-shaped capabilities

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active — the RFP cannot praise the favored vendor without procurement-integrity violations. The mechanism deliberately stays in the criteria, not the marketing.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The favored vendor submits a low-effort bid that is mechanically responsive to every line item, while the procurement officer's award decision is shielded from challenge by the bid's exact compliance.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Competitors' substantive strengths — innovation, price, breadth, prior performance — are non-scoring because the rubric does not weight them. The benefits exist; the procurement cannot see them.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Competitors must protest the specification itself, an expensive and rarely-successful posture that signals sour-grapes and damages future bidding standing.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A department chair drafts the position announcement for a senior search with required qualifications carefully selected — specific subfield boundaries, particular journal-publication thresholds, named methodological commitments — that fit the preferred internal candidate's profile and exclude a heterodox external scholar whose work the chair finds threatening. The search committee then conducts a procedurally rigorous review within those boundaries.

**Sample X:** Confirm the preferred candidate for a tenured faculty position at a research university

**Sample Y:** Hire a methodologically heterodox scholar whose record is strong on substance but lighter on the department's signal-credentials

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active — the chair cannot advocate openly for the preferred candidate without compromising committee independence. The signaling happens entirely through the criteria, which read as the department defining its standards.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The preferred candidate's CV maps exactly onto the announcement; their selection requires no extraordinary justification from the committee.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The heterodox scholar's substantive contributions become formally invisible because they don't match the published subfield definition or journal list — the committee never has to deliberate over them.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Advocating for the heterodox scholar would require a committee member to argue that the position announcement was itself misdrawn — a posture that picks a fight with the chair before the deliberation even begins.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies anywhere selection or allocation is mediated by written criteria drafted upstream of the decision — hiring, procurement, grant awards, regulatory licensing, immigration visa categories, electoral district maps, school admissions rubrics, accreditation standards, certification regimes. The structural requirement is a separation between rule-writers and rule-appliers that obscures the connection between criteria-design and outcome. Less effective in genuinely contested forums (open primaries, free-form deliberation, market competition) where the criteria themselves are subject to revision in real time.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A post-merger HR leader writes new-org role descriptions whose certification requirements (a specific ERP credential held by acquirer staff, not target staff) determine the staffing outcome before any interview is held.
- Government: An agency drafts a sole-source contract justification listing capabilities only one vendor possesses, where the capability list was developed in consultation with that vendor.
- Academic: A grant-making body publishes a call for proposals whose eligibility criteria (institution type, prior award history, methodological keywords) deliver funding to a known set of recipients while appearing competitive.
- Lobbying: An industry coalition shapes regulatory licensing requirements (capital reserves, specific certifications, audit cadences) that incumbents trivially meet and new entrants cannot, presented as consumer-protection standards.
- Activism: A community-board nomination process specifies residency, prior-volunteer-hours, and language-proficiency requirements that exclude newer immigrant residents from contesting long-time board incumbents.
- Diplomacy: A multilateral organization writes membership criteria — democratic-governance benchmarks, financial contributions, regional balance — that pre-determine which states can join while preserving the appearance of universal openness.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Inside this cluster of HR-process weaponization tactics, THE DISQUALIFICATION FRAME is distinctive in operating before the formal evaluation begins. Siblings like THE 360-DEGREE HIT JOB, THE PERFORMANCE REVIEW AMBUSH, and TALENT REVIEW WEAPONIZATION manipulate the evaluation moment itself; THE DISQUALIFICATION FRAME makes the evaluation moment cosmetic by predetermining who can even appear in it. The pattern is upstream criteria-control: the political fight is moved out of the visible selection room and into the invisible rubric-drafting room, where opposition cannot organize because no decision appears to be happening yet.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's deepest power is that it converts political contestation into procedural compliance — the favored outcome is no longer something the actor must defend, it is something the rubric produces. This shifts the burden of resistance from 'making the case against the appointment' (a normal political posture) to 'challenging the standards of the profession' (an embarrassing and rarely-winnable one). Practitioners should recognize that the moment criteria are being written, the political contest has already begun even though no one will name it as such. Counter-play requires intervention at the rubric stage: forcing transparency in criteria-authorship, demanding justification of each requirement, and inserting independent review before — not after — the selection process opens.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a particular role-design or hiring controversy where the question is whether the criteria were tailored to predetermine the outcome. Use the generic lens when designing safeguards against criteria-rigging across any selection regime — procurement integrity, search-committee charters, regulatory rulemaking — because the same structural fix (separating criteria-authorship from beneficiaries, requiring criteria-justification, sunlighting the drafting process) addresses the underlying pattern regardless of domain.

## Disqualification Frame Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Political work is performed by definitions, not advocacy. The actor's influence is exerted entirely upstream of the visible decision and is invisible to participants in the decision itself.

**Escalation Pattern.** Begins with seemingly reasonable criteria additions ('we need someone with platform X experience'); accumulates exclusions through a series of plausibly-defended specifications; culminates in a job description that reads like a generic professional standard but is, in fact, a single resume rendered in abstract form.

**Counter Measures.** Demand a written justification for each requirement linked to demonstrated business need; require sign-off from a stakeholder who does not benefit from the criteria; benchmark the criteria against external comparable roles before publication; insist on an explicit list of incumbents who would be excluded under the proposed rubric and the reasoning for each exclusion.

**Exit Criteria.** The tactic completes when appointments are made and incumbents have accepted exit or reassignment without a procedural challenge. Once the selection cycle closes, the criteria become the new normal — challenging them later requires re-opening a closed decision, which the organization is structurally reluctant to do.
