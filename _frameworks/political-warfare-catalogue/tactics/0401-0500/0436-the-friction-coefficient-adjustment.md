# 0436 — THE 'FRICTION COEFFICIENT' ADJUSTMENT

> Intentionally increasing the administrative burden and approval steps for actions that counter the transformation, while simultaneously streamlining processes for aligned activities.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C11 — Bureaucratic Friction & Red-Tape Weaponization

**Cited principles:** Coase, Williamson, Thaler, Sunstein

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors the move in 'the transformation' (a named organizational context), but the mechanism — asymmetrically calibrating procedural drag so that one direction of behavior is cheap and the opposing direction is expensive — is a generic technology of governance that reappears wherever process owners control approval architecture.

**Specific Interpretation.** Inside an active transformation program, the change leader (or PMO) deliberately re-routes opposing requests through extra signatures, committees, and documentation while quietly converting aligned requests into one-page templates or pre-approved fast lanes, so that resisting becomes a part-time job and aligning becomes the path of least resistance.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any actor controlling the approval architecture of a system can tune the coefficient of friction along different paths through that system — slowing rivals, accelerating allies — without ever banning anything or making a visible policy stand. The mechanism is asymmetric process design, applicable from tax codes to platform terms of service.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The transformation framing is a vivid example, but the operating principle — that whoever controls process design controls outcomes by tuning effort costs differentially — is reusable in any system with discretionary procedural authority. The mechanism is more fundamental than its transformation-era illustration.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Pursue or comply with the transformation's preferred actions (which now flow through streamlined, lightweight processes)
- **Option Y:** Pursue actions that work against the transformation (which now require extra committees, sign-offs, and documentation)
- **Context:** A change program where the transformation leader controls process design authority over approvals, requisitions, hiring, vendor selection, or capital allocation, and uses that authority to differentially calibrate procedural cost along the two paths.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not change the substantive merits or upside of transformation-aligned actions — it only changes how easy they are to execute. The work product itself isn't more valuable; it's just cheaper to get to. This is what distinguishes it from a Full Spectrum play.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Streamlining processes for aligned activities is the explicit second half of the tactic — fast-track approval lanes, pre-cleared templates, single-signatory authority, waived committee reviews. Aligned actors find that the same proposal that would have taken six weeks now clears in five days.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not attack the substantive merit of counter-transformation actions — a competitor product line, a divestiture-resisting business unit, an old budget allocation. Those activities retain their underlying value; the tactic operates purely on procedural cost, not on perceived worth.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Adding approval steps, layering committee reviews, requiring detailed business cases, demanding cross-functional sign-offs — each added hop multiplies elapsed time and political exposure. The resister isn't told 'no'; they are told 'fill out section 7(b) and route to compliance, legal, finance, and the steering committee.' By the time they finish, the transformation window has closed.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates entirely on the cost dimension — it never claims the transformation is better and never says the alternative is worse on the merits. It simply makes one path cheap and the other expensive in time, paperwork, and political exposure. This produces compliance through exhaustion and convenience rather than through persuasion, which is precisely why it is hard to argue against: there is no rhetorical claim to attack.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires genuine control over process design (PMO charter, COO sponsorship, or delegated authority over a workflow system). Effectiveness collapses if the asymmetry becomes too visible — sophisticated opponents will reframe 'extra rigor for opposition requests' as 'discriminatory governance.' Best deployed when each individual added step has a plausible standalone justification (audit, risk, controls) so the pattern is only legible in aggregate. Most effective against opponents who are time-constrained or operationally stretched.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Thaler & Sunstein's Choice Architecture and Effort-Based Decision Avoidance — small frictions ('sludge,' in Thaler's later terminology) systematically suppress action by exploiting humans' bounded rationality and present-biased preferences. Cass Sunstein's work on administrative burden, and earlier Coase/Williamson transaction-cost economics, both formalize how procedural cost reshapes which choices actually get made even when the nominal option set is unchanged.

**Decision Alteration.** The actor does not change the option set, ban anything, or argue for the preferred outcome. Instead, they tune the marginal effort cost of each path. Because humans heavily discount distant rewards relative to immediate effort, and because effort costs are paid up-front while benefits arrive later, even small asymmetries in procedural friction reliably bend behavior toward the low-friction path. The decision feels free; the architecture is doing the work.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works when (a) one actor controls process design with enough discretion to make per-step justifications plausible, (b) targets are time-constrained or attention-constrained, (c) the friction is calibrated to look like generic rigor rather than targeted obstruction, and (d) outcomes are evaluated on the volume of completed actions rather than on procedural symmetry.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An HR director wants to migrate the workforce off a costly legacy benefits plan. Rather than terminating the old plan (which would provoke union grievances), they introduce a slick digital alternative on a fast track and quietly add 'enhanced verification' to the old workflow.

**Sample X:** An employee files a benefits claim through the new self-service portal that auto-approves in 48 hours

**Sample Y:** An employee files the same benefits claim through the legacy paper process that requires manager signature, HR review, and a phone interview

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active — the new plan need not be substantively superior; effort asymmetry alone drives migration

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Fast-tracking the new digital workflow turns a multi-week claim into a two-day click-through, making the preferred plan dramatically less work to use

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — not active — the legacy plan's benefits are not disparaged; doing so would invite union pushback

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Adding verification calls, additional signatures, and paper forms to the legacy workflow burdens it with effort that competes against employees' limited attention

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A federal agency's general counsel, facing scrutiny from a specific investigative outlet, formally instructs FOIA processors to apply 'standard rigor' to all requests but informally calibrates which subject-matter areas trigger the slow-track review queue.

**Sample X:** A friendly think-tank submits FOIA-style information requests that the agency answers within standard 20-day windows

**Sample Y:** An adversarial journalist submits identical requests that the agency routes through 'consultation with originating offices,' privacy review, and litigation hold checks

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active — the value of the information returned to friendly requesters is unchanged; only the speed of delivery is

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Routing friendly requests through expedited processing reduces the lag between request and product, making favorable coverage easier to generate

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — not active — the underlying records that adversarial requesters would receive are not falsified or stripped of value

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Adding consultation, privacy, and litigation-hold review queues to adversarial requests stretches turnaround from weeks to years, often outliving the news cycle the requester needed to inform

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any system with discretionary process design — corporate workflows, regulatory permitting, immigration processing, judicial scheduling, tax administration, platform content moderation queues, university promotion committees, hospital prior-authorization systems. The constraint is not domain but the existence of a process owner with enough scope to tune asymmetrically while maintaining per-step deniability. Less effective in systems with hard service-level agreements, automated equal-treatment audits, or strong external scrutiny of process metrics.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A COO routes vendor renewals from strategic-partner suppliers through a one-page approval template while requiring incumbent legacy vendors to re-bid through a 60-page RFP with five committee gates.
- Government: A permitting authority creates a 'fast-track innovation lane' for development aligned with the administration's housing priorities while subjecting opposing land-use proposals to expanded environmental and community-input procedures.
- Academic: A department chair fast-tracks tenure cases aligned with the department's emerging methodological school by waiving external review committees, while tenure cases from rival methodological traditions are routed to full external review with expanded letter requirements.
- Diplomacy: A consular system streamlines visa processing for nationals of allied states (interview waivers, drop-box submission) while requiring nationals of strategic rivals to complete in-person interviews, supplemental questionnaires, and inter-agency security clearance review.
- Platform governance: A social media platform's trust-and-safety team routes appeals from verified, brand-safe accounts through automated review while routing appeals from accounts in politically contested categories through manual, multi-tier human review with longer queues.
- Healthcare: An insurer streamlines prior authorization for generic and preferred-formulary drugs (electronic approval in 24 hours) while requiring brand-name and non-formulary requests to clear step-therapy documentation, peer-to-peer phone reviews, and medical director sign-off.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific (transformation-era) and generic (sludge architecture) readings converge on the same lever signature — Cost Contrast (Strategy 8) — and this convergence is itself the diagnostic point. THE 'FRICTION COEFFICIENT' ADJUSTMENT is a pure cost-axis tactic; it neither sells the preferred outcome nor disparages the alternative, distinguishing it sharply from full-spectrum plays and from rhetorical persuasion. It belongs to a small family of moves (alongside WEAPONIZED BUREAUCRACY, 0107) that operate entirely by making one path cheap and the other expensive, but whereas WEAPONIZED BUREAUCRACY emphasizes the unilateral loading of red tape onto opponents, THE 'FRICTION COEFFICIENT' ADJUSTMENT is specifically symmetric in design — the streamlining of allied paths is half the mechanism. Without the C(X)↓ half, you have obstruction; with both halves calibrated together, you have architecture.

### Strategic Implications

Because the tactic operates without rhetorical claims, it has no rhetorical surface to attack. Opponents who try to argue against the merits of the transformation are arguing against the wrong target — the friction calibration would continue working even if every substantive argument went the opponent's way. The counter-move is therefore not rhetorical but procedural: demand symmetric service-level agreements across paths, surface aggregate metrics on time-to-decision broken out by request type, and require any new process step to be either universal or absent. Practitioners deploying the tactic should remember that aggregate audit visibility is its single greatest vulnerability — each step is defensible alone, but the pattern across hundreds of requests is not. Calibrate the asymmetry to fall below the noise floor of routine governance review.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when coaching an actor inside a live transformation who controls the PMO charter and needs to translate the principle into specific approval-architecture decisions during a defined change window. Use the generic lens when teaching the underlying mechanism, designing counter-measures, or analyzing analogous moves in non-corporate domains (permitting regimes, content moderation queues, immigration processing). The two readings are not in tension — the generic explains why the specific works.
