# 0428 — THE BYPASS OPERATION

> Creating new roles or committees that duplicate the functions of an existing resistant group, gradually diverting workflow and resources until the original group is obsolete.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C91 — Encirclement, Jurisdictional Creep & Cannibalization

**Cited principles:** Williamson

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a concrete organizational maneuver (standing up new committees or roles to replace a resistant unit), but the underlying mechanism — routing the flow of work and resources around an obstructive structure until atrophy makes it irrelevant — is the abstract strategic pattern of encirclement-by-substitution that recurs in military, regulatory, technological, and ecological contexts.

**Specific Interpretation.** In the organizational reading, this is the deliberate construction of a parallel reporting line, task force, or office whose remit overlaps the resistant group's, with the implicit promise to internal customers that the new path is faster and friendlier — eroding the incumbent group's caseload until budget and headcount cuts become the natural-looking next step.

**Generic Interpretation.** Abstractly, this is a structural-routing tactic: when a node in a network blocks the flow you need, you do not attack the node — you build a parallel channel with lower friction, let traffic re-route, and let the disused node wither. The tactic operates on the topology of work, not on the resistant actor's mind.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Within an organization the tactic is recognizable as a familiar power play, but its strategic signature — bypass-and-atrophy rather than confront-and-defeat — is reusable wherever a blocker can be made structurally irrelevant rather than overcome. Regulators do this with sandbox carve-outs around incumbent legal regimes; tech platforms do it with new APIs that orphan older ones; militaries do it with envelopment that leaves enemy strongpoints starved rather than reduced. The mechanism is more fundamental than any one organizational instance of it.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Route requests, approvals, and resources through the newly created parallel committee or role
- **Option Y:** Continue submitting work through the established but resistant group whose remit overlaps
- **Context:** A senior executive faces a unit (legal review, an architecture review board, a compliance committee, a regional VP, a powerful incumbent department) that reliably slows, dilutes, or kills initiatives the executive wants advanced. Rather than confront the unit directly — which would trigger sponsor protection, union grievance, or governance scrutiny — the executive charters a new body with overlapping remit and slightly different framing (innovation council, transformation office, fast-track committee), positioned to handle a growing share of the same workflow.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The new committee is rarely sold on its intrinsic merit; that would invite a debate the actor wants to avoid. The point of a bypass is not to argue that the new structure is better — it is to make it the path of least resistance. If the actor had to publicly defend the new body's superior value proposition, the existing group's defenders would treat the entire creation as a frontal attack and mobilize. The tactic's elegance is that benefits remain implicit, almost incidental.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. The new structure is engineered for lower friction at every touchpoint internal customers care about — faster turnaround, looser documentation, a more accommodating chair, fewer review gates, an explicit charter to 'unblock.' Whatever the resistant group was using to slow work is the exact axis on which the new body is positioned to move faster. The decision to route through X becomes obvious without anyone needing to argue for it.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

As work migrates away, the resistant group's reviews and approvals matter to fewer high-visibility decisions, and the body's perceived consequentiality fades. People stop bringing important questions; the group's outputs become technical rather than strategic. Crucially, this benefit erosion is structural rather than rhetorical — no one disparages the old group, but its diminishing docket speaks for itself in the next budget cycle.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Routing through the resistant group remains technically permitted but becomes professionally inadvisable: slower, more uncertain, and now visibly anomalous when peers are using the new channel. Mid-level managers who continue to file through the old body start to look behind the curve, and the lengthening queue in the old group becomes its own deterrent without anyone needing to forbid the route.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** Bypass operations deliberately suppress B(X)↑ to stay below the resistant unit's threat threshold; the move only works if the new body is not framed as a replacement but as an addition. The three operating levers — making X cheap to use, draining Y of consequential work, and making Y comparatively painful — converge on a single outcome: the resistant group is starved rather than fought. This is the textbook profile of Strategy 14, where the actor refuses to sell X precisely because selling it would expose the campaign.

**Effectiveness factors.** Success requires three conditions. First, executive sponsorship strong enough to charter the new body but quiet enough that the charter looks like routine organizational design, not a coup. Second, real friction in the old group that internal customers already resent — without latent demand for a faster path, the new body sits empty. Third, patience: the bypass works on timescales of quarters and budget cycles, not weeks. It fails when the resistant group has a powerful sponsor who recognizes the pattern early and either reabsorbs the new body or forces a public consolidation review, or when the new body's lower friction comes from skipping legitimate controls, producing a high-visibility failure that justifies the resistant group's gatekeeping.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Williamson's Transaction Cost Economics combined with Hannan & Freeman's Organizational Ecology (structural inertia and selection). Williamson's insight that economic actors route activity through whichever governance structure imposes the lowest transaction costs explains why work migrates without anyone needing to be persuaded. Hannan & Freeman's complement — that organizations are slow to adapt and can be selected against by environmental shifts they cannot match — explains why the resistant group cannot simply 'speed up' once the bypass exists. The bypass operator is doing applied organizational ecology: changing the selection environment so the resistant unit becomes maladapted.

**Decision Alteration.** Rather than persuading any individual to choose differently, the tactic re-engineers the topology of choice so that the preferred route becomes the path of least resistance for every individual acting in self-interest. Each user choosing the new channel is making a locally rational decision; the aggregate effect — atrophy of the bypassed structure — is invisible to them. The blocker is defeated not by changing minds but by depriving its mind-changing of any object to act on.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best in organizations or systems where (a) the blocker holds positional rather than substantive authority — it can refuse but cannot prevent alternative paths from being chartered, (b) there is genuine pent-up demand for the workflow the blocker is throttling, (c) the actor controls the resource flows (budget, headcount, attention) needed to seed the parallel structure, and (d) the system has enough slack to tolerate a period of duplication. Fails in tightly coupled systems where parallel structures generate immediate conflict requiring resolution.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A large enterprise software company where the Architecture Review Board has become a notorious bottleneck — staffed by senior engineers who reflexively block anything that touches the legacy platform. A new CTO charters an Innovation Council with overlapping remit but a charter explicitly to 'unblock cloud-native work,' and quietly directs the cloud product lines through it.

**Sample X:** Submit a new product feature for review by the newly chartered 'Fast-Track Innovation Council'

**Sample Y:** Submit the feature through the existing Architecture Review Board

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The CTO carefully avoids claiming the Innovation Council is technically superior — doing so would trigger the ARB's defenders to demand a head-to-head governance review. The Council is positioned as a supplement, not a replacement.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The Innovation Council meets twice a week instead of monthly, has a written charter to default-approve within 10 business days, and requires a one-page brief instead of the ARB's 40-page template. Each engineering team feels the cost reduction personally on the first submission.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Within two quarters, the ARB is reviewing maintenance work on systems the company is winding down. Its decisions stop appearing in CEO updates. The benefit of ARB approval — once a marker of strategic relevance — quietly evaporates as the strategically relevant work flows elsewhere.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Engineering managers who continue routing through the ARB start missing release commitments. In quarterly business reviews, the lengthening ARB queue is cited as a 'legacy process risk.' Choosing the ARB becomes a career-limiting move without anyone being told not to use it.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A national financial regulator whose Banking Supervision Division has, over decades, accumulated a reputation among innovative firms as a flat 'no' on novel product categories. Political pressure from the Treasury to support fintech growth produces a new Innovation Hub within the same agency, with statutory authority to grant time-limited testing licenses on lighter prudential standards. Over five years, the Banking Supervision Division's docket of novel-product applications collapses to near zero, and its political clout follows.

**Sample X:** License the new fintech offering under the regulator's recently launched innovation sandbox

**Sample Y:** Pursue licensure under the existing prudential framework administered by the long-standing supervisory division

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Treasury and the agency head pointedly do not say the Innovation Hub is the 'right' path or that Banking Supervision is wrong. Public statements emphasize that both routes remain available. The Hub's existence is the message; arguing for its superiority would trigger the supervisory division's parliamentary allies.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The Hub has a 90-day decision SLA, a single point of contact, and explicit authority to waive specified prudential requirements during the testing window. Application cost falls by an order of magnitude.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — As innovative firms migrate to the Hub, the Banking Supervision Division's portfolio increasingly consists of incumbent banks doing routine renewals. Its strategic relevance — and the political weight that flowed from being the gatekeeper of financial innovation — drains away.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Firms still pursuing the prudential framework face longer queues now that the Division's resources have not grown to match the Hub's intake, and their applications attract questions from board investors about why management chose the slow route. The path becomes self-stigmatizing.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** The tactic works wherever a blocking node exists within a system that permits parallel structures — corporate, governmental, military, technological, ecclesiastical, academic. It requires the actor to control charter authority and resource flows, and the system to tolerate temporary duplication. Less effective in systems with constitutional mono-authority (a single supreme court, a single sovereign), or where parallel structures are explicitly prohibited by binding rules. The closer a system is to a market, the more naturally this tactic operates; the closer to a unitary command structure, the more political cover the bypass operator must accumulate before chartering the parallel body.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A new Chief Digital Officer creates a Digital Transformation Office whose remit overlaps the existing IT division's, gradually absorbing budget and projects until IT becomes a back-office cost center.
- Government: An executive branch creates a White House policy office to develop signature initiatives, bypassing the relevant Cabinet department whose career staff are seen as obstructive — over time the department's policy shop is reduced to implementation only.
- Military: Encirclement doctrine in operational planning — rather than reducing a fortified strongpoint at high cost, friendly forces bypass it, sever its supply, and let it surrender as the war moves on (the Wehrmacht's Maginot Line bypass in 1940, Allied 'island-hopping' in the Pacific).
- Technology platforms: Apple's introduction of Swift alongside Objective-C without ever announcing Objective-C's deprecation; the new language carried all new tooling investment, and developers migrated of their own accord.
- Religious organizations: A bishop unable to discipline a powerful parish creates a new diocesan office for the parish's signature ministry, gradually reassigning resources until the parish's autonomy becomes formal rather than substantive.
- Family business: An heir who cannot displace a long-tenured CFO charters a new 'Strategic Finance' role reporting directly to the CEO, slowly migrating treasury, M&A, and investor relations away until the CFO's portfolio is reduced to accounting compliance.
- Academic: A university president unable to reform a tenured department creates an interdisciplinary institute that hires in the same area, captures the grant funding and graduate students, and leaves the original department as a teaching unit only.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both readings converge on a deep insight: the bypass is the tactic of choice when the blocker's positional power exceeds the actor's ability to remove them, but the blocker's structural lock-in is weaker than they assume. The actor recognizes that the resistant group's strength lives in the workflow that flows through it; cut the flow, and the strength evaporates without a fight. Within the cluster (Encirclement, Jurisdictional Creep & Cannibalization), this tactic is distinguished from THE 'CANNIBALIZATION' STRATEGY by intent — bypass aims at irrelevance through parallel construction, while cannibalization actively consumes the old function's resources from within. It differs from THE 'ADJACENCY' STRATEGY (ENCIRCLEMENT) in timing and visibility: adjacency builds outward over time without targeting any specific incumbent, while bypass is a deliberate move against a known blocker. Bypass is the cluster's most surgical and most deniable instrument.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's deepest strategic property is that it weaponizes the blocker's own preference. Resistant groups typically resist by saying no — that is their power. The bypass exploits the asymmetry that a group that lives by saying no cannot say no to a structure created next to it; their veto operates only on what is brought to them, and the bypass makes sure less and less is brought to them. This is why Strategy 14 (Resistance Triple) rather than 15 (Full Spectrum) is the correct fit: explicitly promoting X (B(X)↑) would supply the blocker with a target to veto. The actor's discipline in withholding promotion is what makes the tactic survive contact with organizational antibodies. Practitioners should also note the temporal signature: bypass operations look like normal organizational evolution at any single point in time and only resolve into a strategic pattern when viewed across budget cycles. This is what makes them politically survivable.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing or executing a bounded campaign against a known internal blocker — the choice of new body's charter, its meeting cadence, its reporting line, and its initial workload are tactical decisions where specific-context judgment matters most. Use the generic lens when assessing strategic position more broadly: is the actor in a system that permits parallel structures? Does the blocker derive power from positional gatekeeping (vulnerable to bypass) or from substantive expertise the work genuinely requires (resistant to bypass)? The generic frame also illuminates defenses — a blocker who recognizes a bypass operation early can counter not by attacking the new body (which legitimizes it as a rival) but by demanding consolidation under a single governance review, forcing the bypass back into a confrontation the actor was trying to avoid.

## Bypass Operation Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Parallel construction with suppressed promotion. The new structure must be charterable as routine organizational design, and its advantage over the incumbent must be felt by users rather than declared by the actor.

**Escalation Pattern.** Phase 1 (charter): new body stood up with overlapping but not identical remit, framed as supplemental. Phase 2 (seeding): early adopters routed through the new path, often the actor's own initiatives. Phase 3 (migration): broader workflow shifts as friction differential becomes common knowledge. Phase 4 (resource reallocation): budget and headcount follow workload in the next planning cycle. Phase 5 (consolidation): the old body is wound down or absorbed, usually under a neutral-sounding banner like 'streamlining governance.'

**Counter Measures.** The bypassed group's only effective response is to (a) detect the pattern in phase 1 or 2, before migration becomes self-sustaining, and (b) force a consolidation review that compels the sponsor to either fold the new body or publicly justify the duplication. Direct attacks on the new body in phase 3 or later typically fail because the work has already moved.

**Exit Criteria.** The operation concludes when the bypassed group's docket falls below the threshold at which its existence can be justified in budget review. The actor often delays formal dissolution beyond this point to avoid the optics of an explicit kill, letting the unit decay naturally over an additional cycle.
