# 0469 — THE 'CONSULTANT CAROUSEL'

> Hiring and firing a series of external consultancies until one delivers the pre-desired recommendation, creating the illusion of rigorous external validation.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#11 X-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓` · **Cluster:** C53 — External Actors as Political Instruments (Consultants, Vendors, Validators)

**Cited principles:** Cialdini

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a concrete corporate-political move (serially hiring and firing management consultancies), but the underlying mechanism — searching across multiple ostensibly-independent sources until one returns the desired verdict — is a general epistemic-laundering pattern observable far beyond consulting.

**Specific Interpretation.** A senior executive or board faction with a predetermined organizational decision (e.g., a layoff target, a divestiture, a reorganization design) commissions, terminates, and re-commissions outside consultancies until one produces a report endorsing that decision, which is then presented as the dispositive 'independent finding.'

**Generic Interpretation.** Selection-bias-as-strategy: when an actor controls the iteration of supposedly-neutral expert opinion, they can manufacture the appearance of objective external validation for any predetermined conclusion by simply discarding non-conforming outputs until a conforming one arrives.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The consulting context is the most recognizable instance, but the mechanism — serial sampling from an 'independent' source until a preferred reading is obtained — is the defining feature and recurs in legal opinions, medical second-opinions, scientific review panels, regulatory comment processes, and even hiring committees. The carousel is what makes the tactic; the consultants are just one substrate.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Board, peer executives, and stakeholders endorse the actor's predetermined decision as evidence-based and externally validated
- **Option Y:** Stakeholders demand internal debate on the merits or commission a single, binding outside review the actor cannot replace
- **Context:** A senior executive in a large organization who has already chosen a contentious course (e.g., shuttering a division, mandating a tech-stack migration, justifying a leveraged acquisition) and needs to neutralize foreseeable resistance from the board, peer C-suite members, or works-council-type bodies before announcing it

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The conforming final report transforms the actor's predetermined choice from a personal preference into an 'evidence-based recommendation from McKinsey/BCG/Bain.' The brand equity and presumed methodological rigor of the surviving consultancy are imported wholesale onto the preferred decision, which is the core operating move of the tactic.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The actor pays a smaller personal-accountability price for the decision because authorship is laundered through an external party. If the decision later sours, the consultancy absorbs reputational blame ('we followed the independent recommendation'). The cost is reduced, though real fees are still paid and some sophisticated observers see through it.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The earlier discarded consultancies — and any internal dissenting analysis — are implicitly reframed as inferior, lower-quality, or 'not the right fit,' which devalues the perceived merit of alternative views. Internal experts find their credibility outranked by the surviving brand-name report.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — Not active

The tactic does not directly punish dissent or raise the cost of advocating for the alternative — it operates by stacking the apparent evidence base in one direction rather than by sanctioning those who object. Dissenters can still speak; they simply find themselves arguing against a thick stack of binders rather than against the executive personally. The absence of explicit coercion is part of what makes the move 'civilized' enough for board-level deployment.

### Strategy

**#11 X-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓`

**Rationale.** The carousel works by manufacturing the appearance of independent expert consensus around a predetermined answer. That manufactured consensus simultaneously inflates the preferred decision's perceived merit (it now carries McKinsey's imprimatur), shields the actor from solo accountability (the consultancy 'recommended' it), and crowds out competing views (internal analysts and the earlier-fired consultancies look comparatively unauthoritative) — three levers acting in concert without resorting to direct coercion of dissenters.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on: (1) the actor controlling the procurement, scoping, and termination authority for consulting engagements without independent oversight; (2) sufficient budget to absorb multiple six- and seven-figure engagements as 'cost of doing business'; (3) board-level sophistication being moderate rather than high — sophisticated boards demand the engagement letters, the scopes-of-work, and the prior-engagement history that expose the pattern; (4) consultancy market norms that treat replaced engagements as confidential; (5) genuine ambiguity in the underlying business question so that a defensible, plausibly-rigorous report can be produced for the preferred answer. Fails when a single board member insists on a sole-source engagement with binding terms, or when whistleblower channels expose the serial replacements.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Selection bias weaponized — the 'garden of forking paths' (Andrew Gelman) applied to expert opinion — combined with Cialdini's Authority Principle. The tactic exploits decision-makers' tendency to evaluate the surviving evidence in isolation, without conditioning on the selection process that produced it.

**Decision Alteration.** Stakeholders evaluate the final report as if it were a random draw from the population of expert opinion, when in fact it is a maximally-biased draw — the only one that survived a filter explicitly designed to retain conforming outputs. The decision is altered because the perceived posterior probability that the preferred course is correct gets anchored to an authoritative-looking endorsement whose conditional-on-selection information value is near zero.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when (a) the actor controls the iteration loop without transparency, (b) the supposedly-independent sources are numerous enough to support multiple draws, (c) audiences cannot easily see the discarded outputs, and (d) the underlying question admits enough genuine analytical ambiguity that a defensible-looking conforming opinion can be produced.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A real-estate developer seeking a zoning variance who commissions traffic-impact and environmental studies from a rotating set of private consulting firms, terminating each that concludes the project would harm the neighborhood, until one produces a favorable study that is then submitted to the planning commission as the 'expert analysis.'

**Sample X:** A municipal council approves a contested zoning variance

**Sample Y:** The council rejects the variance after a single binding environmental review

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The conforming study's authority and technical apparatus (modeled traffic counts, decibel projections, acceptable mitigation) make the variance appear demonstrably safe and beneficial.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Council members who approve can point to 'the expert study' as the basis for their vote, reducing personal political exposure to opposing constituents.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Community-group analyses and earlier negative studies are sidelined as 'one study among several' or 'not from a credentialed firm,' diminishing their apparent merit.

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — The tactic does not directly punish opponents — it merely outweighs them with manufactured expert support.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A pharmaceutical sponsor running multiple Phase II protocols across different CROs (contract research organizations) and statistical-analysis plans, terminating arms or shifting endpoints that fail to show signal, until one configuration produces submittable results — then submitting only that pivotal trial as the basis for approval, while characterizing earlier failed work as 'dose-finding' or 'exploratory.'

**Sample X:** A drug receives regulatory approval based on a pivotal trial design

**Sample Y:** The regulator demands additional independent confirmatory trials

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The surviving trial's statistically-significant primary endpoint, produced by a credentialed CRO using a pre-specified protocol, carries the regulatory weight of a single positive finding.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The sponsor's executives and medical reviewers face less direct accountability for the approval decision, which now rests on 'the data' produced by independent third parties.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The case for additional confirmatory trials is undermined by the existence of a clean pivotal result — why duplicate what's already been shown? Earlier negative work is reframed as methodologically inferior.

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — Skeptics within the regulator are not directly sanctioned; they are simply outnumbered by the apparent weight of evidence.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any decision context where (a) supposedly-independent expert opinion is decision-relevant, (b) the actor controls procurement and iteration of that opinion, and (c) audiences evaluate the surviving output without auditing the selection process. Observed in corporate strategy, regulatory submissions, litigation (expert-witness shopping), academic peer review (re-submitting until a favorable referee), medical second-opinions, judicial venue selection, and government policy commissions. Less effective in contexts with mandatory single-source authority (e.g., a constitutionally-designated arbiter) or with full procurement transparency.

### Core Strategy

**#5 X Optimization** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CEO determined to divest an underperforming division commissions three strategy firms in succession, retaining the one whose report recommends precisely the divestiture structure already drafted by the CFO.
- Litigation: A patent-holder engages and dismisses several technical expert witnesses until finding one whose deposition testimony will support the preferred infringement theory, then designates only that expert at trial.
- Government: An administration seeking justification for a new sanctions regime commissions intelligence assessments from multiple analytical units, elevating the one whose findings most cleanly support the predetermined policy.
- Medical: A patient (or a hospital quality committee) seeking a particular diagnosis or treatment path consults specialists serially until one endorses the preferred course, presenting that opinion as 'the specialist consultation.'
- Academic: A faculty hiring committee commissions external letters from a curated list of referees known to favor the inside candidate, while letters from a broader pool would have produced a mixed picture.
- Lobbying: An industry association funds a series of think-tank white papers on a regulatory question, prominently citing only those whose conclusions support the industry's preferred rule.
- Family business: A founder seeking to justify passing the firm to one child over another commissions multiple succession-planning advisors until one recommends the preferred heir on 'objective leadership-assessment grounds.'

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both lenses reveal that the carousel's power lies not in any single consultant's report but in the invisible selection process behind it. The specific organizational reading exposes how the tactic crowds out internal expertise (B(Y)↓ is more pronounced when the actor needs to dismiss known internal dissenters), while the generic reading clarifies that the core mechanism is just two-lever — manufactured authority (B(X)↑) plus laundered accountability (C(X)↓). Within cluster C53, the carousel is distinguished from THIRD-PARTY VALIDATION PLOY and EXTERNAL VALIDATION ECHO CHAMBER by its serial, iterative quality: those siblings stage a single validation event, while the carousel runs the validation process until it produces the desired output. It is the 'p-hacking' member of the external-validation family.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic depends on an information asymmetry between the actor (who knows how many consultancies have been fired) and the audience (who sees only the survivor). Two structural defenses follow: first, governance bodies should require disclosure of all engagements on a topic over the prior 24-36 months before accepting any single report as dispositive; second, sole-source binding engagements — where the consultant's terms include a non-replacement clause for the duration of the question — neutralize the iteration loop entirely. The cost to the organization is rarely just the consulting fees: the carousel quietly trains internal experts that their analyses are window-dressing, accelerating their departure or disengagement and hollowing out the in-house judgment the firm will eventually need when no consultant is available.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing an actual board-level or C-suite situation where the question is 'is this report actually independent?' — the bounded analysis surfaces the three operating levers and the governance interventions that disrupt them. Use the generic lens when teaching the underlying epistemic vulnerability — that selection-controlled evidence is near-worthless evidence — which applies to anyone evaluating expert opinion procured by an interested party, including investors reading sell-side research, regulators reading sponsor-funded trials, and judges weighing party-retained experts.
