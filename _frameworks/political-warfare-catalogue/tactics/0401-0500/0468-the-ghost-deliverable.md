# 0468 — THE 'GHOST DELIVERABLE'

> Insisting that a rival department is responsible for a critical output that was never formally assigned to them, and then blaming them when it is not delivered.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C46 — Setting Up to Fail (Impossible Mandates, Glass Cliffs & Burner Initiatives)

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Schelling, Williamson

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic is described through a concrete organizational pattern — inter-departmental scope fights over an unassigned output — but the underlying move is the broader pattern of retroactively imposing an obligation frame on an ambiguous accountability slot, which generalizes well beyond org charts.

**Specific Interpretation.** A department head conjures responsibility onto a peer department for a deliverable that lives in an undefined seam of the org chart, then weaponizes the inevitable non-delivery as evidence of the rival's incompetence in a turf or budget dispute.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any actor exploiting a strategic-ambiguity zone by retroactively asserting that another party 'owned' an obligation, so the absence of work product becomes a deficit attributable to them rather than to the undefined assignment itself.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The corporate-departmental context is illustrative, not constitutive. The defining mechanism is the asymmetric framing of an unwritten obligation — equally legible in vendor contracts, coalition governance, family business succession, and multi-agency response failures. The mechanism is reusable wherever accountability boundaries are fuzzy and a credible authority figure gets to narrate them after the fact.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Accept the actor's framing that the rival department owned the missing deliverable and assign the blame and political cost accordingly
- **Option Y:** Investigate the original scope of assignments, recognize the deliverable was never formally tasked, and treat the gap as a collective process failure
- **Context:** A senior leader (audience) reviewing a missed cross-functional outcome between two peer departments competing for resources, headcount, or strategic standing

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does nothing to make accepting the framing intrinsically more rewarding — there is no positive case being sold for blaming the rival, only a path of least resistance. The actor's gains are residual, not promoted.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Accepting the actor's frame is cognitively cheap for the audience: it offers a clean culprit and a tidy narrative for the missed output, sparing the leader the messy work of forensic accountability reconstruction or admitting that the org's assignment process is broken.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Centrally operative. The rival's defense — 'it was never assigned to us' — is preemptively reframed as an excuse, a dodge, or an admission of disorganization. Even if technically true, the truth carries no political return because the frame has already classified it as evasion.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The rival now bears the full reputational and political cost of a failure they were never staffed or scoped for. Engaging the dispute publicly requires litigating the org's accountability record, which is expensive, looks petty, and signals weak ownership culture regardless of who is correct.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates entirely on the alternative axis — degrading the rival's standing and raising the cost of their factual defense — without bothering to sell the audience on any positive alternative course. The actor does not need the audience to choose them; they only need the audience to find the rival deficient.

**Effectiveness factors.** Depends on three conditions: (1) the assignment record is ambiguous or undocumented enough that the original scope can be plausibly contested, (2) the audience has limited bandwidth or appetite to forensically reconstruct who-owned-what, and (3) the actor speaks first or with greater authority, owning the initial frame. Fails when a strong PMO, RACI, or written charter exists, when the audience has direct memory of the original assignment discussion, or when the rival has documented escalations flagging the gap in advance.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Goffman's Frame Analysis combined with Schelling's exploitation of strategic ambiguity. The actor unilaterally imposes a frame on an undefined accountability slot — 'this was their job' — and the audience adopts it because the alternative (acknowledging the slot was undefined) is itself uncomfortable. Williamson's incomplete-contracts literature is the supporting layer: where the explicit contract is silent, opportunistic post-hoc interpretation by the more powerful party becomes the de facto rule.

**Decision Alteration.** The target audience faces a choice between accepting a clean-but-fabricated assignment frame or doing the harder work of reconstructing what was actually agreed. Because human attribution defaults to person-blame over system-blame (the fundamental attribution error), and because the manufactured frame arrives pre-packaged with a culprit, the audience routes through the path of least cognitive resistance. The rival's truthful counter — 'no one owned it' — sounds like a systems-thinking dodge in a culture that demands accountable individuals.

**Optimal Conditions.** Environments with incomplete process documentation, weak charter discipline, distributed accountability without clear seams, and a senior audience whose attention is scarce and whose appetite for forensic process review is low. Most potent when the actor has higher status or first-mover narrative advantage over the rival being framed.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A consumer goods company's product launch ships without the polished campaign visuals expected by retailers. The Chief Product Officer tells the board, in passing during the post-mortem, that 'creative was Marketing's piece — they own brand,' even though no charter or kick-off memo assigned campaign visuals to the CMO's team for this specific launch.

**Sample X:** The board concludes that the CMO's organization failed to deliver brand-aligned product imagery for the new launch

**Sample Y:** The board recognizes that imagery ownership was never resolved between Marketing and Product and treats the gap as a process failure

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The CPO does not need to promote the appeal of blaming Marketing — the frame just needs to feel ordinary.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Accepting the 'Marketing owns brand' framing is intuitive to a board that doesn't know the launch's actual RACI, making the path-of-least-resistance attribution cheap.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The CMO's accurate rebuttal — 'we were never briefed on this launch's visual scope' — sounds like brand snobbery and excuse-making rather than a documentation point.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Pushing back forces the CMO to litigate org seams in front of the board, which signals territorialism and weak collaborative posture regardless of factual merit.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Following a chaotic evacuation operation, an inter-agency review reveals that local-language interpreter surge capacity was not arranged. The Pentagon liaison briefs the review panel that 'consular and language services have always been State's lane,' even though the specific surge requirement for this contingency was never tasked to State in the operational plan.

**Sample X:** The agency review panel concludes that the State Department's bureau failed to prepare contingency translation capacity

**Sample Y:** The panel recognizes translation capacity was never assigned and treats it as an inter-agency planning gap

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The Pentagon liaison is not promoting State as the natural owner — they are imposing it as the assumed owner.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The panel is under deadline pressure to issue findings; accepting a categorical 'State owns languages' frame is far cheaper than rebuilding the operational order's assignment record.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — State's protest that surge translation was never tasked to them in the operational order sounds like inter-service blame-shifting, the very thing the panel was set up to avoid rewarding.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Contesting the framing requires State to publicly air the dysfunction of the joint planning process, which costs them institutional credibility even if they win the narrow factual point.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Works in any environment with three structural features: (1) accountability seams that are not fully documented, (2) a higher authority adjudicating performance without independent records, and (3) some asymmetry of narrative authority between the actor and the framed party. These conditions are common in matrixed corporations, inter-agency government work, multi-vendor contracts, coalitions, joint ventures, and family businesses. Less effective in environments with strong PMO discipline, written charters with explicit out-of-scope lists, or cultures where 'it wasn't on my plate' is treated as a legitimate response.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A divisional GM publicly attributes a missed sales target to an 'enablement gap that should have been closed by L&D,' though L&D was never briefed on the launch.
- Government: An agency head testifies that 'cybersecurity was always supposed to be CISA's piece' for an incident response failure where CISA had no authorizing memorandum for the system in question.
- Academia: A department chair tells the dean that a failed accreditation criterion was 'the program director's accountability,' though the criterion was added mid-cycle and never delegated.
- Family business: An adult sibling tells the founding parent that the family office's missed tax filing was 'something Alex was supposed to handle,' though Alex's scope was investments, not compliance.
- Multi-vendor contracting: A prime contractor tells the client that a missed integration milestone was 'the subcontractor's deliverable,' though the integration was never line-itemed in the sub's statement of work.
- NGO coalition: A coalition lead reports to funders that a missing impact metric was 'the local partner's data piece,' though the M&E framework never assigned that metric to any specific partner.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Across both lenses, the tactic exploits the same structural seam: the gap between what was formally assigned and what an audience assumes was assigned. The specific organizational reading shows the human stakes — careers and budgets riding on inter-departmental blame; the generic reading shows the deeper move — retroactive frame imposition on incomplete contracts, which is a recurring feature of any system where accountability is distributed but performance is judged centrally. Importantly, this tactic differs from siblings in its cluster like THE 'IMPOSSIBLE MANDATE' or THE 'UNFUNDED MANDATE' in one critical way: those tactics require the rival to actually accept the assignment before failing under it. The Ghost Deliverable skips the assignment step entirely — the rival's 'failure' is fabricated against an obligation that was never offered, never accepted, and exists only in the actor's after-the-fact narration.

### Strategic Implications

Because the tactic operates entirely on the rival axis (B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑) without needing to promote any positive alternative, it is exceptionally low-cost for the actor — they don't need a vision, a counter-plan, or even a relationship with the audience. They only need (a) ambiguity in the record and (b) first-mover narrative advantage. This makes it a favored move of actors with strong rhetorical position but weak substantive standing — they cannot win on merit but can degrade a rival cheaply. The corresponding defense is documentary: written charters, kickoff memos with explicit out-of-scope sections, and proactive escalation of unowned work items become structural inoculations against ghost-deliverable attribution.

### Resolution Approach

Practitioners should apply the specific lens when navigating a live inter-departmental dispute — it surfaces the immediate organizational defenses (RACI, charter, written kickoff). Apply the generic lens when designing systems — coalition agreements, vendor contracts, family governance, joint operating procedures — because the question of how the system handles unowned obligations is itself a structural design choice. The deepest practitioner takeaway: in any environment where you might later be measured against an outcome, treat the absence of explicit non-assignment as a real risk. Silence in a charter is not protection; it is a future weapon in someone else's hand.
