# 0321 — THE 'NEXT BIG THING' DIVERSION

> Constantly introducing new management fads or methodologies (e.g., Agile, OKRs) to create the illusion of progress and keep the organization perpetually off-balance and focused on adapting rather than resisting.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C32 — Innovation Theater, Optics & Performative Austerity

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Festinger, Olson

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and examples anchor in a recognizable corporate context (rolling out Agile, OKRs, Lean, Six Sigma, OKRs-again-but-with-a-twist), yet the operating mechanism — perpetually moving the target so that no resistance coalition can form around a stable referent — is a broadly portable political move.

**Specific Interpretation.** A leadership team announces successive management methodologies as flagship transformations, sequencing rollouts so the organization is always digesting, training on, or pivoting away from a framework — never settling long enough to evaluate outcomes or organize around grievances.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any actor who controls the agenda introduces a constant stream of novel initiatives, narratives, or reform targets such that the audience's attention, identity-investment, and coordination capacity are absorbed by adaptation, leaving no stable object around which opposition can converge.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While 'management fads' is the canonical example, the underlying mechanism — denying opponents a fixed referent — recurs across political regimes (permanent revolution), media strategy (flooding the zone), and household power dynamics (constant rule changes). The fad-rollout flavor is one instantiation of a much older pattern of agenda-cycling as control.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Engage earnestly with each new methodology rollout — attend trainings, adopt the vocabulary, retool one's work to fit the current framework
- **Option Y:** Organize sustained resistance to the cycle itself, or push for stability and evaluation of the prior framework before adopting the next
- **Context:** A mid-to-large organization (often led by a CEO or transformation officer seeking visibility) where leadership announces a new operating methodology every 12-24 months, framing each as the strategic answer, while the organization is still mid-implementation on the prior one

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Adapting to the new framework is wrapped in the rhetoric of modernity, learning agility, and forward-leaning culture. Early adopters get visibility with leadership, training certificates, and inclusion in the 'transformation cohort' — real but modest career benefits that make compliance attractive without being the central driver.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Notably, adaptation is NOT made cheap — the tactic actually relies on adaptation being costly enough to absorb organizational bandwidth. If retraining and retooling were frictionless, the diversion wouldn't divert. The cost of compliance is a feature, not a bug, of the mechanism.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Resistance is framed as nostalgia, change-resistance, or 'attachment to the old way.' But more potently, any criticism of the current rollout is dismissed as missing the point because the next framework is already incoming — the benefit of being right about Lean is nullified once OKRs are the new conversation. The referent moves before the critique lands.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Sustained opposition requires a stable target. The constant methodology turnover means anyone who organizes against 'the Agile rollout' finds, six months later, they're fighting yesterday's war. The cost of resistance compounds because each cycle drains the opposition's credibility ('still complaining about Agile? we've moved on') and resets their organizing investment to zero.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration produces compliance not by making adaptation easy or appealing on its merits, but by ensuring that the alternative — sustained, coordinated resistance — has no fixed object to organize against. Each new framework rollout simultaneously offers a modest carrot (adoption status), discredits the prior critique as outdated, and resets the coordination clock for any forming opposition. The mechanism is temporal: it weaponizes the latency between coalition-formation and target-shift.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effective when leadership controls the methodology agenda, has resources to fund successive rollouts (consulting fees, training budgets, internal comms), and operates in a culture that valorizes 'transformation' as inherent virtue. Fails when the workforce develops shared cynical literacy about the cycle ('here comes another one'), when external scrutiny (board, investors, regulators) demands outcome evidence on the prior framework before approving the next, or when a charismatic internal voice successfully names the cycle itself as the problem.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Schelling's Coordination Problem combined with Olson's Logic of Collective Action — sustained opposition requires a stable focal point around which actors can converge, and constantly shifting the referent denies opponents the coordination object they need. Adjacent to organizational change-fatigue research (Abrahamson on 'management fashion cycles') and Festinger's cognitive dissonance (constant adaptation prevents identity attachment to any prior state worth defending).

**Decision Alteration.** Targets cannot evaluate the merits of resistance because the thing they would resist is already being replaced. The decision shifts from 'should I oppose this?' to 'is it worth opposing anything when the agenda will turn over before my opposition matures?' Most rational actors conclude no — and either comply performatively or disengage entirely, which is functionally equivalent for the actor running the tactic.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where the actor controls the agenda unilaterally, where the audience lacks institutional memory (high turnover, weak documentation), where novelty itself carries cultural prestige, and where evaluation of prior initiatives is structurally deferred or absent. Degrades when the audience develops shared meta-awareness of the cycle.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A newly-appointed CEO of a Fortune 500 consumer goods company announces a 'customer-first transformation' in year one, a 'digital-first transformation' in year two, and an 'AI-native operating model' in year three — each with its own SVP, its own consulting firm, its own metrics that will never be reported on after the next rollout supersedes them.

**Sample X:** Engage with the current strategic initiative as if it represents the organization's real direction

**Sample Y:** Organize a coalition to demand evaluation of the prior three initiatives before launching the next

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Each new initiative offers fresh opportunities for visibility — being named to the steering committee, presenting at the all-hands, getting selected for the early-adopter cohort. The carrots are real but small.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — adaptation is genuinely effortful, and the tactic depends on that effort absorbing attention. Cheap adaptation wouldn't divert.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Anyone calling for evaluation of the prior initiative is framed as obstructing the new one. The value of being right about last year's failure is zero once this year's transformation owns the narrative.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Coalition-building takes 6-18 months; the agenda turns over in 12-24. Any coalition that forms is already organizing against a target that's depreciating in salience, making the political ROI of resistance negative.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** An authoritarian or hybrid regime cycles through ideological framings — anti-corruption month, patriotic-education year, anti-Western-influence campaign, technological-self-reliance drive — keeping dissidents, journalists, and civil society perpetually responding to the latest theme rather than building durable critique infrastructure around the regime's underlying patterns.

**Sample X:** Adapt to the regime's newest ideological campaign or terminology shift

**Sample Y:** Maintain principled opposition rooted in a stable critique of the regime's core conduct

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Public alignment with the current campaign earns regime favor, contract eligibility, and protection from being on the wrong side. The benefits are tangible if modest, and they compound for those who pivot fastest.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Performative alignment costs identity coherence and self-respect — these are real costs the tactic does not reduce. Compliance erodes the comply-er; that erosion is part of how the tactic disorganizes the dissident class.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — An op-ed mapping the regime's 2023 corruption pattern lands in 2025 as a curiosity — the regime has moved on, and the public, exhausted by intervening campaigns, treats the critique as stale.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Dissident networks built around a specific abuse find themselves out-of-date by the time they have organizing capacity. Each new campaign forces reallocation of scarce attention, and sustained focus appears parochial against the regime's perpetual reframing.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any context where a single actor or coalition controls agenda-setting and faces a less-coordinated opposition: corporate transformations, authoritarian regimes, media-saturated political campaigns ('flood the zone'), family-business succession dramas with rotating crises, and even pedagogical or therapeutic settings where a counselor uses constant reframing to prevent a client from settling into entrenched objection. Less effective in stable institutional environments with strong evaluation cultures (academic peer review, regulated public utilities, mature regulatory agencies) where the audience has both the memory and the procedural standing to demand accounting on prior initiatives.

### Core Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A multinational CEO launches Six Sigma, then Agile, then OKRs, then 'AI-first,' announcing each as the operating system of the future and never publishing post-mortems on the prior three.
- Government: An administration cycles through signature legislative themes — infrastructure week, education month, border-security quarter — so that any coordinated opposition is always one cycle behind the messaging.
- Media/political: A populist leader generates a new controversy or enemy every news cycle, ensuring that critics responding to last week's outrage seem out of touch by the time their critique is published ('flooding the zone').
- Academic: A university president announces successive strategic plans — internationalization, interdisciplinarity, sustainability, AI-readiness — each launching before the prior is evaluated, keeping faculty senate critics adapting rather than organizing.
- Religious/cultic: A high-control group introduces new doctrinal emphases or revelations periodically, ensuring members are continually realigning rather than questioning the underlying authority structure.
- Family business: A founder-CEO rotates the heir-apparent narrative every 12 months, keeping each potential successor adapting to new expectations rather than coalescing with siblings into stable demands for clarity.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Across both lenses, the tactic's signature is temporal rather than substantive — it doesn't win the argument about any particular framework; it wins by ensuring no argument has time to mature. The methodology content is interchangeable (Agile, OKRs, the next thing); what matters is the cycle's tempo, which must be faster than the opposition's coordination clock. Within Cluster C32 (Innovation Theater & Optics), this tactic distinguishes itself from siblings like INNOVATION THEATER (which performs progress on a single sustained narrative) and PERMA-PROJECT (which keeps one initiative alive forever as cover) by inverting both: rapid succession of completed-then-abandoned initiatives, where the abandonment is the point.

### Strategic Implications

For the practitioner running this tactic, the resource demand is unusual — it requires not deep commitment to any framework but the agenda authority and budget to keep launching new ones. The cost is institutional memory: organizations subjected to this tactic lose the capacity to learn from any single initiative, which is fine for the agenda-runner but catastrophic for organizational performance over the long run. For the practitioner defending against it, the counter is not opposing any single framework but naming the cycle itself — making the meta-pattern legible so the audience develops shared literacy about what's happening.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a particular organization's transformation fatigue — it gives a concrete vocabulary for naming what employees often feel but cannot articulate. Use the generic lens when analyzing political agenda control, media manipulation, or any setting where a controlling actor exploits coordination latency. The mechanisms align (Strategy 13 in both); the application contexts diverge dramatically, and the choice of lens determines which counter-moves are even imaginable.
