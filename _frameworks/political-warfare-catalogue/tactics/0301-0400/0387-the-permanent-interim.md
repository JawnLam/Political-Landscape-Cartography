# 0387 — THE PERMANENT INTERIM

> Placing an individual in an 'Acting' leadership role indefinitely, granting them the responsibility to implement difficult changes without the formal authority, making them both dependent and disposable.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C83 — Strategic Ambiguity, Acting Roles & Ambiguous Authority

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Williamson, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a particular HR/organizational artifact — the 'Acting' designation held indefinitely — but the underlying mechanism is the timeless principal-agent move of yoking responsibility to revocable status. The specific designation and the abstract pattern are both substantively informative.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads as a concrete succession and reorganization device: a CEO, board, or parent-company executive places a manager into an 'Acting' role to drive an unpopular mandate (restructure, layoffs, integration) while withholding the title that would confirm their authority — keeping the option to confirm, replace, or scapegoat indefinitely.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads as the universal pattern of conferring responsibility without commensurate authority on an agent whose status is revocable at the principal's pleasure — engineering a maximally dependent, maximally disposable executor of difficult work.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The 'Acting' title is one cultural instantiation; the operative mechanism is the structural decoupling of responsibility from authority under a revocable status, which appears in regents, provisional governments, interim cabinet ministers, probationary tenure, and many contractual arrangements. The mechanism reusable beyond corporate succession is what gives the tactic its enduring shape, making the generic interpretation more fundamental.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Loyally execute the principal's difficult mandate (the restructure, the cost cuts, the integration) on the principal's terms, hoping confirmation to the permanent role follows
- **Option Y:** Exercise independent judgment, push back on the mandate, or build a personal power base that would let one negotiate authority commensurate with responsibility
- **Context:** A senior leader (CEO, board, parent-company head) appoints a manager to 'Acting' status — usually following a sudden departure or as a deliberate interim arrangement — and indefinitely defers the decision to confirm them, while assigning them the politically toxic work the principal does not want associated with a permanent successor

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The unspoken promise of confirmation — the title, the compensation jump, the permanent legitimacy — is the dangling carrot that makes diligent execution of the principal's mandate feel like an audition with real upside. The benefit is implied, not guaranteed, which is precisely what gives the principal continued leverage.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not make implementing difficult changes any easier — in fact it makes it harder, because the Acting holder cannot credibly invoke permanent positional authority to overrule resistance. The absence of this lever is diagnostic: this is not a facilitation tactic, it is an extraction tactic.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Independent judgment is hollowed out by the very designation. An Acting executive who tries to act like a permanent one finds their decisions discounted by subordinates ('she might not be here next quarter'), by peers ('let's wait for the real appointment'), and by external counterparties ('we need a permanent signatory'). The role itself drains the benefit of acting independently.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Pushing back on the principal, building a personal coalition, or asserting authority beyond the leash carries the immediate, visible cost of forfeiting confirmation. The principal can remove the Acting executive at any moment without the political cost of firing a confirmed officer — the disposability is permanent and unilateral. This is the operating core of the tactic.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic shifts the Acting executive's utility calculation by making loyal execution the only path with potential upside (B(X)↑), draining the benefits of independent action by structurally undermining their authority (B(Y)↓), and making any assertion of independence personally catastrophic by leaving their status revocable (C(Y)↑). The omission of C(X)↓ is the cruelty of the design — the work itself is not made easier; only the path to confirmation runs through compliant execution.

**Effectiveness factors.** Depends on the principal's credible discretion over confirmation, the absence of alternative landing places for the Acting executive (a strong outside option breaks the tactic), the political toxicity of the mandate (which would be costly to attach to a confirmed successor), and the cultural normalization of indefinite 'Acting' status. Fails when the Acting holder builds independent constituency strong enough to force confirmation, develops outside options, or recognizes the trap and either renegotiates terms early or walks away.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer's Power-Dependence Theory (drawing on Emerson) combined with Williamson's analysis of asymmetric asset specificity and hold-up. The Acting status engineers a maximally dependent agent: their accumulated investment in the role is highly specific to this principal's confirmation, while the principal retains a costless exit. Schelling's logic of credible non-commitment also applies — the principal carefully avoids any promise that would constrain their later choice.

**Decision Alteration.** Reframes the agent's choice from 'how should I exercise this role?' to 'how do I avoid being non-confirmed?' The decision horizon collapses from strategic stewardship to immediate principal-pleasing. Because authority is borrowed and revocable, every action is filtered through 'will this jeopardize my confirmation?' rather than 'is this right for the institution?' The agent self-disciplines more thoroughly than any explicit instruction could.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when the principal has unilateral control over confirmation, when the agent has high sunk costs (relocation, reputational stake, foregone alternatives), when the mandate is politically toxic enough that the principal wants distance from it, and when the cultural environment normalizes prolonged 'Acting' status as legitimate rather than a red flag.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A newly-installed Cabinet Minister inherits a department with deep cuts mandated by Treasury. Rather than appointing a permanent Deputy who would then have civil-service protections and an independent voice, the Minister keeps the previous Deputy in 'Acting' status while signaling that 'we're evaluating the right permanent appointment' — keeping the cuts moving while preserving full freedom to scapegoat if implementation goes badly.

**Sample X:** Acting Deputy Minister loyally implements the unpopular budget cuts the Minister has mandated, expecting eventual permanent appointment

**Sample Y:** Acting Deputy Minister pushes back, proposes alternative cuts that protect their own portfolio, or builds independent civil-service constituency

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The implied path to permanent appointment — the order-of-Canada-track career capstone, the pension uplift, the ability to refuse a future patronage demand from political position — hangs in the Minister's gift

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The actual work of cutting programs and laying off civil servants is not made easier — the Acting Deputy still has to face the unions and the affected directorates without the protective authority a confirmed Deputy would wield

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Pushing back loses its institutional weight because subordinates and external stakeholders treat the Acting Deputy as a placeholder whose objections need not be honored long-term

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Independent action invites the Minister to find an 'alternative permanent candidate,' ending the Acting Deputy's career trajectory without ever needing to formally dismiss them

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Mid-season the General Manager of a professional sports franchise fires the Head Coach and elevates an assistant to Acting Head Coach. The team is rebuilding; ownership wants visible accountability for a losing season; the GM wants no permanent commitment that would constrain a real coaching search at season's end. The Acting Coach takes the visible heat while the GM keeps options open.

**Sample X:** The Acting Head Coach delivers the rebuilding season on the General Manager's terms — playing the prospects, accepting the losing record, suppressing media frustration

**Sample Y:** The Acting Head Coach lobbies ownership directly, builds his own coaching staff loyalty, or publicly disagrees with the GM's roster strategy

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Removing the 'Acting' tag — and the multi-year guaranteed contract that comes with it — is the career-defining outcome dangled by compliant performance during the audition

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Coaching a losing rebuild against media skepticism is not made easier by Acting status; if anything, players know the role is unstable and discipline harder to enforce

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Public disagreement with the GM's roster decisions is structurally discounted — beat writers and players know an Acting Coach lacks the standing to overrule front-office strategy

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — End-of-season the GM hires an outside name as permanent Head Coach and quietly does not retain the Acting Coach on staff — no firing required, no buyout, no story

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies wherever a principal can structurally separate responsibility from authority under a revocable designation — corporate succession, civil service appointments, sports coaching, academic department chairs, military theatre commands, religious orders' provisional appointments, family-business interim leadership during succession disputes. Less effective in tenure-protected academic appointments or unionized environments where 'Acting' status carries explicit time limits, and ineffective when the agent has strong outside options that make the implicit confirmation promise insufficient leverage.

### Application Examples

- A private equity firm installs an 'Interim CEO' at a portfolio company to drive aggressive cost cuts and headcount reductions, keeping the option to replace with a permanent operator after the unpopular work is done
- A new university president keeps a controversial department chair in 'Acting' status while pushing through curriculum reforms the chair would have political cover to resist if confirmed with full faculty governance protections
- A regional sales VP elevates a manager to 'Acting Regional Director' during a territory consolidation, extracting integration work without granting the title and base-salary commitment a confirmed appointment would carry
- A federal administration retains an 'Acting' Secretary for years, exploiting the role to implement controversial policies without enduring Senate confirmation scrutiny and preserving the option to remove without political cost
- A bishop appoints a priest as 'Administrator' of a troubled parish — formally an interim canonical role — while indefinitely delaying the canonical appointment as Pastor that would confer ecclesiastical security of tenure
- A founder-CEO grooming a successor names them 'Acting COO with President duties' during a difficult turnaround, using the indefinite status to test loyalty and extract the hardest restructuring work before deciding on permanent succession

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The tactic's signature is the deliberate engineering of asymmetric reversibility: the principal can withdraw at any moment without cost, while the agent's accumulated investment is highly specific and non-portable. The 'Acting' designation is the visible artifact of an underlying hold-up problem made tactical — Williamson's asset-specificity logic operationalized as a personnel device. Distinguishing this from the sibling 'ACTING ROLE TRAP' (0434): the trap framing emphasizes the inducement and entrapment of accepting the role at all; the permanent interim framing emphasizes the indefinite duration as the operative cruelty — the trap deepens with every month not confirmed.

### Strategic Implications

For the principal, the tactic offers political distance from toxic work, retained optionality on succession, and a maximally compliant executor — at the cost of suboptimal execution (an Acting holder cannot wield full authority to make decisions stick) and the moral hazard of cultivating yes-men. For the agent, the only winning moves are to either secure a hard time-bound commitment before accepting the role, develop outside options that shorten one's tolerance window, or recognize the structure early and renegotiate terms before sunk costs make exit prohibitive. For the organization, prolonged Acting status is an institutional red flag: it usually signals that the principal is unwilling to commit to a real appointment because the work being delegated would not be politically survivable if attached to a permanent name.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing a particular succession decision, a single 'Acting' appointment, or counseling an executive considering whether to accept such a role — the choice architecture and effectiveness factors map directly to the practitioner's question. Use the generic lens when teaching the broader pattern of power-dependence engineering, when comparing across institutional settings (government, sports, religious orders, corporations), or when designing organizational policies (mandatory time limits on Acting status, automatic conversion clauses) intended to disable the tactic structurally.
