# 0376 — PROJECT REMORA

> Attaching a politically favored, low-value pet project to a critical, must-pass initiative, ensuring the pet project receives funding by riding the coattails of the essential program.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C34 — Trojan Horse Initiatives & Bait-and-Switch Scope

**Cited principles:** Kahneman, Tversky

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The metaphor anchors in a particular budget-cycle move (a 'pet project' grafted onto a 'must-pass initiative'), but the underlying mechanism — bundling a marginal item with an indispensable host so that approval of the host smuggles the marginal item across the threshold — is a generic packaging-of-decisions move with deep roots in legislative logrolling, contract bundling, and software dependency packaging.

**Specific Interpretation.** Within an organization, a sponsor with a low-priority initiative that could not survive standalone scrutiny attaches its scope, budget, and timeline to a critical program (a regulatory remediation, an ERP rollout, a Board-mandated transformation) so that the host program's must-pass status carries the pet project forward without independent justification.

**Generic Interpretation.** An influence pattern in which an actor packages a weak proposal with a strong one such that decision-makers cannot reject the weak item without paying the cost of rejecting the strong item. The mechanism shifts the choice from 'is this pet item worth it?' to 'are we willing to kill the critical thing in order to refuse this rider?'

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the language of 'pet project' and 'must-pass initiative' reads as organizational budgeting, the structural move — coupling a marginal item to a high-leverage host to neutralize its own merit review — is the canonical logrolling/rider tactic studied in public choice theory (Buchanan & Tullock) and reappears across legislation, M&A scope, vendor procurement, and software dependency politics. The host context is illustrative; the coupling mechanism is the durable insight.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Approve the bundled package — funding both the critical program and the attached pet project as a single line of effort
- **Option Y:** Strip the pet project out and approve only the critical program on its standalone merits
- **Context:** An annual operating-plan or capital-allocation review in which a politically connected sponsor has folded a low-value initiative (a pet platform, a vanity dashboard, a favored vendor engagement) inside the work-breakdown structure of a must-pass program such as regulatory remediation, audit response, or a Board-approved transformation

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 1 (Subtle)

The pet project borrows reflected legitimacy from the host program — it gets to wear the language of 'regulatory necessity' or 'strategic transformation' in steering-committee decks even though its own benefits are unchanged. The lift is real but secondary; the tactic's gravity is in the cost levers, not in genuinely making the pet item more attractive on its merits.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Operating core. The pet project's standalone scrutiny cost — business case, ROI defense, prioritization tournament, opportunity-cost comparison — is bypassed because it is reviewed as a sub-line of an already-approved program. Finance and PMO governance applies to the host's gating criteria, not to each embedded sub-scope. This is the entire reason the tactic exists.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

Stripping the pet project (the Y option) still produces the full benefit of approving the critical program; the tactic does nothing to diminish how attractive a clean, focused critical-program approval would be on its own. The sponsor needs decision-makers to find Y costly to execute, not unattractive in principle.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Operating core alongside C(X)↓. Removing the pet project mid-cycle now requires re-baselining the host program's scope, schedule, and budget, re-running governance approvals, and — most painfully — taking on the political cost of being seen to obstruct or delay a must-pass initiative for the sake of a line-item fight. The reviewer who would have killed the pet on its merits becomes the person who 'put the regulatory remediation at risk.'

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates purely on the cost dimension across both choices: it lowers the scrutiny cost of approving the pet project (by folding it into a pre-approved host) while raising the procedural and political cost of stripping it out (by entangling its removal with the host's schedule and the reviewer's reputation for supporting critical work). Benefits on either side remain essentially as they were — what changes is who pays what in process, schedule, and reputational currency to act on them.

**Effectiveness factors.** Works best when (a) the host program is genuinely urgent and time-bound, so re-baselining is expensive; (b) governance reviews scope at program-level rather than line-item level, allowing sub-scope to ride under the umbrella; (c) the bundling is done early enough that the pet project's footprint is normalized in the architecture before it is noticed; (d) reviewers feel reputational exposure for delays to the host. Fails when finance or PMO insists on line-item business cases, when an independent reviewer (audit, regulator) can demand scope decomposition, or when the host program's leader resents the parasitic attachment and is willing to publicly disown it.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Logrolling and package-deal coupling (Buchanan & Tullock, 'The Calculus of Consent,' 1962), reinforced by Tversky & Kahneman's bundling/integration effects — when a marginal item is integrated into a larger transaction, its costs are evaluated against the gross host figure rather than on its own scale, so the marginal addition looks small relative to the bundle and is psychologically absorbed.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic dissolves the standalone decision the marginal item could not survive. Instead of asking 'does this pet project clear the bar?' the decision-maker is forced to ask 'am I willing to blow up the host transaction over this rider?' Because the host's value vastly exceeds the rider's, even a decision-maker who would have rejected the rider on its merits rationally swallows it to preserve the host. The marginal item rides through on the host's surplus.

**Optimal Conditions.** Bundled approval architectures with limited line-item veto, time pressure on the host transaction, governance that scopes review at the package level, and a host whose collapse imposes asymmetrically heavy cost on the very reviewers who would otherwise scrutinize the rider. Effectiveness compounds when the attached item's footprint is small enough relative to the host that its inclusion seems administratively trivial.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A U.S. congressional appropriations cycle in which the chair of a subcommittee inserts a district-specific earmark into a continuing resolution that must pass by the fiscal year-end to avoid a government shutdown

**Sample X:** Pass an omnibus appropriations bill containing both critical defense funding and an unrelated rider funding a single legislator's pet infrastructure project in their district

**Sample Y:** Strip the pet infrastructure rider and pass a clean defense appropriations bill

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The rider inherits the rhetorical legitimacy of 'voting for our troops' or 'keeping the government open,' giving the earmark a halo it could not claim on its own merits

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The earmark bypasses standalone committee hearings, CBO scoring debate, and floor amendments — it is approved by inclusion in a package whose passage is pre-determined by the host's necessity

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — A clean defense bill would deliver its full intended benefit; the tactic does not degrade what a stripped bill would accomplish

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Stripping the rider requires reopening the package, restarting negotiations, and risking a shutdown — turning every opponent of the rider into a person 'willing to shut down the government over an earmark'

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A B2B contract negotiation between a manufacturer and a payer covering tens of millions of patient-lives, where the blockbuster's formulary placement is the strategic prize and the legacy compound is a low-margin product the manufacturer wants to keep above an unprofitable threshold

**Sample X:** A pharmaceutical company bundles a low-volume legacy compound into the contract terms for a high-volume blockbuster drug rebate negotiation with a national health insurer

**Sample Y:** The insurer negotiates the blockbuster rebate on its own terms and either drops or separately negotiates the legacy compound

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The legacy compound's clinical value to the insurer is unchanged by the bundling — its therapeutic profile is what it is

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Accepting the legacy compound's terms inside the master agreement avoids opening a separate negotiation track and consuming additional pharmacy-committee review cycles

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — A standalone blockbuster deal would still deliver the blockbuster's full value; the tactic doesn't reduce what the insurer gets from the headline product

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Refusing to bundle puts the blockbuster negotiation itself at risk and signals the insurer is willing to delay formulary placement over a minor-product term — a position no payer-relations team wants to defend internally

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** The tactic generalizes to any context with package-level approval and limited line-item veto: legislative riders and omnibus bills, M&A scope inclusions, vendor master agreements, software dependency bundling (a package manager pulling in a maintainer's unrelated library), university capital campaigns, and consent-decree negotiations. It loses force only where governance can decompose the package and approve sub-items independently, or where the host's champions actively police what gets attached.

### Core Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- A legislator attaches an unrelated district earmark to a defense authorization bill that must pass before the fiscal year ends, daring opponents to vote against the troops
- A divisional VP folds a favored consulting vendor's discovery work into the scope of a Board-mandated ERP program, sidestepping the standalone vendor-selection RFP
- A grants administrator at a research university packages a low-impact pet center into the indirect-cost allocation of a large NIH-funded program project, riding the program's overhead approval
- A union negotiator inserts a job-classification protection for a small bargaining unit into a master contract whose ratification the employer needs to avert a strike
- A software library maintainer ships a controversial sub-module as a non-optional dependency of a critical security patch, ensuring downstream adoption by everyone who upgrades
- A trade-deal negotiator attaches a domestic industry's narrow protection clause to a multilateral tariff agreement that the other side cannot walk away from without losing the headline market access
- A diocese folds funding for an unpopular pastoral initiative into the cathedral restoration capital campaign, knowing donors won't pull pledges over a sub-line they only see in the appendix

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

PROJECT REMORA is a pure cost-architecture move: it does not try to make the pet item attractive, it makes the act of refusing the pet item expensive. This places it close to its cluster siblings (TROJAN HORSE INITIATIVE, THE 'BLANK CHECK' INITIATIVE, BEAR HUG) but distinguishable from each — the Trojan Horse smuggles a controversial change inside an innocuous shell so it isn't recognized; the Blank Check expands authority once approval is given; the Bear Hug overloads a rival with embraced commitments. The Remora is narrower and quieter: it leaves the host program intact and recognizable, attaches a separable rider, and counts on the rider being too small to fight over given what fighting would cost.

### Strategic Implications

Reviewers facing a suspected remora attachment should resist the impulse to litigate it on the merits in real time — that fight is rigged because the host's urgency is doing the rider's work. The asymmetric move is procedural: insist on line-item decomposition before the host's deadline calcifies, route the rider to an independent review track, or make the host's champion publicly defend the attachment (which often produces voluntary detachment). For sponsors using the tactic, the fragility is visibility: once a remora is named as such, the cost-contrast collapses because reviewers can now strip it without being seen as obstructing the host — the obstruction frame transfers to the sponsor who tried to smuggle it.

### Resolution Approach

Read the tactic as specific (named-organizational remora attachment) when diagnosing a particular budget-cycle move and deciding whether and how to challenge it in current governance. Read it as generic (logrolling/package-bundling) when designing governance systems that should resist it structurally — line-item review mandates, scope-baseline freezes, independent decomposition rights — or when recognizing the same move appearing in non-budget contexts (legislative riders, M&A scope, vendor bundling, software dependencies). The strategy classification is consistent across both readings — Cost Contrast (8) — because the underlying lever architecture is identical; only the host and rider change.
