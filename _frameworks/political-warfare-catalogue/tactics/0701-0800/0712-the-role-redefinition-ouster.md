# 0712 — THE 'ROLE REDEFINITION' OUSTER

> Eliminating a targeted individual by redefining their existing role with a new set of requirements they clearly lack, making them unqualified without a direct firing.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C41 — Termination Tactics, Departure Engineering & Resignation Politics

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Williamson

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a concrete HR/managerial move (rewriting a specific person's job description with requirements they demonstrably lack) but the underlying mechanism — retroactively shifting qualification criteria to engineer disqualification — is a reusable pattern that appears in promotion gating, tenure review, vendor requalification, regulatory licensure, and even immigration policy.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads the tactic as an organizational HR/leadership maneuver: a manager wants a specific incumbent out, but firing them outright would carry severance, legal, reputational, or relational costs, so the role itself is rewritten — typically under cover of a strategy refresh, transformation, or reorganization — so that the incumbent fails a face-neutral qualification screen.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads the tactic as a broader influence pattern of retroactive criteria-shifting: any actor with authority over the rules of a position uses that authority to redefine the position around the target's gaps, manufacturing legitimate-looking disqualification rather than confronting the underlying preference.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The named HR context is the most visible expression, but the operational signature — using control over criteria-definition to disqualify rather than confront — recurs in any institution where the criteria for fitness/eligibility/qualification are themselves authored by an interested party. The mechanism is more fundamental than any single context, so the generic reading is the load-bearing one.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The targeted employee resigns, accepts a 'mutual separation,' or is non-controversially exited for failing to meet the new role requirements
- **Option Y:** The targeted employee remains in their role, continues drawing salary and political standing, and forces the manager to either accept them or fire them outright
- **Context:** A senior manager wants to remove a tenured, protected, or politically inconvenient subordinate (long service, demographic protection, board relationships, union shield, or a strong performance record on the old criteria) without absorbing the costs of a direct termination

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not sweeten the exit for the target — there is typically no enhanced severance, no graceful narrative offered, no career-bridge support. The benefit of leaving is whatever the labor market provides; the perpetrator deliberately avoids investing in making exit attractive because doing so would signal that the redefinition is a pretext.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Leaving is not made easier. In fact the tactic often makes resignation more humiliating by forcing the target to either lie about the reason or admit being squeezed out. The absence of this lever is a signature feature — true facilitation would compromise the manager's plausible deniability.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The redefinition strips the role of the elements the incumbent excelled at and reweights it around capabilities they conspicuously lack. Their accumulated credibility, network, and institutional knowledge — the benefits of staying — are revalued to near zero under the new scorecard. Yesterday's strength becomes today's irrelevance.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Staying now means working under criteria the incumbent cannot satisfy, with predictable bad reviews, performance improvement plans, peer awareness of the mismatch, and an eventual for-cause termination on paper rather than off it. The cost of continuing is the slow public dismantling of one's professional identity.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic does not sell exit; it makes incumbency untenable. By acting only on the alternative axis — devaluing what the incumbent brings while raising the daily cost of remaining — the manager forces a 'voluntary' resignation that looks like the incumbent's own decision. Crucially, leaving untouched both levers on X preserves the fiction that the exit was the target's free choice rather than an engineered outcome.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on (a) the manager's unilateral or covered authority to rewrite the role under a plausible business pretext such as a strategy shift, transformation, or org redesign, (b) the new criteria being defensibly framed as forward-looking business needs rather than personal preference, (c) the target lacking external leverage — a board champion, regulator visibility, or union grievance route — that could force the redefinition into discovery, and (d) the timeline being long enough for the slow disqualification to play out before the target can mount a discrimination or constructive-dismissal case.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Goffman's frame analysis and spoiled identity, reinforced by Williamson's logic of opportunistic recontracting under asset specificity. The target's professional identity is reframed from 'competent incumbent' to 'misfit' by changing the frame through which their work is read; the move is economically possible because the target has made role-specific investments (institutional knowledge, internal networks) that have low value elsewhere, making them dependent on the very actor who controls the criteria.

**Decision Alteration.** The target is not persuaded that leaving is good — they are placed in a situation where remaining ceases to be coherent. The tactic alters the choice by re-describing reality: the role the target was succeeding at no longer exists as defined; what now exists in its place is a role they cannot do. The decision shifts not because new information enters the target's utility function but because the object of their attachment has been redefined out from under them.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when (a) the criteria-author has structural authority to revise role definitions without independent review, (b) the redefinition can be wrapped in a legitimating narrative such as transformation, modernization, or strategy refresh, (c) the target has high asset-specific investment and limited external mobility, and (d) the organization's culture treats job descriptions as objective business documents rather than as politically contestable artifacts.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A mid-cap industrial company under activist-investor pressure to digitize. The CEO wants a digital-native lieutenant but cannot fire a respected operator with board allies. The role description is rewritten by HR under the cover of the digital strategy refresh.

**Sample X:** A long-tenured chief operating officer accepts a 'mutual separation' after being told the COO role is being repositioned as a 'Chief Transformation Officer' requiring deep AI/ML and digital-platform experience she does not have

**Sample Y:** She remains as COO, forcing the CEO to either retain her or fire her for cause despite her decade of strong operational reviews

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — No enhanced exit package is offered beyond contractual minimum; doing so would tip off the board that the redefinition was a pretext.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The exit narrative is not softened — the target must explain to her network why she is leaving a role she had held successfully for years, with no graceful frame provided.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Her operational track record is revalued to zero under the new role spec, which centers on capabilities she never claimed to have; staying means holding a title whose substance has been hollowed out.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Remaining means working under KPIs tied to digital initiatives she cannot lead, predictable failed performance reviews, and eventual for-cause termination — a worse exit than resigning now.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A research university where the dean wants a more grant-aggressive chair but cannot remove a sitting humanities-oriented chair mid-term without provoking a faculty senate fight. The position description is rewritten as part of a 'strategic plan refresh.'

**Sample X:** A tenured academic department chair quietly steps down from the chair role after the dean revises the position's expectations to require a major grant portfolio she does not have

**Sample Y:** She insists on completing her chair term, forcing the dean to either tolerate her or initiate a contested removal under faculty governance procedures

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — No enhanced exit is offered — she simply returns to her tenured faculty line, no sweetener attached.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Stepping down still carries reputational cost; the dean does not provide a graceful framing of her departure.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The administrative authority and convening power she enjoyed as chair are recast as inadequate without the new grant-development mandate; the role's status under the new definition is diminished.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Continuing as chair means being annually evaluated against grant metrics she will predictably miss, with public chair-performance reviews now built into the new role definition.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** The tactic generalizes to any institutional context where (a) a target occupies a defined position, (b) someone other than the target controls the criteria that define that position, and (c) direct removal carries costs the criteria-author wishes to avoid. This includes corporate role redefinition, academic position re-specification, regulatory requalification of incumbent license-holders, vendor re-prequalification rounds engineered to drop a specific supplier, board committee charter rewrites that disqualify particular directors, and even immigration visa category redefinitions used to retroactively reduce eligibility of incumbent holders. Less effective when the criteria are co-authored (collective bargaining, joint committees, regulator-imposed) or when the target has standing to sue for pretextual treatment.

### Core Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CEO restructures a respected division head's role to require capabilities the incumbent lacks, framed as a 'pivot to AI-led operations,' producing a quiet resignation rather than a board-visible firing.
- Academic: A provost rewrites a tenured chair's job description to add 'industry partnership development' as a core duty, knowing the humanities-trained chair has no such network, prompting her voluntary return to faculty.
- Government: A new administrator rewrites a career civil servant's position description to add specialized credentials the incumbent lacks, allowing a non-political reassignment that bypasses civil service protections.
- Regulatory: A licensing body revises continuing-education requirements for an entire class of incumbent practitioners, retroactively disqualifying a politically inconvenient subset under a face-neutral standard.
- Procurement: A corporate procurement office rewrites vendor prequalification standards mid-cycle — adding ESG, data-sovereignty, or geographic-redundancy requirements — to drop a long-standing supplier the new CFO wants replaced.
- Diplomacy/NGO: A multilateral organization rewrites the terms of reference for a country-director post under the cover of a 'localization strategy,' producing the non-renewal of an internationally recruited incumbent without the optics of removal.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both readings converge on a single operating signature: the perpetrator never argues against the target on the merits and never directly invokes the desired outcome (the target's exit). Instead, control over the rules of the position is used to redefine the position around the target's gaps. This is what distinguishes the tactic from siblings in the cluster — unlike the 'Graceless Exit' or 'Exit Strategy Blackmail,' which act on the target directly, role redefinition acts on the role and lets the target's response to a changed environment supply the exit. The cluster's preemptive surrender and fake-recruiter ploys try to make leaving attractive; role redefinition makes staying impossible. Y Degradation, not X enhancement, is the engine.

### Strategic Implications

Role redefinition is the criteria-author's signature move, and its power is proportional to how unilaterally the criteria can be set. Practitioners on the offensive should recognize that the tactic's defensibility lives in the legitimating wrapper — strategy refresh, transformation, modernization, ESG, AI pivot — because the wrapper provides the face-neutral justification that distinguishes engineered disqualification from disguised firing. Practitioners on the defensive should recognize that by the time the new role description is circulating, the decision has already been made: contesting individual criteria is a losing game because each criterion appears defensible in isolation; the contestable move is the redefinition itself, framed as pretext, and the time to challenge it is before HR signs off, not after the first performance review under the new standard.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when the unit of analysis is a single incumbent and the question is whether to resign, contest, or invoke external leverage — here the lever configuration of Y Degradation maps directly to the experience of being squeezed out without being fired. Use the generic lens when the unit of analysis is institutional design or governance reform — here the takeaway is that giving any single actor unilateral authority over position-criteria definition creates a structural vulnerability that no individual performance-management policy can close. The deepest practitioner takeaway: where you cannot fire and where firing would be costly, you can almost always rewrite — and the legitimacy of the rewrite is supplied by the strategy narrative wrapping it, not by the rewrite's merits.
