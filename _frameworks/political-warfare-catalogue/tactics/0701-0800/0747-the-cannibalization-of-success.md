# 0747 — THE 'CANNIBALIZATION' OF SUCCESS

> Undermining a successful existing product or service to promote a new, politically favored initiative, even if the new initiative is less effective.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C91 — Encirclement, Jurisdictional Creep & Cannibalization

**Cited principles:** Festinger, Staw

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a specific corporate phenomenon (product/portfolio cannibalization) but the mechanism — actively degrading something that works to clear room for a sponsor's pet project — generalizes far beyond product lines into any setting where a working incumbent embarrasses an emerging favorite. The ambiguity sits in whether 'cannibalization' is read as a product-economics term or as the political destruction of a functioning asset.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads as a corporate-portfolio maneuver: an executive sponsoring a new initiative starves, hobbles, or rebrands a successful existing product so its continued success cannot show up the new bet on metrics, narrative, or budget allocation.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads as the universal political pattern of sacrificing a working incumbent — a program, team, methodology, or person — that has become a comparison threat to a politically-backed alternative. The mechanism is destruction-by-neglect or destruction-by-disparagement of evidence that would otherwise refute the favored choice.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The corporate-product framing is the vivid setting, but the political logic — eliminating a benchmark that would expose the favored option's inferiority — appears identically in government program cuts, academic methodology displacement, military doctrine fights, and family business succession. The mechanism is what travels; the product context is just the most legible host.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Resources, attention, and organizational narrative shift to the politically favored new initiative
- **Option Y:** The successful existing product retains its funding, talent, and visibility as the comparison standard
- **Context:** A senior sponsor has personally tied their reputation to a new initiative (often a digital reinvention, a 'next-generation' platform, or a vanity-acquired capability) inside a company where the legacy product still generates the majority of revenue or customer satisfaction. The legacy product's continued success would make the new initiative look unnecessary or weaker on every measurable axis.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The new initiative is framed as the strategic future — 'where the puck is going,' 'the platform of the next decade,' the answer to a board-level mandate — giving its sponsors and adopters a forward-looking halo that the legacy product, by definition, cannot share.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not make the new initiative cheaper or easier to execute — in fact it usually makes the new bet more expensive and risky. The mechanism operates by removing competition, not by reducing X's own friction. This absence is diagnostic: cannibalization-of-success is a destruction play, not a facilitation play.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The successful product is actively reframed as 'legacy,' 'declining,' 'cash cow only,' 'not where growth is' — language that drains the perceived strategic benefit of its continued performance regardless of the actual financials. Roadmap items are denied, headline customers are reassigned to the new initiative's reference list, and reporting categories are redrawn so the old product's wins disappear into 'mature business' line items.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Talent is poached onto the new initiative, budget is reallocated mid-cycle, the product's GMs are quietly denied promotion paths, and any defender of the working product is branded a backward-looking 'optimizer' rather than a future-builder. Continuing to champion the legacy product becomes a career-limiting move even when it is objectively the better business decision.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The political payoff requires the legacy product to look worse than it is, not the new initiative to look better than it is. So the tactic is asymmetrically weighted on the Y side — degrading the benefit of the working asset and raising the cost of defending it — while the X side gets only the narrative lift of 'future-facing.' Reducing the new initiative's own cost would mean actually de-risking it, which the sponsor often cannot or will not do; easier to destroy the comparison than to win it.

**Effectiveness factors.** Works when the sponsor controls portfolio reporting categories, headcount allocation, and the strategic-narrative apparatus (offsite framing, analyst-day language, board decks). Fails when the legacy product has external champions — large customers, regulators, a press story — who notice and protest the deliberate starving. Also fails when finance has the independence and stomach to publish the actual revenue and margin contribution side by side, since the numbers usually betray the politics.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance Theory (closest single fit), reinforced by Staw's escalation-of-commitment dynamic. When a sponsor has staked identity on a new bet, the continued success of a working alternative produces dissonance — the favored choice cannot be 'the future' if the present already works. Dissonance is resolved not by abandoning the new bet (too costly to identity) but by destroying the dissonant evidence: the successful incumbent itself.

**Decision Alteration.** Removes the comparison case rather than winning against it. Decision-makers further up the chain who would otherwise notice the legacy outperforming the favorite are denied the comparison — the legacy is reclassified, defunded, or rhetorically downgraded until it no longer serves as a benchmark. The choice the organization 'sees' is no longer 'working thing vs. new thing' but 'declining thing vs. future thing.'

**Optimal Conditions.** A sponsor with portfolio authority, identity-stake in the new initiative, and enough narrative control to redefine reporting categories. Most virulent when the legacy success was built by a rival or predecessor whose continued credit would politically threaten the sponsor.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A newly appointed agency head launches a flagship modernization office to demonstrate impact within their term. An older bureau in the same agency has been quietly solving the same problem at lower cost for fifteen years. The bureau's continued success would make the flagship redundant, so the new agency head reorganizes reporting so the bureau's metrics now roll up under the modernization office's banner, then 'sunsets' the bureau as a separate line item.

**Sample X:** A new government department's signature program absorbs the funding, mandate, and press of an older bureau

**Sample Y:** The older bureau retains its budget and its visible performance record on the same problem

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The modernization office is positioned as 'this administration's signature reform' — congressional testimony, ribbon-cuttings, and budget justifications all attach political identity to it.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — the new office is more expensive and slower to deliver. The tactic does not facilitate it; it removes its rival.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The old bureau's wins are folded into the new office's quarterly reports, so the bureau no longer accrues visible credit even when its underlying work continues to drive the results.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Senior career civil servants who built the bureau are reassigned, retired, or denied the political cover they previously enjoyed; defending the bureau publicly becomes career-ending under the new agency head.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A new department chair at a teaching hospital has secured a multi-million-dollar grant tied to robotic surgical adoption rates. Senior surgeons have data showing the manual technique still produces equivalent or better outcomes for the most common case mix. The chair restructures the case-assignment system so the most teachable cases route to the robotic suite, reframes the manual technique as 'traditional' in resident curricula, and quietly closes the operating slots that supported the manual program's volume — making the manual technique's outcome data degrade simply by atrophy.

**Sample X:** A surgical department adopts a new robotic procedure championed by the department chair as the preferred technique

**Sample Y:** The established manual technique, performed by senior surgeons with better published outcomes, remains the recommended standard

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Robotic adoption is tied to the department's external reputation, grant renewal, and the chair's national profile — supporting it signals being 'on the right side of the field.'

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — robotic surgery remains more expensive per case. The chair's tactic destroys the comparison case rather than improving robotic economics.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Curricular reframing and case-assignment reroutes drain the manual technique's institutional visibility; over time its data shrinks because its volume shrinks.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Senior surgeons who publicly defend the manual technique are described as 'resistant to innovation,' affecting their committee assignments and trainee allocation.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any setting with (a) portfolio authority concentrated in one sponsor, (b) identity-stake in a new bet, and (c) an existing working asset whose continued success would embarrass the bet. Seen across corporate product portfolios, government program rationalizations, academic methodology fights, military doctrine succession battles, nonprofit program transitions, and family business generational handovers. Less effective where the working asset has external constituents — paying customers, voters, regulators — who can independently observe and protest the deliberate degradation.

### Core Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- A consumer-tech CEO sunsetting a profitable legacy hardware line to force users onto a subscription platform she championed publicly, even as customer satisfaction with the hardware exceeded the platform on every measured dimension.
- A new defense secretary defunding a successful procurement program built by the previous administration to clear budget and narrative room for a flagship next-generation system whose performance is still unproven.
- An incoming department chair restructuring graduate funding away from a productive research methodology associated with their predecessor and toward a competing methodology they have published in.
- A regional sales VP reassigning the top-performing account managers off a profitable mature territory and onto a new-market initiative, so the mature territory's declining numbers can be cited as evidence that 'the future is in the new region.'
- A foundation's incoming president letting a long-running, externally celebrated grants program quietly atrophy through staff non-replacement and renewal-cycle delays, freeing the budget for a signature initiative bearing their name.
- A second-generation family business owner withdrawing distribution support from the founder's flagship product to push a personally-designed replacement, allowing the flagship's market share to decline as evidence the replacement was 'inevitable.'

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

This is destruction-of-evidence dressed as portfolio management. Unlike the sibling tactic 'CANNIBALIZATION STRATEGY' (which can be legitimate strategic obsolescence — Apple killing the iPod for the iPhone), the 'cannibalization OF SUCCESS' variant is specifically political: the kill happens not because the new bet is genuinely better, but because the continued visibility of the working incumbent would refute the sponsor's chosen narrative. The tactic's signature is the asymmetric lever pattern — heavy on Y-side destruction, light on X-side merit — which is exactly the inverse of what a confident sponsor of a genuinely superior alternative would need. When you see a sponsor unable to win on X's own benefits and instead pouring energy into degrading Y, you are watching this tactic.

### Strategic Implications

The Y-Focused Triple configuration is the diagnostic tell. A leader confident in a new initiative's intrinsic merit would activate the X-Optimization or Enhancement combinations — they would lower the cost of adoption and amplify the new initiative's benefits, letting comparison happen on level ground. The decision to instead degrade the incumbent reveals that the sponsor knows, at some level, that fair comparison would lose. For practitioners, this is a high-signal tactic: it works in the short term (sponsor wins the budget cycle) but tends to destroy enterprise value, since the cash-generating asset has been deliberately starved to support an identity-protective bet. Defenders should refuse to play on rhetoric and instead force same-page side-by-side metrics — finance discipline is the kryptonite for this move, which is why this tactic also tends to attack the independence of financial reporting.

### Resolution Approach

Treat the specific (product-portfolio) reading as the most useful frame when working inside a company watching this happen in real time — it names the levers and predicts which numbers will be reclassified next. Treat the generic (destruction-of-comparison) reading as the more useful frame when advising boards, regulators, or successor leaders trying to recover from the damage after the favored bet underperforms and the legacy asset has been hollowed out beyond recoverability. The two readings converge on Strategy 13 (Y-Focused Triple) precisely because the tactic's asymmetry is its essence in both lenses.
