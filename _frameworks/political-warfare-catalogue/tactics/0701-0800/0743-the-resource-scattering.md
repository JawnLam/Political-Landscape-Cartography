# 0743 — THE 'RESOURCE SCATTERING'

> Fragmenting the resources, data, or personnel required for an initiative across multiple departments, making coordination impossible without centralized political intervention.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C15 — Scarcity Engineering & Resource Access Politics

**Cited principles:** Williamson, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description names a concrete organizational maneuver (deliberately distributing budget, data, and headcount across departmental silos) but the operative force is a generic structural principle: any actor who can force a coordination problem into existence becomes the natural broker of its solution. The title points to a specific anti-pattern, while the mechanism reads as an instance of artificial transaction-cost manufacturing.

**Specific Interpretation.** A power-holder, typically a senior executive or chief of staff in a matrixed organization, intentionally splits the resources needed for a target initiative across reporting lines so that no single sponsor can move it forward without routing through their office for arbitration, prioritization calls, and resource unlocks.

**Generic Interpretation.** The fundamental influence pattern is the deliberate manufacture of coordination friction to create dependence on a gatekeeper. Any system where assets can be intentionally distributed across separately-controlled nodes admits this move — the broker's value is proportional to the friction they alone can resolve.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Although the language of 'departments' and 'centralized political intervention' anchors the tactic in corporate structure, the underlying principle — controlling power by controlling whose problem the coordination problem is — generalizes to coalitions, supply chains, legislative committees, and federated systems. The corporate dressing is one application of a structural move that long predates the modern firm.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Sponsors of the initiative approach the central political broker to negotiate cross-departmental resource unlocks, coordination, and prioritization
- **Option Y:** Sponsors attempt to coordinate the fragmented resources directly through lateral department-to-department negotiation, without the broker's involvement
- **Context:** A matrixed organization (e.g., a multi-business-unit corporation or a complex government agency) where a strategically important initiative depends on data held by IT, budget controlled by Finance, headcount owned by HR, and operational capacity assigned to a business unit — each reporting through different chains to a senior executive who has engineered or quietly preserved the fragmentation

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not enhance the intrinsic value of routing through the central broker — it does not promise faster execution, better outcomes, or strategic benefit. Approaching the broker is attractive only because it is the sole viable path, not because of any positive feature added to that path. The absence of B(X)↑ is diagnostic: this is structural coercion, not persuasion.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Routing through the broker becomes the lowest-friction option by construction. The broker has the standing to override departmental priorities with a single conversation, collapsing what would otherwise be weeks of lateral negotiation into a single decision point. The 'cost' of the political path is reduced relative to the alternative — though only because the alternative has been deliberately inflated.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The perceived value of direct lateral coordination is diminished because each fragmented piece, taken alone, is insufficient — a sponsor who secures the data without the budget, or the budget without the headcount, ends up with stranded partial wins. The yield from heroic lateral effort looks low even when the effort is heroic.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. Each department head has their own priorities, incentives, and political alliances; assembling the full resource bundle through lateral negotiation requires the sponsor to spend political capital with multiple counterparties, accept timing delays as departments serialize their attention, and absorb the reputational risk of being seen as 'going around' the senior whose office would normally arbitrate. The cost of the direct path compounds with every additional fragment that must be re-integrated.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic produces the intended routing not by selling the broker's path as inherently valuable but by making every alternative path so costly and so unrewarding that the broker becomes the rational default. The configuration is asymmetric on the cost side — the broker doesn't need to promote themselves, only to ensure that the topology of fragmentation persists. The absence of B(X)↑ is what distinguishes this from active patronage politics: the broker stays clean, lets the structure do the work, and is approached rather than approaching.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on the broker's standing being credible enough that sponsors do not bypass them upward to the CEO or board; on the fragmentation being old enough or naturalized enough that it appears as 'how the org works' rather than a deliberate choice; and on no countervailing executive launching a re-integration or process-redesign campaign. Failure modes include a CEO who personally champions an initiative and crashes through the fragmentation, or a sponsor coalition large enough to publicly name the broker's gatekeeping as a bottleneck.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer & Salancik's Resource Dependence Theory, combined with Williamson's transaction cost economics. Power accrues to actors who control critical resources and the terms of their exchange; when resources are fragmented across independently-controlled nodes, transaction costs for any consumer rise, and whoever can lower those costs becomes structurally indispensable.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic shifts the consumer's calculus from 'which path delivers the most value' to 'which path is feasible at all.' By manufacturing coordination cost in the alternatives, the broker's path becomes the path of least resistance — not by being attractive, but by being the only one that closes. Over time, the consumer stops even evaluating direct paths, and the broker's role naturalizes into 'how things get done here.'

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in systems with (a) genuinely interdependent resources whose value depends on being assembled, (b) decentralized resource ownership with no countervailing integrator role, (c) opacity about who could approve re-integration, and (d) low transparency on the cost of fragmentation itself, so sponsors blame their own coordination skills rather than the structure.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A legislative chamber where jurisdiction over a complex bill (touching tax, commerce, judiciary, and appropriations) has been preserved across four separate committees rather than consolidated into one, and the whip is the only office that habitually arbitrates the scheduling conflicts between them

**Sample X:** A coalition seeking legislative passage routes through the majority whip's office to coordinate committee scheduling, witness lists, and amendment timing

**Sample Y:** The coalition attempts to coordinate directly with each committee chair, ranking member, and floor leader to assemble a path to passage

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Routing through the whip does not improve the bill's substantive merits or its odds of passing on policy grounds — it only resolves the procedural deadlock.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The whip can resolve four committee-scheduling conflicts in a single phone call, while the coalition would need to negotiate separately with each chair's staff over weeks.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Even successful direct coordination with three of four committees produces a stalled bill — partial wins do not translate into a floor vote, devaluing heroic lateral effort.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each direct approach to a committee chair burns favor with that chair while implicitly disrespecting the others, and consumes the coalition's limited political capital faster than the bill's calendar allows.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A multi-national, multi-service military campaign where logistics, intelligence collection assets, airlift, and special operations forces remain under separate service or national command, and the CJTF headquarters is the only entity with cross-cutting authority to deconflict competing demands

**Sample X:** Joint operations commanders request assets through the Combined Joint Task Force headquarters, which arbitrates inter-service prioritization

**Sample Y:** Commanders attempt to negotiate directly with each service's logistics command for sealift, airlift, satellite bandwidth, and special-operations capacity

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Routing through CJTF does not improve the tactical effectiveness of the requested assets — it only secures their availability.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — A single CJTF tasking order can synchronize four services' contributions, whereas direct service-to-service negotiation requires four separate liaison relationships.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Assets delivered without synchronization (airlift without escort, intel without dissemination authority) lose most of their operational value.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Bypassing CJTF damages the commander's standing within the alliance, invites retaliation through future tasking cycles, and risks violating multinational command protocols.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable in any system with separable, interdependent resources under decentralized control — matrixed corporations, federated governments, multi-service military commands, legislative bodies with overlapping committee jurisdiction, multi-vendor supply chains, federated open-source projects, and inter-agency task forces. Less effective in unified hierarchical systems where a single commander can simply re-aggregate resources by order, and in transparent systems where the artificial nature of the fragmentation can be named and challenged.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A COO quietly preserves the split of customer data across CRM, billing, and product telemetry systems owned by three different VPs, ensuring any cross-functional analytics initiative must route through her office for data-sharing approvals.
- Government: A White House chief of staff preserves the distribution of a policy domain across four cabinet agencies' authorities so that any presidential directive on the matter requires his coordination to land.
- Military: A theater commander prevents the consolidation of cyber, electromagnetic, and information-operations capabilities under a single subordinate, keeping each capability under separate component commands so the theater HQ remains the only synthesis point.
- Academic: A dean preserves the historical separation of a research domain across three departments' faculty lines and lab space, ensuring that any interdisciplinary center proposal must clear her office for joint-appointment and budget approvals.
- Lobbying: A lobbyist working a regulatory issue ensures that drafting input is solicited separately from compliance teams, government affairs offices, and outside counsel, so that consolidating the industry position requires routing through his shop.
- NGO: A foundation program officer keeps a portfolio's grantees siloed into thematic clusters that cannot pool resources directly, requiring her convening role to enable any cross-cluster initiative the donors might want.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The tactic operates in the negative space of the choice architecture — the broker's power comes not from making their path attractive but from foreclosing every other path. Within the cluster of scarcity-engineering tactics, Resource Scattering is distinctive for being structural rather than episodic: it works through the persistent topology of who owns what, not through a discrete act of withholding. Compared to ARTIFICIAL SCARCITY TACTICS (which engineer shortage of a single resource), Resource Scattering engineers the dis-assembly of a bundle — the resources may be individually abundant, but their combination is rationed. Compared to OPERATIONAL BOTTLENECKING (which inserts a bottleneck into a process flow), Resource Scattering inserts the bottleneck into the org chart itself, making it harder to name and harder to remove.

### Strategic Implications

For the actor deploying the tactic, the asymmetry is favorable — the broker invests no positive effort to be approached, because the structure does the soliciting. The fingerprints are correspondingly faint: any individual instance of fragmentation can be defended on legitimate grounds (functional expertise, span-of-control limits, audit separation), and the political work is in resisting consolidation campaigns rather than initiating exclusion moves. For a counterparty trying to neutralize the tactic, the critical realization is that the visible problem (the broker's gatekeeping) is downstream of the invisible problem (the topology). Attacking the broker directly is usually a losing move; the durable counter is to surface the cost of fragmentation itself and to recruit a higher authority — a CEO, board chair, agency head — willing to sponsor structural re-integration. Without that, every initiative will keep arriving at the broker's door.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a particular organizational broker's position — mapping which resources they uniquely synthesize, which sponsors are currently routing through them, and where the structural reinforcement points are (reporting lines, budget authorities, system ownership). Use the generic lens when recognizing the pattern across domains or designing structural defenses against it — the prescriptions (integrator roles, consolidated ownership, transparency on coordination costs) generalize even when the specific resources do not. Both lenses converge on the same diagnosis: the tactic's signature is the absence of B(X)↑, and any analysis that detects positive promotion of the broker's path is probably mis-identifying a different tactic that merely co-occurs with scattering.
