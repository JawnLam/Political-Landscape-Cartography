# 0723 — THE 'REBRANDING' AS ERASURE

> Changing the names of departments, roles, or key processes not just for clarity, but to erase the historical identity and psychological ownership associated with the old regime.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C75 — Historical Revisionism, Founder-Myth, Artifacts & Institutional Amnesia

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Bourdieu

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic anchors in a concrete organizational maneuver (renaming departments, roles, and processes during a regime change) but its operative move is a broader symbolic-power play — using the authority to name as a tool to dissolve the prior regime's claim on the institution. The title says 'rebranding,' which is marketing vocabulary; the description says 'erasure,' which is regime-consolidation vocabulary. That gap is the ambiguity.

**Specific Interpretation.** A new leader (incoming CEO, post-merger integrator, turnaround executive) systematically renames the surviving organization's units, titles, and rituals so that day-to-day speech no longer references the predecessor's structure. The change is presented as clarification or modernization but functions to detach employees from the predecessor's identity capital.

**Generic Interpretation.** The tactic represents the political principle that whoever controls nomenclature controls the felt reality of the institution. Renaming is the cheapest form of symbolic conquest: it requires no material reorganization, but it forces every actor to either adopt the new lexicon (and re-anchor identity to the new regime) or audibly cling to the old terms (and mark themselves as a holdout).

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The organizational-change framing is the most common venue for this tactic, but the underlying mechanism — using the power to name as a means of dispossessing predecessors of their psychological grip on an institution — operates identically in political revolutions, religious schisms, post-acquisition integrations, and even family-business succession. The mechanism is reusable beyond the specific organizational-transformation context, so the generic reading is more fundamental.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Adopt the new names for departments, roles, and processes, and over time stop referencing the old labels
- **Option Y:** Continue using the legacy nomenclature, treating it as the 'real' name of the work and the unit
- **Context:** A post-transition organization (new CEO post-founder exit, post-merger integration, or post-takeover restructuring) where the incoming regime has launched a 'clarity' or 'modernization' rebrand of internal structures — new department names, new role titles, new process labels — while leaving most actual work and reporting lines substantially intact

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 1 (Subtle)

The new names typically come with a thin coat of forward-looking rationale (alignment, clarity, customer focus) that gives adopters a modest signaling benefit — appearing on-board with the new direction. But this is genuinely modest; the tactic does not depend on the new names being inherently better, and most employees recognize the rebrand as cosmetic.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Adopting the new vocabulary is itself a cognitive cost — relearning the map of the institution, re-anchoring mental models, and accepting unfamiliar terms for familiar work. The tactic does not lower this cost; it imposes it. The interesting thing about this tactic is that it works even though it raises the cost of compliance, because it raises the cost of non-compliance more.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. By removing the old names from official documents, org charts, email signatures, and meeting agendas, the tactic strips the legacy identity of its institutional carrier. Veterans who once derived status and belonging from 'Engineering Excellence' or 'The Founder's Studio' find those identities no longer exist in any official artifact. The historical regime's psychological ownership — built up over years of repetition — is hollowed out without a single confrontation.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Continuing to use the old names becomes socially costly. In meetings, the holdout has to translate or explain, marking themselves as someone who 'hasn't gotten the memo.' Worse, the holdout is increasingly read as politically attached to the old regime — nostalgic, resistant, not a team player. The cost of clinging to legacy nomenclature compounds with every meeting until silence is easier than translation.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic works not by selling the new identity (which is rarely compelling) but by systematically dispossessing the old one of its institutional footholds. Strip the legacy names from artifacts (decrease_benefit_y) and impose social cost on continuing to use them (increase_cost_y), and the predecessor regime's psychological grip dissolves without any direct attack on its substance. Within a year, new hires don't know the old names existed; within three, veterans struggle to remember them.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on three conditions: the new regime's control over official artifacts (org charts, HR systems, intranet, email templates) so that the new names become the only operational reality; sufficient turnover or distraction so that the daily friction of relearning is absorbed; and a plausible cover story (clarity, customer alignment, modernization) so that resistance can be framed as anti-progress rather than anti-erasure. The tactic fails when the old names are tied to external brand equity, when veterans are organized enough to maintain the legacy vocabulary as a counter-culture, or when the rebrand is so transparently cosmetic that even loyalists mock it.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Bourdieu's symbolic violence and the power of nomination — the imposition of categories of perception by those with the institutional authority to name. Reinforced by Goffman's frame analysis: changing the labels that people use to organize experience changes the experience itself. The act of authoritative naming is not description; it is constitutive.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic alters target behavior not by changing what people believe about the institution but by changing what words they have available to think about it. Once the old names are removed from official discourse, expressing attachment to the old regime requires more linguistic effort, more social risk, and more cognitive overhead than going along. The target's choice is shifted not at the level of preference but at the level of expressibility — the alternative becomes literally harder to articulate.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when the actor has unilateral control over official nomenclature (HR systems, org charts, internal communications, formal artifacts), when the institution has enough scale that informal counter-naming cannot keep up with official renaming, and when the targets' identification with the old names was implicit rather than explicit — felt, not theorized. Hardest in small tight-knit institutions where the old names live in spoken memory; easiest in large bureaucracies where official documents are the institution.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A revolutionary government that has just deposed the previous order renames every ministry ('Ministry of War' becomes 'Ministry of Peace'), every rank ('Marshal' becomes 'Commander of the People'), every public square, and the currency itself — not to functionally restructure governance, which remains largely intact, but to ensure that no daily transaction can occur without verbally acknowledging the new regime

**Sample X:** Adopt the new regime's nomenclature for ministries, ranks, and currency in all official documents and public speech

**Sample Y:** Continue using the prior regime's names in private and public usage

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Speaking the new nomenclature signals loyalty to the new order, providing modest reputational benefit in a high-surveillance environment

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active in this configuration — adopting the new lexicon is itself cognitive and psychic work, particularly for citizens with deep memory of the prior order

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Removes the old names from every official artifact (currency, signage, documents), stripping the prior regime of its identity infrastructure even where its functional structures persist

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Using the old names becomes a marker of disloyalty — in some regimes, prosecutable; in all regimes, socially costly. Speakers must either translate or be marked as nostalgic for the deposed order

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A large tech company has acquired a beloved smaller competitor whose products carried significant emotional weight with their team. The acquirer rebrands every product line, dissolves every team name, and replaces internal process labels — not because the new names are better, but because every survival of an old name represents an unresolved loyalty to the prior identity

**Sample X:** Use the acquirer's product names, team names, and process labels in all post-acquisition communications

**Sample Y:** Continue referring to the acquired company's products, teams, and processes by their pre-acquisition names

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The new names come with the acquirer's brand prestige and integration into the broader product ecosystem, offering acquired employees a path into the larger organization

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — for acquired employees, learning the new lexicon while their products and teammates are still recognizably the same is cognitively dissonant and effortful

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Removing the acquired company's names from internal artifacts dissolves the symbolic remainder of the standalone identity. The acquired team's pride in 'we built X' has no institutional referent once X has been renamed

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Using the old product or team names in meetings flags the speaker as not-yet-integrated, with cumulative reputational cost. Holdouts are gradually filtered out through promotion decisions and assignment patterns

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Works in any institution where the actor can control the official vocabulary — corporate, governmental, ecclesiastical, military, academic, family-business. The tactic generalizes because every institution depends on shared names to coordinate; whoever controls those names controls the felt continuity of the institution. Less effective in flat, oral-tradition cultures where naming authority is diffuse, or in institutions whose external brand equity is tied to legacy names (in which case the rebrand cannibalizes the institution itself).

### Core Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A turnaround CEO renames every business unit and product line in the first 90 days, citing 'customer clarity,' so that within a year no employee can describe the company's offerings without using the new CEO's vocabulary.
- Government: A new administration renames federal departments and agency programs (the prior administration's signature initiative is rebranded into invisibility) so that future policy debate is forced to use the current administration's frame.
- Religious: A reforming abbot renames the orders' prayer hours, devotional practices, and even the chapel itself, so that the prior abbot's spiritual program can no longer be referenced in liturgy without anachronism.
- Military: A new chief of staff renames units, operational doctrines, and even decorations, dissolving the symbolic continuity that career officers had built around the predecessor's command culture.
- Academic: An incoming dean renames departments and degree programs, so that within an admission cycle the prior dean's curricular vision is unrecognizable even to its original architects.
- Post-acquisition tech: An acquirer renames every product, team, and Slack channel from the acquired company, so that the small acquired culture has no official scaffold to organize around within six months.
- Family business: A successor heir renames the family enterprise and its key product lines, severing the rhetorical link to the founder generation's contributions and consolidating the heir's claim on the institution's identity.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both the specific organizational reading and the generic regime-consolidation reading converge on the same lever configuration (Y Degradation, strategy 6) — the tactic operates almost entirely on the alternative's value and cost, leaving the preferred outcome (compliance with the new vocabulary) largely unsold. This is rare and diagnostic: the tactic works not because the new identity is attractive but because the old identity is being symbolically dispossessed. Within cluster C75 (historical revisionism, founder-myth, institutional amnesia), this tactic is the symbolic-infrastructure variant: where AMNESIA ENGINEERING attacks memory directly and LEGACY DEMOLITION PLANNING attacks artifacts, REBRANDING AS ERASURE attacks the names themselves — the linguistic scaffolding on which all other memory and artifacts hang.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's power lies in how cheap it is relative to its effect. Renaming requires no firings, no reorganization, no public conflict — it can be sold as administrative housekeeping. Yet within two or three years the predecessor regime's psychological footprint has been dissolved, because the names by which veterans organized their loyalty no longer exist in any official artifact. This makes the tactic especially attractive to leaders who lack the political capital for direct confrontation: they can dispossess predecessors without ever attacking them. The cost is borne by the institution's memory infrastructure: legitimate continuity claims are erased alongside illegitimate ones, and the institution's ability to learn from its own history is degraded as the vocabulary needed to discuss that history disappears.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a concrete transition (new CEO, post-merger integration, succession event) where the rebrand is the visible artifact of a less-visible regime change — the renaming pattern is often the earliest detectable signal that the new regime intends to dispossess rather than coexist with the prior identity. Use the generic lens when analyzing the deeper political principle: that the authority to name is itself a power resource, separable from operational control, and that controlling the institutional vocabulary is often a precondition for controlling everything else. Practitioners on the defensive side should recognize that defending the old names is rarely a winning fight; instead, the counter-move is to ensure the new names carry forward the substantive commitments of the prior regime, so that the rebrand becomes nominal cover for continuity rather than the vehicle for erasure.
