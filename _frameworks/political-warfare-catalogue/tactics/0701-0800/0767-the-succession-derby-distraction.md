# 0767 — THE 'SUCCESSION DERBY' DISTRACTION

> Intentionally creating ambiguity about the succession plan for a key leadership role to incite competition and infighting among potential candidates, diverting their attention.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C54 — Succession Engineering, Heir-Apparent Manipulation & Power-Vacuum Bait

**Cited principles:** Kahneman, Lazear & Rosen, Lazear, Rosen

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title anchors in a particular organizational scene — a succession contest for a key leadership role — but the underlying move is a generic attention-displacement play in which a manufactured prize is dangled to redirect rivals' energy away from the actor's actual vulnerabilities. The 'succession' detail is the costume; the mechanism is rivalrous distraction.

**Specific Interpretation.** Within a defined organization, the incumbent (or their proxy) deliberately leaves succession ambiguous — multiple plausible heirs, no announced timeline, deniable signals — so that potential successors burn their political capital fighting each other rather than scrutinizing the incumbent's record, building cross-faction coalitions, or pursuing reform.

**Generic Interpretation.** An actor with power over a scarce, ambiguously-defined prize keeps the contest open and the rules unstated, weaponizing the rivals' own ambition to consume their attention, deplete their alliances, and prevent any one of them from organizing against the prize-giver.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The succession context is vivid but the operating mechanism — keep a prize ambiguous to set rivals on each other — recurs across royal courts, papal conclaves, gangland successions, family business handoffs, partnership tracks, and political party leadership races. The mechanism survives any change of context; the context is the example, not the heart.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Compete openly against fellow candidates for the unspecified succession slot — court power brokers, undermine rivals, signal loyalty, hoard visible wins
- **Option Y:** Set the derby aside and instead build cross-candidate coalitions, scrutinize the incumbent, or pursue substantive reform work that doesn't directly serve the candidacy
- **Context:** A CEO or division head approaching retirement age leaves the succession process deliberately undefined — multiple internal SVPs and EVPs are quietly told they're 'in the mix,' no formal process, no timeline, no announced criteria. The incumbent thereby keeps the C-suite focused on each other instead of on the incumbent's strategic decisions, board relationships, or post-tenure plans.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The dangled prize is enormous — the top job — and the ambiguity itself inflates each candidate's perceived odds. Without a named front-runner, every contender can talk themselves into believing they're closest, so the subjective expected value of fighting for the slot soars beyond what any honest probability assessment would justify.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not make competing cheaper — in fact the cost of campaigning (favor-trading, sleep, reputation risk) is high. The point isn't to ease the candidates' path; it's to make them want to pay the price. The lever's absence is diagnostic: this is a tactic of attraction-by-prize, not friction-removal.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Activities not tied to the candidacy — coalition work with peers, substantive reform that doesn't visibly accrue to one candidate, even normal operating work — get reframed in the candidate's own mind as 'not advancing my candidacy,' draining their perceived value relative to the contest. The benefit of those activities doesn't actually drop, but their psychological weight does.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

While a candidate is doing anything else, the others are visibly campaigning. Stepping back from the derby — even briefly — feels like ceding ground to a rival. The cost of not-competing is measured in real-time as rival visibility gains, making opt-out psychologically catastrophic even when strategically wise.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic works by making the contest so vivid and the alternative so unbearable that rivals self-select into mutually-consuming competition. The incumbent doesn't have to lower the cost of competing — candidates pay it gladly — they only have to inflate the prize and make withdrawal feel like surrender. The configuration is heavy on prize-glamour and opt-out punishment, light on facilitation.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) genuinely scarce prize where only one can win, (b) plausible plurality of candidates so no front-runner is obvious, (c) the incumbent's deniable control over the timing and criteria, and (d) a candidate pool culturally inclined toward direct rivalry rather than alliance. Fails when candidates wise up and form a cross-candidate pact, when the board imposes a structured process, or when one candidate is so far ahead that the others give up and start watching the incumbent instead.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Girard's mimetic rivalry and the tournament-incentive structure (Lazear & Rosen). When rivals fixate on each other, they begin to want the prize because the others want it, not for the prize's intrinsic value. The actor creating the tournament harvests their displaced attention. Adjacent mechanism: the cognitive narrowing documented by Easterbrook (1959) and developed by Kahneman as attentional capture — high-stakes competition consumes working memory, leaving little for peripheral threats including the tournament-master.

**Decision Alteration.** Rivals shift from analyzing the broader strategic environment (including the actor) to analyzing each other. The decision space collapses from 'what should I do to advance the organization / my career / my values' to 'how do I beat the others to the prize.' Once inside that narrowed frame, behaviors that don't serve the contest feel irrational even when they are objectively the highest-value moves.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when the prize is genuinely scarce and zero-sum, the rules are ambiguous enough that perceived odds can be subjectively inflated, the rivals are roughly matched (no obvious front-runner), and the contest's framing is supplied by someone with enough authority to make it seem real. Fails when rivals can collectively renegotiate the contest, when one party defects to a meta-strategy, or when the prize is exposed as either certain to go to one candidate or never actually on offer.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An aging absolute monarch refuses to name an heir despite multiple legitimate candidates (sons, nephews, a strong consort). Court factions form around each plausible successor and consume themselves in intrigue. The monarch — who can no longer rely on physical strength or fresh political capital — survives in office because no faction can spare the attention to organize a coup or force abdication.

**Sample X:** Princes and senior nobles spend their political energy maneuvering against each other for the succession

**Sample Y:** Princes and nobles form a cross-faction alliance to constrain the aging monarch's prerogative or accelerate the transition on their terms

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The throne's value is fully on display while each candidate's perceived probability of winning is inflated by the absence of any official ranking

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Court intrigue is expensive — assassinations, bribes, marriages of state — but candidates pay willingly; the tactic does not lower their cost

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — A cross-faction pact to constrain the monarch yields collective gain but no individual claim to the throne, so it feels like trading a possible crown for a share of a smaller pie

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any noble who steps back from the succession contest watches rivals consolidate court positions in real time, raising the cost of non-participation to existential loss of rank

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A managing partner at a mid-sized law firm signals that two named-partner slots will open 'within the next few years' and that four senior associates are 'all being watched.' No criteria are published, no timeline confirmed. The four spend three years competing against each other for visibility while the managing partner runs the firm without governance challenge and quietly arranges his own equity exit.

**Sample X:** Junior partners compete for the named-partner slot through billable-hour stacking, client-poaching, and committee politics

**Sample Y:** Junior partners coordinate to renegotiate the firm's compensation structure or governance model with the managing partner

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Named-partner equity is life-changing money and the ambiguity of the process means each associate can plausibly tell themselves they're in the top two

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Eighty-hour weeks and broken marriages are the price of contention; the tactic doesn't lighten that load

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Cross-associate coordination on governance reform produces gains shared with three other people, while winning the named-partner slot is a private fortune — the alternative looks dilute by comparison

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any associate visibly working on collective governance reform looks 'not focused on the practice' and falls behind the others in the unstated promotion criteria

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Works in any structure with (a) a meaningful scarce prize concentrated in one or few slots, (b) a gatekeeper with discretionary control over selection, and (c) multiple plausible aspirants. That covers monarchies, papal conclaves, corporate succession, partnership-track professional services firms, political party leadership races, dynastic family businesses, gang and cartel successions, academic chair successions, sports team captaincy, and even reality-television formats. Less effective in genuinely rule-bound succession systems (formal primogeniture, term-limited democratic elections with published rules) where the ambiguity lever is unavailable.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: a long-tenured CEO keeps three division presidents in 'horse race' positioning for years while quietly negotiating a generous exit package the board would scrutinize harder if any single successor had organized internal support
- Religious: a cardinal known to be papabile is quietly named to multiple high-visibility roles alongside three other plausible candidates, ensuring curial factions consume each other in pre-conclave maneuvering rather than confronting the pope's policy choices
- Political: a long-serving party leader privately encourages multiple potential successors to test the waters, knowing that as long as the leadership race is multi-candidate and unscheduled, no single challenger can build the coalition needed to force them out
- Family business: an aging patriarch lets all four children believe they're the favorite to inherit the company, keeping them in competition with each other rather than coordinating to force a structured generational transition
- Organized crime: a boss in declining health refuses to name a capo as heir, letting underbosses kill each other and prove themselves while removing the most ambitious threats to his own seat
- Academic: a department chair retiring in 'two or three years' lets four senior faculty position themselves as the successor, ensuring no coordinated push for departmental reform — including the reform of the chair's own legacy programs
- Diplomatic: a regional hegemon keeps several client-state factions in succession contention by hinting that each could become the favored partner, preventing any of them from organizing regional resistance to the hegemon's influence

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific (corporate succession ambiguity) and generic (mimetic rivalry harvested for attention displacement) readings converge on Strategy 13 — both inflate the prize, devalue the alternative of coalition, and raise the cost of withdrawing from the contest, without making the contest itself cheaper. What distinguishes this tactic within Cluster C54 (Succession Engineering) is its purpose: where THE HEIR APPARENT TOURNAMENT (0039) uses competition to identify the best successor, and SUCCESSION POISONING (0190) discredits a specific named heir, THE SUCCESSION DERBY DISTRACTION is not actually about choosing a successor at all — the succession is a pretext, the distraction is the product. Sibling tactics see candidates as inputs to a selection process; this one sees them as raw material whose attention can be combusted to keep the incumbent's flanks unmolested.

### Strategic Implications

Practitioners reading this tactic — whether deploying or defending against it — should attend to the asymmetry between the candidates' frame and the incumbent's frame. Inside the derby, candidates experience the contest as the most important event in their professional lives; from the incumbent's seat, the contest IS the cover. The defensive move is to step outside the frame and ask 'who benefits from us being in this contest right now, and what are we not watching while we run?' The offensive move, once recognized, is to coordinate with at least one rival on a meta-question — not 'which of us wins' but 'why is the gatekeeper letting this drag.' Even a partial cross-candidate coalition collapses the tactic, because the incumbent's protection depends on candidates atomizing against each other.

### Resolution Approach

Apply the specific lens when diagnosing concrete situations — an actual succession in an actual organization, where the question is whether the ambiguity is incompetence, indecision, or deliberate distraction. Apply the generic lens when teaching the pattern or scanning for it in unfamiliar domains — the family business, the diocese, the cartel — where the surface looks different but the mechanism is identical. The deepest insight is that competition among rivals is itself a resource for whoever controls the prize: it doesn't have to be resolved to pay dividends, and in fact resolution ends the dividend. A succession contest that never quite concludes is more valuable to the incumbent than one that does.

## Succession Derby Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Ambiguity-as-prize: the gatekeeper monetizes uncertainty itself, harvesting rivals' attention as the dividend

**Escalation Pattern.** Begins with deniable signals to multiple candidates → candidates form retinues and burn favors → cross-candidate animosity hardens → coalition with rivals becomes culturally impossible → incumbent operates unwatched → exit occurs on incumbent's terms with succession 'unresolved' until the last moment

**Counter Measures.** Cross-candidate meta-coordination (even partial); demand for published criteria and timeline; appeal to a higher governance body (board, conclave-rules, party constitution); deliberate withdrawal by one candidate to reveal the structure to others

**Exit Criteria.** Tactic ends naturally when the incumbent achieves their actual goal (clean exit, expired threat, completed move) and either announces a successor or lets the derby resolve itself — by which point the candidates have already paid the full cost of distraction
