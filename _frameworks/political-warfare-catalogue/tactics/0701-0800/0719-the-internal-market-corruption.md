# 0719 — THE 'INTERNAL MARKET' CORRUPTION

> Creating a system where internal services (like IT or HR) must be 'purchased' by departments, but manipulating the internal pricing to penalize rivals and subsidize allies.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C15 — Scarcity Engineering & Resource Access Politics

**Cited principles:** Akerlof, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a concrete organizational artifact (the internal chargeback/transfer-pricing system for shared services) but the operative move — selectively pricing an ostensibly neutral allocation mechanism to advantage allies and tax rivals — generalizes to any administered-market or rate-setting system. The corruption sits not in the market's existence but in who controls the price book.

**Specific Interpretation.** A finance, IT, HR, or shared-services leader sets transfer prices, service tier definitions, and chargeback rules so that departments aligned with their agenda enjoy subsidized rates or favorable cost categorization, while rival departments are billed at penal rates, charged for previously bundled items, or pushed into higher-cost service tiers.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any actor controlling the rules of an internal pseudo-market — pricing, eligibility, tier definitions, billing categorization — converts that rule-making authority into a redistributive instrument that looks like neutral economics while functioning as discretionary patronage and punishment.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the chargeback context provides vivid mechanics, the deep move — laundering political favoritism through the apparent objectivity of price signals — is reusable wherever administered markets exist (transfer pricing, allocation formulas, regulatory rate-setting, grant pools, capitation rates). The market apparatus is the costume; the price-setting authority is the weapon. This makes the generic pattern more fundamental than any single chargeback implementation.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Allied departments accept and rely on the internal market, treating its prices as legitimate cost signals (and quietly enjoying their subsidized rates)
- **Option Y:** Rival departments either absorb the inflated charges, escalate the pricing scheme to leadership, or attempt to bypass the internal provider for external alternatives
- **Context:** A central shared-services function (IT, HR, Finance, Facilities, Legal) operates as an internal vendor under a chargeback or transfer-pricing regime, with its leader controlling the rate card, the service tier definitions, and the classification rules that determine which department gets billed for what

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Allied departments receive disproportionate value — premium service tiers at standard rates, generous bundling, fast-tracked tickets, custom builds folded into baseline allocation — making the internal market a net positive for them and reinforcing their dependence on the patron

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The core operating mechanism: allies pay below true cost through favorable rate categorization, generous service-level inclusion, depreciation re-allocation, and quiet write-offs of overruns to a central pool. Subsidized pricing is the patronage currency

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not attack the perceived value of the services rivals are buying — those services remain the same products. The play is on price, not on the utility of what is purchased. Attacking the benefit would expose the manipulation since rivals can compare deliverables directly with allies

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Rivals are billed at penal rates through tier-bumping, declassifying previously-bundled items into à la carte line items, surcharges for 'complexity' or 'priority,' aggressive depreciation on shared assets, and chargebacks for support tickets that allies have absorbed into baseline. Their unit economics deteriorate while their P&L looks like operational failure

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The whole tactic operates on the cost dimension across both choices: it cheapens internal service consumption for allies and inflates it for rivals, while the underlying services themselves remain nominally identical. This is asymmetric cost-loading wearing the costume of neutral pricing — the patron does not need to disparage rivals or sell allies on value, only to move numbers in the rate sheet.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires three structural conditions: (1) the patron controls the rate-setting methodology with low scrutiny — chargeback rules are notoriously opaque even to CFOs; (2) departments lack a credible external benchmark or exit option (regulated mandates, security policies, and integration overhead usually preclude going outside); (3) the corporate culture treats internal pricing as a technical matter handled by finance, not a political matter handled by the executive committee. The tactic decays when an audit committee or new CFO commissions a pricing-fairness review, when a rival successfully escalates with a comparative cost analysis, or when business units gain authority to buy services externally.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Akerlof's Information Asymmetry combined with Pfeffer's analysis of organizational power through control of resource allocation rules. The methodology of internal pricing is opaque to the consumers of the service, while the price-setter understands exactly which knobs move which P&Ls. Pfeffer (Managing With Power) treats control over allocation criteria — not just allocation outcomes — as a higher-order form of power because it sets the rules within which everyone else optimizes.

**Decision Alteration.** Targets do not perceive themselves as making a political choice at all — they see invoices, rate cards, and tier definitions that look like cost data. The political content is encoded into the technical artifacts. Allies experience the world as 'shared services are working' and rivals experience it as 'our cost structure is uncompetitive,' with neither group recognizing the upstream patronage logic. Behavior aligns with the price signal rather than with the underlying merit of consumption decisions.

**Optimal Conditions.** Administered markets where prices are set by an internal authority rather than discovered through competition; significant information asymmetry between rule-setter and rule-takers; weak external benchmarks; cultural framing of the pricing system as technical/objective; absence of an audit function with mandate over allocation methodology; multi-layered cost accounting that makes cross-subsidy hard to trace.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A multinational with a powerful corporate treasury function uses internal cost-of-capital transfer pricing to determine which business units get green-lit for investment. The CFO and treasurer favor legacy business units they came up through and view the digital unit's growth as a career threat

**Sample X:** A regional sales business unit accepts the corporate finance team's quarterly cost-of-capital allocation that charges them a below-market internal rate

**Sample Y:** A new digital business unit, viewed as a threat to the legacy P&L, accepts an inflated risk-adjusted cost-of-capital allocation that crushes its hurdle-rate economics

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The legacy unit's projects are not made more valuable by the favorable rate — their underlying economics are unchanged. The tactic operates purely on the cost denominator

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Below-market internal capital charges make legacy investments look like easy wins against the hurdle rate, freeing investment dollars for projects that would not clear a fair-rate test

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The digital unit's actual revenue potential and market opportunity are not disparaged — that would be too visible. The numbers are quietly rigged on the input side

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Inflated cost-of-capital charges make digital projects fail the hurdle-rate test even when they would create more value than approved legacy projects, starving the threat unit of capital while preserving plausible 'we follow the discipline' deniability

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** An academic medical center uses an internal capitation and allocation methodology to assign overhead, facilities cost, and ancillary service charges to clinical departments. The CFO works closely with the CMO; the methodology is reviewed annually but the inputs are crafted by the finance team

**Sample X:** Hospital departments aligned with the CMO receive favorable capitation rates and bundled-service allocations under the internal cost-allocation methodology

**Sample Y:** Departments led by physicians who clash with administration are billed for facilities, support staff, and pharmacy at unbundled premium rates

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Friendly departments do not gain clinical capability from the methodology — only margin headroom. The benefit dimension of their actual service is unchanged

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Generous allocation rules (low overhead absorption, bundled support staff, favorable depreciation of shared equipment) make friendly departments appear highly margin-positive on their internal P&L, building the case for their leaders' expansion requests

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The disfavored departments' clinical reputation is not attacked; that would be career-ending for the CMO. The mechanism is hidden inside accounting choices

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Unbundled facilities charges, premium-rate ancillary services, and aggressive overhead absorption make disfavored departments appear chronically unprofitable on internal reporting, providing the rationale for resource cuts, leadership replacement, or service-line consolidation under their rivals

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies wherever an internal authority sets prices for services that users cannot easily purchase externally — corporate shared services (IT, HR, Finance, Legal, Facilities), transfer pricing across business units, cost-of-capital allocation, capitation and overhead allocation in healthcare and universities, government interagency working capital funds, military Defense Working Capital Funds, regulated utility transfer pricing across affiliated businesses. Less effective where consumers have real exit options or where allocation methodologies are reviewed by independent auditors with subject-matter expertise.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: An IT chargeback regime where allied business units pay a flat 'standard service' rate that includes everything they actually consume, while rival units are billed à la carte for every ticket, custom integration, and capacity peak
- Government: A federal agency's working-capital fund where the administrator quietly sets favorable rates for bureaus aligned with the political appointee's priorities and penal rates for bureaus pursuing inconvenient regulatory agendas
- Academic: A university's indirect-cost recovery methodology that allocates lower overhead rates to departments the dean favors and higher rates to departments the dean wants to consolidate or shrink
- Healthcare: A hospital's internal pharmacy or facilities chargeback that subsidizes physician groups aligned with administration and over-charges groups whose leaders resist administrative consolidation
- Military: A Defense Working Capital Fund where the program executive office sets logistics service rates favorable to programs run by trusted commanders and penal to programs run by rivals, masked as actuarial cost recovery
- Conglomerate: A holding-company transfer-pricing system where the patriarch-CEO subsidizes subsidiaries run by family members through favorable intercompany pricing while squeezing margins from subsidiaries run by professional managers being maneuvered out

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

This tactic distinguishes itself from sibling cluster members by exploiting the specific camouflage of pseudo-market machinery. ARTIFICIAL SCARCITY TACTICS restrict supply; OPERATIONAL BOTTLENECKING throttles throughput; the QUEUE JUMP grants priority access; TRANSFER PRICING POLITICS adjusts cross-unit prices. INTERNAL MARKET CORRUPTION is uniquely the move where an actor weaponizes the rate-setting authority of an entire administered internal market — turning a system that was built to instill cost discipline and price signals into a redistributive patronage instrument. The deep pattern across all these cluster siblings is that resource-allocation machinery, once erected, becomes itself a political asset; this tactic is the most insidious variant because the manipulation hides behind the legitimacy of price.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's power comes from its dual disguise: prices look like economics, and economics look like discipline. Targets who absorb inflated rates often internalize them as evidence of their own operational underperformance — the rival's P&L genuinely looks worse, and they often cannot articulate why without sounding like complainers attacking the finance function. Practitioners deploying it should understand that the tactic generates real performance distortions (allies underinvest in efficiency because their costs are hidden; rivals over-optimize for the wrong cost drivers because they are responding to corrupted signals), which eventually produce visible operational dysfunction the auditor will find. Defenders should know that the countermove is not to attack the prices but to demand methodology transparency — making the algorithm public is fatal to discretionary pricing because it forces the patron to defend each rule on its merits.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a particular shared-services chargeback, transfer-pricing scheme, or capitation methodology — when the question is 'is our IT chargeback being politically gamed' or 'why does the digital unit always fail the hurdle rate.' Use the generic lens when teaching the deeper move (administered markets are political artifacts) or scanning the organization for analogous corruptions in allocation formulas, grant pools, internal-bank rates, or any system where someone sets a price that everyone else must pay. The specific lens detects an instance; the generic lens predicts where the next instance will appear.
