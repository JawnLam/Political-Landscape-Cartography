# 0267 — RISK DISPLACEMENT

> Assigning the most precarious or failure-prone aspects of the transformation to resistant departments or individuals, setting them up to fail and thereby neutralizing their credibility.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C26 — Scapegoating, Blame Architecture, Expendable Allies & Ghost Sponsorship

**Cited principles:** Akerlof, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in 'the transformation' as a specific organizational context, but the operative move — selectively routing the worst work to one's opponents so they discredit themselves through visible failure — is a fully reusable pattern of structural sabotage that appears across competitive and bureaucratic settings.

**Specific Interpretation.** A change sponsor (or transformation lead) hands the resistant departments the riskiest, most failure-prone slices of the transformation program — under the cover of 'shared accountability' or 'meaningful involvement' — so the resistors' inevitable failures discredit them as obstructionist or incompetent, removing them as credible voices against the transformation.

**Generic Interpretation.** An actor with portfolio-allocation authority routes the highest-variance, lowest-probability-of-success work to rivals, dressing the assignment as opportunity, recognition, or stretch responsibility. When the work fails — as it was structurally designed to — the rivals own the failure, and their credibility on the broader issue collapses with it.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The transformation context is a vivid use case, but the mechanism — using one's control of work allocation to engineer the failure that discredits an opponent — is the durable, transferable insight. Once the practitioner sees the pattern, they recognize it in legislative committee assignments, hostile audit scoping, military 'forlorn hope' deployments, and partner work-pool politics. The context is a costume; the routing logic is the body.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Decision-makers and the wider organization dismiss the resistant departments' continued criticism of the transformation
- **Option Y:** Decision-makers continue to treat the resistant departments as credible, reasoned voices on the transformation's risks
- **Context:** A transformation program where certain departments openly oppose the changes but cannot be fired or formally sidelined — they retain operational mandate and political standing — so the sponsor needs them discredited through their own visible record rather than by direct attack

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does nothing to make the transformation itself look better on the merits. Dismissing the resistors becomes rational not because the transformation is now more attractive, but because the resistors look incompetent. The lever for promoting X is untouched — that is a separate persuasion job and not what this tactic does.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Supporting or ignoring the transformation is not made operationally easier or cheaper by this tactic. If anything, the assigned failures create real operational damage that someone has to clean up. The tactic does not facilitate X — it strips Y of standing.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. By routing failure-prone work to the resistors, the sponsor manufactures a visible track record of incompetence that decision-makers will weigh against any future objection the resistors raise. Their analytical credibility — the only real benefit they brought to the debate — is destroyed. Granovetter's threshold model also applies: once a critical mass of decision-makers stops crediting the resistors, the social cascade against them runs on its own.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Aligning with the discredited resistors becomes politically expensive. Anyone publicly defending them gets tarred with the same failure. The resistors themselves are now consumed firefighting the bad work they were handed — meaning their cost of mounting any further organized resistance has gone up dramatically because their political bandwidth is depleted. Williamson's asset-specificity logic applies: the resistors are forced to make sunk investments in the failing work, which raises their exit cost from owning it.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The sponsor never has to argue against the resistors on the merits of the transformation — a debate the resistors might actually win if they have operational knowledge the sponsor lacks. Instead, the sponsor uses portfolio routing to ensure the resistors generate their own discrediting record. By the time the resistors object to the transformation again, decision-makers privately discount them as 'the team that couldn't even deliver their own piece,' and supporting them becomes politically toxic. The choice tilts toward dismissing Y not because X became more appealing but because Y's voice has been structurally devalued.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires that the sponsor genuinely controls work allocation and has plausible cover for why the failure-prone work was routed to the resistors ('they have the domain expertise,' 'they asked for ownership,' 'we wanted to be inclusive'). Fails when the resistors have allies sophisticated enough to surface the routing pattern itself, when the assigned work is so visibly impossible that observers see the trap, or when the resistors successfully reframe the failure as a sponsor-design problem rather than an execution failure. Most effective in environments where outcome accountability is read as a simple win/lose ledger rather than examined for structural priors.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross) combined with Source Credibility Bias and Akerlof's information-asymmetry logic. Observers reliably attribute failure to the failing actor's competence rather than to the assignment structure they were handed, especially when the routing decision is buried upstream and the failure is highly visible downstream.

**Decision Alteration.** Audiences who would otherwise weigh the rival's substantive arguments instead come to dismiss them preemptively. The mechanism does not refute the rival's position — it destroys the rival as a credible source by ensuring their nearest visible track record is one of failure. Once the source is discredited, all future arguments from that source decay in weight regardless of merit. This is ad hominem by portfolio engineering rather than by direct attack.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where (a) the actor controls allocation of risk, work, or responsibility; (b) outcomes are visibly measured but underlying assignment risk is not; (c) observers lack the contextual literacy to distinguish 'failed because incompetent' from 'failed because given an impossible task'; (d) failure is attributable to a single owner rather than diffused across a team; and (e) the targets have enough professional pride to attempt the assignment rather than refuse it on principle.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A US Senate majority leader assigns the leadership of a doomed, high-visibility procedural fight (one with no possible win condition under current chamber math) to a senator from a rival faction within the same caucus, framing it as recognition of the senator's seniority and skill.

**Sample X:** The Senate majority leader successfully marginalizes the rival faction's signature policy demands in the next session

**Sample Y:** The rival faction continues to extract concessions by leveraging their reputation for legislative competence

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Does not improve the substance of the majority leader's preferred agenda

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Does not lower the operational cost of advancing the leader's agenda — it only removes a critic

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The rival senator's reputation for legislative skill — their core political asset — is gutted by a publicly lost fight, so their future objections carry less weight in caucus deliberation

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Other senators who would have aligned with the rival faction now hesitate to do so publicly, since association with the 'failed' fight carries reputational drag during the next primary cycle

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A senior partner at a law firm — threatened by a rising associate's intellectual reputation and growing client following — assigns the associate as lead on a pro bono case the firm has already privately concluded is unwinnable, framed as a career-defining opportunity for stretch responsibility and trial experience.

**Sample X:** Senior partners conclude the rising associate is 'not partnership material' and pass them over

**Sample Y:** The associate's track record continues to justify their advancement on the partnership track

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The senior partner's own standing is not improved by the routing — the move is purely defensive

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Passing over the associate is not made operationally cheaper; the lever simply removes a competing candidate

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The associate's win-loss record — the metric the partnership committee actually reads — now carries a high-visibility loss that wipes out the strategic value of their prior wins

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Other partners who were quietly sponsoring the associate now distance themselves to avoid being associated with a publicly visible loss right before the vote

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any hierarchical setting where an actor controls work allocation and outcomes are publicly attributed to individual owners — corporate transformations, legislative bodies, military command, professional partnerships, academic department politics, intelligence-community task assignment, and family businesses dividing operating roles. Less effective in flat, peer-controlled environments (cooperatives, partnerships with full work-distribution transparency) where the routing pattern is visible to all participants in real time, and ineffective where targets refuse high-risk assignments as a matter of cultural norm rather than treating them as career opportunity.

### Core Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CFO assigns the integration of an acquired company's legacy ERP — known internally to be irreparable — to the SVP who publicly opposed the acquisition, ensuring the SVP owns the inevitable cost overruns
- Military: A theater commander assigns a politically inconvenient subordinate to a high-casualty 'forlorn hope' position, dressing it as a vote of confidence in the officer's tactical ability
- Government: A White House Chief of Staff hands the politically toxic cabinet portfolio (immigration enforcement, post-disaster recovery) to a secretary who has been independently building a national profile, ensuring that profile gets tarnished
- Academic: A department chair assigns the unteachable required course — historically with the worst student evaluations on campus — to a tenure-track rival, framing it as recognition of their pedagogical reputation
- Family business: A founder grooms two siblings for succession by assigning the failing legacy division to the sibling whose vision differs from the founder's, guaranteeing the founder's preferred heir takes the growing divisions
- Consulting: A managing partner staffs a known-doomed client engagement (legacy client, unworkable scope, hostile counterparty) entirely with junior partners they want pushed out, then uses the engagement's losses in the up-or-out review

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both analyses converge on Strategy 6 (Y Degradation) — the tactic operates by destroying the rival's credibility rather than by directly promoting the actor's preferred outcome. The specific lens shows the transformation-context version where the resistors are entire departments and the failure-prone work is a transformation workstream; the generic lens shows the universal portfolio-routing logic where any actor with allocation authority can engineer the same outcome. They tell the same structural story in different vocabularies.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: Use the specific reading when diagnosing why a transformation seems to be eating its critics — when departments that raised legitimate concerns find themselves owning the program's worst slices. The specific lens helps a practitioner spot the move in real time and either refuse the assignment, surface the routing logic to stakeholders, or insist on shared accountability terms that don't let the routing decision disappear upstream. When to use the generic lens: Use the generic reading when teaching pattern recognition across domains, or when analyzing power dynamics in settings far removed from transformation programs — committee chairmanships, military deployments, partnership track decisions. The generic lens names the underlying portfolio-engineering mechanism that practitioners encounter dozens of times in a career under dozens of cover stories.

### Resolution Approach

The decisive political move is not made when the failure happens — it is made when the assignment is handed out. Practitioners watching for credibility attacks usually scan downstream (the bad performance review, the public criticism, the rumor campaign), but Risk Displacement makes the attack upstream and invisible, hidden inside what looks like an opportunity or a fair distribution of work. The countermove is to read every high-visibility, high-variance assignment as a political document, not just an operational one — and to ask, before accepting, who else was offered this and why it came to you.

## Risk Displacement Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Sponsor controls allocation → routes high-variance, low-probability-of-success work to rivals under cover story of opportunity/expertise/inclusion → rivals attempt the work → visible failure attributed to rivals' competence → rivals' credibility on the broader issue collapses → sponsor's preferred course advances unopposed.

**Escalation Pattern.** First-stage: a single hard assignment. Second-stage: stacked assignments so the rival is firefighting and cannot maintain political activity. Third-stage: the rival's allies are also drawn in, broadening the credibility damage across the resistor coalition.

**Counter Measures.** Refuse the assignment with public reasoning ('this would require resources and authority I do not have, and I will not own a failure designed by upstream constraints'). Demand shared accountability with the sponsor in writing. Surface the routing decision itself as a discussion topic with senior stakeholders. Force pre-mortem analysis that documents the assignment's risk profile before work begins, creating a paper trail that survives the failure.

**Exit Criteria.** Tactic stops working when the sponsor's routing pattern becomes visible as a pattern across multiple assignments (one bad assignment looks like bad luck; three bad assignments to the same opposition cluster looks like design), when an audit function independently reviews assignment risk, or when the failure cost crosses the threshold where the sponsor is held responsible for having routed the work badly.
