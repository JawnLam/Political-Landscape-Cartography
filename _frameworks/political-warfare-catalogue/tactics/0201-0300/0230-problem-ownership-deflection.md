# 0230 — PROBLEM OWNERSHIP DEFLECTION

> Restructuring tasks so that the negative consequences of an ingrained inefficiency fall upon a different, politically powerful department, thereby manufacturing an internal demand for the change you already wanted to implement.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C26 — Scapegoating, Blame Architecture, Expendable Allies & Ghost Sponsorship

**Cited principles:** Coase, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a specific organizational maneuver (restructuring tasks so a powerful department absorbs the pain of an inefficiency), but the underlying mechanism — manufacturing internal demand for a change by relocating the externality onto a party with standing to fight — is a reusable pattern applicable far beyond inter-departmental politics.

**Specific Interpretation.** A middle-power actor wants to fix an ingrained inefficiency they lack authority to change directly. They re-wire workflows so the inefficiency's downstream costs (delays, error rework, blame, customer escalations) now land on a politically dominant department, who then 'discovers' the problem and champions the change.

**Generic Interpretation.** A pattern of pain-relocation: any actor who cannot win a fight on the merits engineers the situation so the cost of inaction falls onto a stakeholder powerful enough to force the change, converting an unwinnable advocacy battle into an inevitability the powerful party demands.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The departmental-restructuring framing is one instantiation, but the move — re-routing externalities to manufacture a sponsor — applies in legislative coalition-building, family business succession, nonprofit board politics, and even military procurement. The mechanism is more fundamental than the corporate-department context that frames it.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Senior leadership endorses and resources the workflow / process change the deflecting actor has wanted all along
- **Option Y:** Senior leadership continues to deprioritize the change, treating it as the deflecting actor's parochial complaint
- **Context:** A mid-level operations or finance manager has been unable to get traction on fixing an ingrained inefficiency (e.g., a manual reconciliation, a redundant approval step, an obsolete handoff) because the affected stakeholders are too junior to command attention from the executive layer.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Restructuring the task so the pain now hits, say, the Sales VP's quarterly numbers or the General Counsel's risk register transforms the change from 'a process improvement nobody asked for' into 'the thing that solves a problem a powerful department is loudly demanding.' The change accrues sponsorship benefit it could never have earned on its merits alone.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The actual implementation cost (engineering work, change management, training) is unchanged. The tactic does not reduce the change's effort cost — it changes WHO has to pay the cost of NOT changing. This asymmetry is part of the tactic's signature.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Once the powerful department starts feeling the pain, the status quo loses its 'invisible default' status. What used to be 'how we've always done it' (a benefit of inertia: no debate cost, no political risk) is now visibly broken from the perspective of someone who matters. The benefit of inaction — being left alone — evaporates.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the central operating lever. The entire tactic is engineered to make the status quo unbearable for a specific powerful party who can now force the change. The cost of continuing the inefficiency is moved from the deflector's department (where leadership ignored it) to a department whose pain leadership cannot ignore.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The change does not get easier to implement; the change gets a powerful patron. By engineering an externality that lands on a politically dominant department, the deflector converts a low-priority improvement into a high-priority demand from above. The status quo's costs are unchanged in aggregate — they are simply re-allocated to a balance sheet that leadership monitors.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) genuine ability to restructure workflows without triggering counter-restructuring by the targeted department, (b) plausible deniability — the rewiring must look like a reasonable operational decision, not a deliberate provocation, and (c) the targeted department must actually have the political capital to drive the change once they feel the pain. Fails when the powerful department blames the deflector for the new pain rather than the underlying inefficiency, or when the rewiring is too visible as a power play.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Coase's Theorem of Externality Allocation combined with Pfeffer & Salancik's Resource Dependence Theory. The Coasean insight: whoever bears the cost of an inefficiency determines whether it gets solved, regardless of who could most cheaply solve it. Resource dependence supplies the second half: the party with structural power is the only one whose felt costs translate into organizational action. Together, they imply that strategic re-routing of externalities is more politically efficient than direct advocacy.

**Decision Alteration.** Transforms the powerful party's preference structure by making their continued indifference materially expensive. The decision-maker shifts from 'this is someone else's problem and I will not spend capital on it' to 'this is now my problem and I will spend capital to make it stop.' Crucially, the deflector never has to make the case for the change — the powerful party makes it for them.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best in organizations with siloed accountability metrics (so re-routed pain shows up cleanly on the target's scorecard), in mature institutions where powerful parties have low tolerance for new friction on their numbers, and in environments where the deflector has enough operational latitude to restructure flows without requiring upfront approval.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A finance director has wanted to replace a patchwork of departmental expense systems for two years but has been unable to get budget approved. She quietly changes the audit reporting structure so that expense compliance exceptions now flow directly into the CFO's monthly board package rather than being aggregated and softened in her own.

**Sample X:** The CFO publicly demands that the company adopt a centralized expense management platform

**Sample Y:** The CFO continues to view expense automation as an HR / IT cost-saving initiative that lacks executive air cover

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The platform now solves a problem the CFO is actively presenting to the board, converting it from a 'finance ops nice-to-have' to a 'CFO-led modernization initiative' — benefiting from the prestige and budget access of CFO sponsorship.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active in this configuration. The platform implementation cost (vendor selection, integration, change management) is unchanged.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The 'just let finance ops handle the exceptions quietly' option loses its appeal once exceptions are visible monthly to the board — the benefit of letting the status quo continue (no executive attention required) no longer exists.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each month of inaction now produces a board-visible compliance exception count attached to the CFO's name. Inaction is now publicly expensive in a way it was not before the audit reporting change.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A logistics officer at a forward operating base has tried for years to replace an obsolete supply-tracking system. She restructures the after-action review process so that supply-readiness failures are reported against the combat readiness scorecard of the line units the commander personally evaluates, rather than against the logistics function's internal metrics.

**Sample X:** The regional military commander champions an upgrade to the supply chain logistics software

**Sample Y:** The regional commander continues to dismiss the logistics modernization as a back-office IT initiative

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The upgrade now improves the readiness scores the commander is judged on, transforming a logistics-office request into a combat-effectiveness imperative championed by line officers.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. The procurement, training, and integration costs of the new system remain identical.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Keeping the legacy system loses its 'invisible and proven' advantage once supply failures are visibly hurting line readiness numbers — the bureaucratic benefit of familiarity is gone.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each supply failure now degrades a metric the commander reports up the chain personally, converting an absorbed back-office cost into a career-relevant readiness number on the commander's record.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable wherever (a) an organization measures performance through siloed scorecards, (b) the actor has enough operational latitude to rewire workflows or reporting structures, and (c) there exists a politically dominant party whose felt pain would translate into action. Found in corporate functions, military commands, government bureaucracies, university administration, and family-business succession dynamics. Less effective in small flat organizations where pain is already universally visible and the powerful party cannot be 'newly informed.'

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A compliance officer routes regulatory near-misses directly to the audit committee's quarterly briefing rather than letting them aggregate in her own report, manufacturing board-level demand for the compliance system upgrade legal has been requesting for years.
- Government: A career civil servant in a small bureau quietly re-codes a workflow so that constituent complaints about outdated forms now route to the elected official's correspondence team rather than being absorbed by the bureau, generating political pressure for the digitization the bureau itself could never win.
- Academic: A department chair restructures the course evaluation reporting so that complaints about an obsolete required textbook now appear on the dean's annual student satisfaction dashboard rather than buried in departmental files, prompting the dean to demand the curriculum change the chair has wanted.
- Lobbying: An industry coalition reframes a regulatory ambiguity so the cost of confusion now falls on consumer-facing companies with brand-protection budgets rather than on B2B suppliers with no political muscle, manufacturing demand for the regulatory clarification the suppliers wanted but couldn't fund the lobbying for.
- Family business: A second-generation operations head reorganizes inventory accounting so the obsolete-stock write-offs that used to be quietly absorbed by the warehouse now hit the patriarch's annual P&L review directly, manufacturing his demand for the inventory system modernization he had previously dismissed as expensive software.
- NGO: A program manager restructures donor reporting so that operational inefficiencies in field offices now surface in the board's quarterly impact metrics rather than in the COO's internal report, generating board-level pressure for the IT modernization the program team had been advocating for unsuccessfully.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both lenses converge on Strategy 13 (Polarization Triple) — B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑ — and on the same fundamental operating signature: the change's actual cost doesn't move, but the political economy around the change is inverted by relocating the externality. The specific lens emphasizes the workflow restructuring as the operational move; the generic lens emphasizes the resource-dependence calculus that determines which party's felt costs become organizational action.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: Use the specific lens when coaching a practitioner who has hit the wall on a particular cross-departmental fight — the specific framing surfaces the exact operational degrees of freedom (reporting structures, workflow handoffs, metric definitions) that can be re-engineered without authorization. When to use the generic lens: Use the generic lens when diagnosing why advocacy efforts are stalling more broadly, or when designing institutional reforms — the Coasean/Pfefferian reading reveals that the bottleneck is rarely the merit of the proposal but rather which party currently feels the pain of inaction.

### Resolution Approach

Within this cluster of accountability-engineering tactics, PROBLEM OWNERSHIP DEFLECTION is distinctive because it does not seek to AVOID blame (unlike BLAME DIVERSION FIELDS, EXPENDABLE ALLIES, or POLITICAL FIREBREAKS in the same cluster) — it seeks to RELOCATE responsibility-for-solving onto a party powerful enough to act. The practitioner takeaway: when you cannot win the argument that something matters, stop arguing and re-engineer the situation so the people who already matter feel the pain. Advocacy is expensive; inevitability is free. The tactic's deepest cost is moral — you are imposing on a colleague the suffering they would not otherwise have borne, in service of a change you believe is right but cannot win on its merits.
