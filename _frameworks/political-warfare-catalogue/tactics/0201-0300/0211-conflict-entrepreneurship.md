# 0211 — CONFLICT ENTREPRENEURSHIP

> Identifying latent disagreements between departments or leaders and strategically escalating them into open conflict to weaken their collective power or distract them from the main agenda.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C50 — Divide and Conquer, Adversarial Collaboration & Coalition Disruption

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Machiavelli, Sherif

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title 'CONFLICT ENTREPRENEURSHIP' is a sociological term of art (Volkan, Kalyvas) describing actors who manufacture and monetize conflict, while the description anchors in a specific organizational frame — departmental and leadership rivalries being surfaced and escalated. The label and the example are doing different work.

**Specific Interpretation.** An internal actor scans the matrix-organization seams — finance vs. operations, region heads vs. product heads, founders vs. incoming professional management — for unresolved jurisdictional or resource grievances, then engineers visible flashpoints (a budget challenge, a memo leak, a public meeting question) to convert latent tension into active feud that absorbs the rivals' attention and political capital.

**Generic Interpretation.** A third party identifies pre-existing fault lines within a coalition that would otherwise oppose its agenda, then invests resources to escalate those fault lines into open conflict so the coalition's collective power is consumed in internal warfare rather than directed outward at the entrepreneur's project.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the description names departments and leaders, the underlying move — third-party investment in escalating an existing fissure to fragment a coalition — is the canonical divide-and-rule mechanism documented from Caesar's Gaul through Bismarck's Kleinstaaterei management through modern proxy-war doctrine. The organizational example is one application of a strategy whose logic was articulated long before the modern firm existed, making the generic reading the more fundamental one.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Rival department heads or executives spend their political capital fighting each other rather than scrutinizing the entrepreneur's initiative
- **Option Y:** Rival department heads or executives recognize their shared interest in blocking the entrepreneur's initiative and coordinate against it
- **Context:** A mid-tier executive (or staff function leader) advancing a controversial agenda — a reorganization, a new budget allocation, a personnel change — where two or more senior peers, if aligned, would have the standing to kill the initiative; the executive notices unresolved historical grievances between them (a previous turf loss, a credit dispute, a hiring veto) that have been kept quiet under a thin veneer of professional cooperation

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does nothing to improve the actual merits of the entrepreneur's agenda. The agenda remains exactly as attractive (or unattractive) as before; the move only operates on the opposition's ability to act on it. A weak proposal still gets weakly received — it just doesn't get coordinated against.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the primary lever. When two senior rivals are fighting each other, the political cost of advancing the entrepreneur's agenda drops sharply: there is no coordinated counter-coalition, no joint memo to the CEO, no aligned questioning in the steering committee. The entrepreneur can move through the resulting vacuum at low resistance, often without ever being identified as the originator of the fight.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The 'benefit' of the rivals' coalition was its joint veto power. Once they are in open feud, that joint power evaporates — each side now views joining the other as endorsing a rival, which carries reputational and tribal costs internally to their own factions. The coalition's payoff is destroyed not because either rival was defeated but because the coalition cannot form.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Sustaining unified opposition becomes actively costly: each rival must spend political capital defending against the other, defending their factional loyalists, and managing the reputational fallout of grievances now surfaced in public. The cost of holding the coalition together exceeds the benefit of opposing the entrepreneur's agenda.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration is diagnostic of the tactic's signature: the entrepreneur never has to defend the merits of their agenda (B(X)↑ stays off), because the entire mechanism operates on the opposition's coordination capacity. By making the rivals' coalition expensive to maintain and worthless to belong to, the entrepreneur's path clears without the entrepreneur ever having to sell X — which is exactly why the move is attractive when X is genuinely weak on merits.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) genuine pre-existing grievance — manufactured conflict from nothing is detectable and backfires; (b) plausible deniability of authorship — the spark must look like it came from the rivals themselves or from neutral organizational machinery; (c) timing such that the conflict peaks while the entrepreneur's decision window is open; (d) cultural context where airing senior rivalry is taboo enough that escalation imposes real reputational cost. Fails catastrophically when the rivals diagnose the entrepreneur as the source and reconcile against the common manipulator — the classic Roman-republic 'concordia ordinum' counter-move.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Coalition Theory (Riker's size principle and minimum-winning-coalition logic) combined with Realistic Conflict Theory (Sherif) — coalitions are fragile equilibria held together by shared external threat, and when latent in-group grievances are surfaced, the perceived cost of cooperating with a former ally exceeds the perceived benefit of joint action against the outsider. The mechanism is also a direct operationalization of Machiavelli's divide et impera and Schelling's analysis of coordination failure under defection incentives.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not persuade the opposition that the entrepreneur is right; it persuades each member of the opposition that their immediate rival is a more pressing threat than the entrepreneur. By raising the salience of intra-coalition grievance above the salience of the external threat, it inverts the threat hierarchy in the targets' minds. The choice they were going to make jointly (oppose X) is replaced by a choice they make individually (defeat their rival), and individual rationality produces collective passivity toward X.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when (a) the targeted coalition is a coalition of convenience rather than conviction, held together by shared opposition rather than shared values; (b) at least one genuine historical grievance exists between coalition members; (c) the entrepreneur has plausible distance from the spark (a leaked memo, an unrelated third party who can be prodded into asking the inflammatory question); (d) the broader system rewards or tolerates internal conflict (zero-sum budget cultures, winner-take-all promotion ladders, low-trust environments).

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A corporate lobbyist representing a manufacturing trade association identifies that labor unions resent environmental groups for past campaigns against unionized heavy industry (steel, coal, auto), while environmental groups resent unions for endorsing pipeline projects. The lobbyist seeds op-eds and committee testimony framing the tax bill as protecting blue-collar jobs threatened by 'green deindustrialization,' forcing each side to choose between opposing the bill and antagonizing their factional base.

**Sample X:** A national legislature passes a corporate-friendly tax reform that a unified labor-environmental coalition would have blocked

**Sample Y:** Labor unions and environmental NGOs coordinate their lobbying and floor-vote pressure to defeat the bill

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The bill's substantive merits are not improved; the lobbyist invests in fragmenting the opposition coalition rather than strengthening the bill's case

- C(X)↓ ✓ — With labor and environmental witnesses arguing past each other at committee hearings, the bill faces no unified counter-narrative, dramatically reducing the political cost legislators pay for supporting it

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The labor-environmental coalition's distinctive value was its ability to credibly claim it represented both working-class and public-interest concerns; once visibly fractured, neither group can deliver that combined credibility, destroying the coalition's primary payoff

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Maintaining the coalition now requires each side to publicly defend the other against attacks from their own base, imposing internal political costs that exceed the marginal benefit of joint opposition

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A junior staff officer working on a joint task force notices longstanding inter-service rivalry between the Navy and Air Force over carrier-strike vs. long-range-bomber doctrine for Pacific deterrence. She prepares a briefing that frames her reform as definitively settling which service 'owns' a contested mission area, knowing the briefing will trigger each service chief to attack the other's claim rather than scrutinize the reform itself.

**Sample X:** A junior officer's reform proposal advances through the joint command structure

**Sample Y:** Senior service chiefs jointly block the proposal as a threat to traditional doctrine

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The reform proposal's strategic logic is not strengthened; the officer relies entirely on opposition fragmentation to clear its path

- C(X)↓ ✓ — With service chiefs locked in turf dispute, the proposal advances through joint-staff review without the unified senior pushback that would otherwise have killed a junior officer's reform

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — A unified service-chief veto carries near-absolute weight in joint-command decisions; once the chiefs are visibly fighting each other over mission ownership, their joint authority over the reform is gone

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each chief now risks losing the mission-ownership fight by appearing to concede the contested ground to the other service, making continued joint opposition to the reform actively expensive in inter-service terms

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable in any context with three or more political actors where coalition arithmetic determines outcomes — corporate boards, legislatures, military joint commands, academic faculties, religious councils, family-business successions, international diplomacy, activist coalitions. Less effective in dyadic settings (only two actors, no coalition to split) or in high-trust monocultures where airing grievance is so taboo that escalation cannot get traction. Also requires the entrepreneur to have access to the seams of the rival coalition — outsiders without informational reach cannot reliably identify which grievances are escalable.

### Core Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CFO advancing an unpopular cost-cutting program leaks competing headcount targets to two regional VPs known to resent each other, ensuring they fight over staff allocation rather than coordinating against the program itself
- Diplomacy: A foreign ministry quietly amplifies a border-water dispute between two neighboring rival states to prevent them from forming a regional bloc against its own trade policy
- Academic: A department chair seeking to push through a controversial tenure case surfaces an old methodological feud between two senior critics so they spend the committee meeting attacking each other's epistemological premises rather than the candidate's record
- Lobbying: An industry association activates the long-standing primacy dispute between two consumer advocacy organizations to prevent them from issuing a joint position paper against pending regulation
- Activism: An incumbent regime amplifies sectarian or generational fissures within an opposition movement — funding the speaking tour of a moderate faction's harshest internal critic — to convert street-level coalition into internecine doctrinal war
- Family business: A non-family CEO advancing a professionalization agenda quietly reopens a decade-old inheritance grievance between two adult siblings on the board, ensuring they cannot align on a unified family veto

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Specific and generic analyses converge on strategy 14 (Resistance Triple) — both readings agree that the tactic's signature is the conspicuous absence of B(X)↑. The entrepreneur never sells the agenda; they only ensure no one is organized to block it. This convergence is itself the interpretive payoff: a tactic whose strategy classification is identical at both the bounded-organizational and abstract-pattern levels is one whose mechanism is genuinely structural rather than incidental to context.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's defining feature — that it operates entirely on the opposition's coordination rather than on the entrepreneur's case — makes it the rational choice precisely when the entrepreneur's agenda is weak on merits. A strong agenda would advance via B(X)↑; only a weak or politically untenable agenda needs Resistance Triple. This is diagnostic: the prevalence of conflict entrepreneurship in an organization is a leading indicator that the organization is making consequential decisions on agendas that could not survive open debate. Counter-defense is institutional, not personal: coalition members who detect they are being played must engage in explicit threat reassessment ('which of us is the bigger danger to both of us — each other, or the third party benefiting from our fight?') — the historical formula of 'concordia ordinum' or 'unite against the common manipulator.' When to use the specific lens: When advising or analyzing within a defined organizational system — corporate, governmental, military — where the named seams (departmental, jurisdictional, generational) provide concrete attack surface and where the institutional pre-existing grievances can be inventoried. The specific lens is also more useful when the question is operational: which fissure, when, with what spark. When to use the generic lens: When the question is strategic rather than operational: should this move be made at all, and what does its availability tell us about the actor's confidence in their own case. The generic lens also surfaces the deepest counter-defense — coalition self-awareness — which the specific lens tends to lose in tactical detail.

### Resolution Approach

The conspicuous absence of B(X)↑ is the tactic's tell. An entrepreneur who never has to argue for their agenda is an entrepreneur whose agenda cannot bear argument; the move's structural signature reveals the substantive weakness it is designed to hide. For practitioners, this cuts both ways — it is a powerful tool when your case is weak, and a powerful diagnostic when you are the target.
