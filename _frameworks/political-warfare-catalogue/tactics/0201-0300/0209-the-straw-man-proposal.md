# 0209 — THE STRAW MAN PROPOSAL

> Deliberately creating and promoting a weak alternative plan to the desired strategy, ensuring the preferred option looks stronger by comparison during the decision-making process.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#11 X-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓` · **Cluster:** C22 — Choice Architecture & Option Engineering

**Cited principles:** Kahneman, Tversky

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title evokes the rhetorical 'straw man' (a generic argumentation pattern of erecting a weakened version of an opposing view) while the description anchors the move in a concrete organizational decision-making process — drafting and tabling an actual alternative plan alongside the preferred one. The tactic lives in both registers: it is a decision-architecture move at a specific table AND a reusable contrast-engineering pattern.

**Specific Interpretation.** An actor seeking approval for a particular strategy authors or sponsors a competing 'serious' alternative that is engineered to look credible enough to pass as deliberation but is deliberately flawed on dimensions the decision-makers care about. The fake competition flatters the preferred option.

**Generic Interpretation.** A choice-set manipulation in which an asymmetrically-dominated decoy is inserted to shift the perceived attractiveness of a target option by contrast. This is the classic decoy effect / attraction effect — the mechanism is reusable in pricing, negotiation, hiring, policy design, and political messaging.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The underlying mechanism — manufacturing an inferior comparator to weaponize the contrast effect — is documented across decision research (Huber, Payne & Puto's asymmetric dominance) and reappears in dozens of domains far beyond proposal politics. The organizational use is one application of the broader pattern, so the generic reading is more fundamental.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The decision body approves the actor's preferred strategy
- **Option Y:** The decision body approves the tabled alternative strategy (or defers/seeks more options)
- **Context:** A formal proposal review — strategy committee, board session, steering committee, executive offsite — where multiple options are expected to be considered as evidence of due diligence, and the actor sponsors both the preferred plan and a fabricated weaker comparator

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The preferred plan's strengths become visible by contrast — gaps in the straw man (under-resourced, slower timeline, weaker KPIs, untested vendors) make the preferred plan's corresponding features read as 'comparatively excellent' rather than 'adequate'. The benefit inflation happens at perception level without changing the plan itself.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Approving the preferred plan becomes politically cheaper because it can be framed as 'the choice that emerged from deliberation' rather than 'the only choice the sponsor brought' — defusing accusations of railroading by manufacturing the appearance of meaningful comparison.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The straw man IS the alternative in the room — and it has been engineered to underperform. Y's benefit is suppressed by construction, not by argument, which is why this lever operates so cleanly: the tactic doesn't have to attack a real rival, it builds the rival to lose.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — Not active

The tactic does not raise the cost of choosing Y through penalties, friction, or coercion. Decision-makers who picked the straw man would face no manufactured downstream punishment — they simply chose the visibly worse option. The tactic operates through degradation of Y's appeal, not obstruction of its selection.

### Strategy

**#11 X-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓`

**Rationale.** The lever configuration is persuasion-heavy and coercion-light: the actor doesn't have to threaten anyone choosing the alternative because the alternative has been built to lose on the merits. The simultaneous activation of B(X)↑ (by contrast), C(X)↓ (by 'we did consider options'), and B(Y)↓ (by construction) produces a decision that feels deliberative to participants while being effectively pre-determined.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires that the actor controls or heavily influences the option-set presented to the decision body, that the straw man is plausible enough to pass surface scrutiny (a transparently weak option blows the cover and signals manipulation), and that the decision body lacks an independent source of alternative proposals. Fails when a skeptical member commissions an outside option, when a sponsor of an unrelated real alternative crashes the meeting, or when the straw man is so weak it reveals the choreography.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Decoy Effect / Asymmetric Dominance (Huber, Payne & Puto, 1982); reinforced by Tversky & Kahneman's framing and contrast effects. A dominated third option shifts preference between the original two by making the target option look comparatively superior on salient attributes.

**Decision Alteration.** Choice between two options is replaced with choice among three, where the third is dominated by the target on the attributes the decision-maker weights most heavily. The dominated option doesn't compete for selection — it competes for attention, redirecting the comparison so the target wins by contrast rather than on absolute merit.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when decision-makers feel pressure to demonstrate that a comparison was performed, when the attributes of options are visible but their underlying value is uncertain, and when the actor controls the option-construction stage. Most powerful when the audience confuses 'multiple options were considered' with 'options were independently sourced'.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A SaaS pricing page introduces a 'Premium' tier at $80/month with features deliberately calibrated to look weaker per dollar than Pro — the Premium tier exists primarily to anchor and make Pro feel like the obvious value choice

**Sample X:** Customer subscribes to the mid-tier 'Pro' plan at $24/month

**Sample Y:** Customer subscribes to the entry-tier 'Basic' plan at $9/month

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Premium's poor value-per-dollar makes Pro's feature-to-price ratio appear excellent by direct adjacency on the same page

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Buyer's deliberation cost drops because the three-way comparison resolves quickly toward the middle option — the decoy short-circuits the harder 'do I really need to upgrade from Basic?' question

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Basic's appeal erodes by association — once Premium establishes the high-end anchor, Basic stops looking 'cheap and efficient' and starts looking 'stripped-down'

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — No penalty is added for choosing Basic — its decline in appeal is purely comparative, not punitive

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Caucus leadership floats a deliberately maximalist 'shadow bill' through a friendly member — a version they know cannot pass and contains provisions designed to be unacceptable — so that the moderate bill subsequently feels like the responsible compromise rather than itself being an aggressive policy shift

**Sample X:** Senator votes for the moderate bipartisan immigration bill

**Sample Y:** Senator votes against any immigration legislation this session

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Moderate bill's provisions read as restrained and reasonable specifically because the shadow bill established the 'extreme' end of the visible range

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Senators voting yes can frame their vote as choosing 'the responsible option that emerged from negotiation,' reducing the constituent-explanation cost

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The 'do nothing' position loses moral weight because the visible debate is now framed as moderate-versus-extreme, not action-versus-inaction

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — No formal cost is attached to a 'no' vote — the tactic shifts the framing, not the procedural consequences

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any decision context with constructed option-sets and at least one party with influence over what enters the choice set — pricing, hiring slates, vendor selections, real estate showings, menu design, RFP shortlists, board proposals, legislative drafting, and negotiation anchoring. Less effective in contexts with truly open option-sourcing (open RFPs with strong third-party submissions) or sophisticated decision-makers trained to spot dominated alternatives (procurement analysts, behavioral economists, experienced VCs).

### Application Examples

- Corporate strategy: a CSO tables a 'comparable' acquisition target with worse fit specifically to make the preferred target's strategic logic look obvious to the board
- Government procurement: a program manager invites a token third bidder known to lack key capabilities so the preferred vendor wins a 'competed' contract that passes audit scrutiny
- Real estate: an agent walks a buyer through two overpriced or poorly-maintained listings before showing the one the agent wants them to buy, leveraging order and contrast
- Academic hiring: a search committee chair quietly advances a weaker third finalist alongside the preferred candidate so the appointment letter reads as the considered choice from a real slate
- Sales negotiation: a vendor presents a deliberately overscoped 'enterprise' package alongside the target package to make the latter feel like restrained, custom-fit pricing
- Diplomatic negotiation: a delegation floats a maximalist opening position through a junior team that everyone knows will be rejected, so the actual desired settlement reads as moderate

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both analyses converge on Strategy 11 (Promotion Triple) — the specific organizational use and the generic decoy pattern operate through the same three-lever signature: B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ with no coercive C(Y)↑. This convergence is itself diagnostic: the tactic's distinctive politeness — its lack of explicit punishment for choosing the alternative — is exactly what gives it deniability and survival value at decision tables where overt manipulation would be repelled.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: When analyzing a single observed decision episode where you suspect option-engineering is in play — strategy reviews, budget cycles, vendor selections, succession discussions. The specific lens forces the analyst to ask: who controlled the option set, who sponsored the weaker comparator, and what does the comparator's calibration reveal about the sponsor's read of the audience? When to use the generic lens: When designing a choice architecture from scratch — pricing pages, RFP structures, ballot constructions, menu engineering — or when training decision-makers to spot the pattern across contexts. The generic lens makes the mechanism portable and teaches the structural countermeasure: independently source one option per decision and refuse to evaluate slates whose construction you don't trust.

### Resolution Approach

The tactic's signature is the conspicuous absence of C(Y)↑ — it never punishes anyone for choosing the alternative because the alternative was engineered to lose. This is what differentiates it from sibling cluster tactics like THE 'NO ALTERNATIVE' FRAMEWORK (which suppresses alternatives entirely) and THE HOBSON'S CHOICE PRESENTATION (which raises the cost of refusal): the straw man preserves the visible ritual of choice precisely so the decision feels owned by the chooser. Practitioners defending against it should not look for pressure — they should audit who built the slate.

## Straw Man Proposal Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** Manufactured asymmetric dominance — the comparator is constructed to be plausible at surface inspection but dominated on the attributes that drive the decision

**Calibration Constraint.** The straw man must be calibrated within a narrow band: weak enough to lose, strong enough not to expose the choreography. Over-calibration (too obviously weak) signals manipulation; under-calibration (accidentally strong) risks actually winning

**Deniability Structure.** Because the tactic operates through option construction rather than overt advocacy, the actor can point to having presented multiple options as evidence of fair process — the manipulation is upstream of the visible deliberation

**Counter Measures.** Independent option-sourcing (require at least one option proposed by a non-sponsor), structured option-evaluation rubrics filled out before discussion, and an explicit 'who built this slate and why these options?' opening question at decision meetings

**Failure Modes.** A skeptical participant who tables a fourth option breaks the curated three-way comparison; an outsider with no allegiance to the sponsor evaluates the slate and notices the dominance pattern; the straw man's sponsor (often a junior collaborator) inadvertently advocates for it sincerely and disrupts the choreography
