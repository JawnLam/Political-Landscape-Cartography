# 0281 — JURISDICTIONAL CREEP

> Slowly and incrementally expanding the responsibilities of a favored department into the domain of another, until a functional takeover is achieved without a formal reorganization.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C20 — Incrementalism, Normalization & Boiling-Frog Drift

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Festinger

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a specific intra-organizational scenario (one department absorbing another's domain), but the underlying mechanism — accumulating small, individually-defensible expansions into a structural fait accompli — is a general pattern visible from imperial border policy to platform feature creep. The ambiguity is whether 'jurisdiction' is read as a literal departmental org-chart boundary or as any contested operational domain.

**Specific Interpretation.** A favored department's leader incrementally takes on adjacent tasks 'just this once' or 'as a favor' until the rival department has been functionally hollowed out and a formal reorganization merely ratifies the existing reality. The decision being engineered is each individual handoff, not the eventual takeover.

**Generic Interpretation.** An actor uses the principle that defensible-at-each-step expansion evades the threshold of resistance that a single large move would trigger. Each step is too small to fight, but the cumulative trajectory is the entire point. This is the strategic logic of normalization-of-deviance applied to boundary disputes.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the departmental-takeover framing is the canonical example, the operational essence is the incrementalism — Schelling's observation that gradual transgressions evade the bright-line responses that step-changes would provoke. The mechanism is reusable wherever a boundary exists between two domains of authority, whether that boundary is between departments, agencies, nations, professions, or technological platforms. The specific reading is one application of a broader strategic principle.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Allow the favored department to handle this 'one-off' task or temporary overflow from your domain
- **Option Y:** Insist that this task remain within your department's formal jurisdiction and refuse the handoff
- **Context:** A mid-tier executive whose department is being slowly encroached upon faces repeated small requests — a system migration, an analytics workflow, a vendor relationship — that the favored department offers to 'help with.' Each request, taken in isolation, looks like collegial assistance rather than territorial absorption.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not enhance the genuine merit of letting the favored department absorb the task — it does not argue that they are objectively better at it. The whole point is that the handoff appears trivial enough that no merit case needs to be made. The lever is not how this tactic operates.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core of the tactic. Each individual handoff is framed as low-cost — a favor, a temporary measure, an efficiency gain, a way to relieve workload during a busy period. Saying yes once carries no apparent political cost because the boundary is being crossed only marginally. The cumulative cost of jurisdictional loss is hidden by the granularity of the asks.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not argue that keeping the task is illegitimate or unappealing on the merits — it simply makes the alternative (refusal) feel pedantic and bureaucratic in the moment. The benefits of jurisdictional defense remain intact in principle; they are simply not invoked because no single instance seems to warrant invocation.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Refusing any single handoff incurs reputational cost — looking territorial, uncooperative, or obstructionist for a trivial matter. The cost is moderate per-instance but consistently present, and accumulates: the executive who repeatedly says no on small matters develops a reputation as the bottleneck, which weakens their position on the larger ones.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates entirely on the cost dimension. Yielding any single piece of jurisdiction is made to feel trivially cheap while defending it is made to feel disproportionately expensive in social and reputational terms. The mechanism deliberately avoids contesting the benefit dimension because doing so would force the explicit territorial argument the tactic is designed to evade.

**Effectiveness factors.** Success requires three conditions: (1) the favored department must have demonstrably better access to leadership cover, so refusals carry political risk; (2) the encroachment must be paced slowly enough that no single ask triggers a 'wait, what is happening?' response; (3) the targeted department's leader must lack the political standing or attentional bandwidth to map the trajectory. The tactic typically fails when the targeted leader keeps a written ledger of handoffs and forces an explicit boundary conversation early, before the cumulative shift is irreversible.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Schelling's Salami Tactics combined with the Normalization of Deviance (Vaughan, building on Festinger). Schelling identified that gradual transgressions evade the bright-line responses that step-changes provoke because each slice falls below the threshold of warranted reprisal; Vaughan's analysis of organizational drift shows how repeated small deviations from a baseline become the new baseline, with the original line forgotten. Together they describe a sequenced influence pattern where each individual move is defensible but the trajectory is decisive.

**Decision Alteration.** The target is never confronted with the actual question — 'will you cede this entire domain?' — because that question would be refused. Instead they are repeatedly confronted with micro-questions whose locally rational answer (yes, that small ask is reasonable) aggregates to the answer the actor wanted to the large question. The decision is altered by disaggregation: a single 100-unit transfer is replaced by 100 single-unit transfers, each below the threshold of contestation.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where (a) jurisdictional boundaries are not codified with hard enforcement (most organizations, most informal alliances, many international borders), (b) the target's attention is consumed by other matters such that small drifts are not tallied, and (c) the pace of encroachment can be calibrated to fall just below the contestation threshold. Fails against targets who institute formal tracking, who treat the first incursion as a precedent worth fighting, or who can credibly threaten symmetric retaliation.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A regional manager at a consumer goods company who has the CEO's ear keeps offering to 'pick up' specific marketing functions — launch coordination, retailer relationships, regional pricing — during periods when central marketing is stretched. The CMO faces each ask in isolation and, after 18 months, discovers their team has been hollowed to a brand-stewardship role while the regional manager runs commercial execution.

**Sample X:** Allow the regional manager to handle this quarter's product launch coordination in addition to their existing remit

**Sample Y:** Insist that product launch coordination remain the central marketing team's responsibility

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The asks are not framed as 'we will do this better' — that would invite the comparison the tactic avoids. They are framed as offers of help, sidestepping the merit question.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Each handoff is framed as a temporary accommodation during a busy quarter, making acceptance the path of least resistance for an already-overloaded CMO. The political cost of yielding feels low because it is framed as time-bounded relief rather than structural loss.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Not active. The CMO's claim to launch coordination remains formally intact and even verbally affirmed — the tactic does not contest legitimacy, only operational reality.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Refusing the offered help in the middle of a stretched quarter would mean visibly struggling to deliver while a colleague offered to help. The reputational cost of refusal in front of the CEO is what tilts each individual decision.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A combatant commander whose theater authority has been chipped at by a newer specialized command (e.g., a cyber or special operations command with direct access to the Secretary of Defense) finds the new command repeatedly proposing to 'lead' specific operations that fall in a gray zone of doctrine. Each operation is plausibly within the new command's remit; cumulatively, the theater commander finds their operational scope narrowed to conventional ground warfare alone.

**Sample X:** Accept the new tactical command's request to coordinate this specific cross-border patrol operation

**Sample Y:** Refuse the request and require the operation be run through the established theater command structure

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The new command does not argue it would conduct operations more effectively — that would force a doctrinal fight. It simply offers to relieve coordination burden on a case-by-case basis.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Each operation looks like a gray-zone edge case where deferring is the safe, conflict-avoidant call. Approving the handoff costs nothing in the moment and avoids a turf war with a command that has direct access to civilian leadership.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Not active. Theater command authority remains formally codified in the unified command plan; no one is arguing it should not exist.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Insisting on theater command primacy for a small operation looks like institutional jealousy and an inability to work jointly — a career-damaging perception for any flag officer in the joint era.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Operates wherever (a) two authorities have adjacent operational domains, (b) the boundary is interpretable rather than hard-coded, and (c) the favored actor has standing to plausibly offer help on the boundary. This includes inter-departmental dynamics in corporations, inter-agency rivalries in government, professional turf disputes (e.g., nurse practitioners absorbing primary care from physicians), platform companies absorbing adjacent product categories, and territorial creep in international relations. Less effective in domains with hard procedural triggers (e.g., regulated banking activities where charter scope is legally bounded) or where the target maintains tight ledgers of incursions.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A favored business unit absorbs analytics, customer data, and finally pricing authority from an adjacent unit over 24 months via repeated 'we'll just take this one' offers during transitions, until the formal reorg merely ratifies the takeover.
- Government: A newer cabinet-level agency (e.g., a homeland security or digital services entity) draws specific programs from established departments under 'whole-of-government coordination' framings, until budget authority for the underlying functions migrates with the work.
- Academic: A growing interdisciplinary center hosts seminars, hires affiliated faculty, and runs joint searches with a traditional department until the department's graduate program has been functionally absorbed without a vote on merger.
- Lobbying: An industry association expands from its original technical-standards mandate into trade policy advocacy by serving on 'consultative' working groups, ending up the dominant voice on issues its original charter did not contemplate.
- Diplomacy: A neighboring state quietly extends civil administration — postal service, electrical grid, school curriculum — into a disputed frontier region one service at a time, until reversal would require expelling functioning institutions.
- Family business: The favored sibling who is helping with 'just the books' progressively absorbs vendor relationships, banking, and finally signing authority, until the founder's intended successor inherits a title without operational substance.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both lenses converge on the same operational signature: the tactic works by disaggregating a contested transfer into sub-threshold installments, each of which falls below the recipient's contestation threshold. The specific reading emphasizes the organizational topology (a favored department, a relative loser, a formal reorg deferred); the generic reading emphasizes the sequencing logic (any boundary, any actor with patience). Within the cluster of incremental tactics, JURISDICTIONAL CREEP is most closely related to MISSION CREEP AS STRATEGY (which expands a single entity's mandate outward into open space) and INCREMENTAL ENCROACHMENT (the abstract pattern); JURISDICTIONAL CREEP is the contested-boundary case where another party's domain is the explicit target, distinguishing it from the open-space land-grab of mission creep.

### Strategic Implications

The deepest implication is that jurisdictional defense is asymmetrically expensive: the defender must contest every incursion at full political cost (looking territorial each time), while the encroacher pays only a marginal cost per ask. This means rational individual responses by the defender (yield on small matters, conserve political capital for big ones) systematically lose to the tactic. Effective counter requires shifting from per-incident response to ledger-based defense: keeping a written tally and forcing the explicit boundary conversation early, before cumulative drift makes the original line illegible. Once jurisdiction is recoverable only by un-doing established practice, the defender has already lost.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when coaching a leader inside an active turf situation — naming the favored department, the pace, and the political cover behind the encroachment makes the dynamic actionable. Use the generic lens when designing institutional safeguards (audit cycles, charter reviews, boundary documentation) that work against the pattern regardless of which specific actor is encroaching. The two lenses also point to different counter-tactics: specific suggests targeted alliance-building with leadership; generic suggests procedural tripwires that fire on cumulative drift rather than individual incidents.
