# 0274 — CRITERIA SHIFTING

> Continuously changing the success metrics or goals for a rival's project after they have achieved the initial objectives, ensuring they always appear to be falling short.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C05 — Data Manipulation & Metric Engineering

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Arkes & Blumer, Arkes, Blumer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a specific dyadic setup (an actor mutating the success metrics of a rival's project after the rival has met them), but the underlying move — unilaterally redefining the finish line after effort is sunk — is a broadly recurring pattern across negotiation, regulation, parenting, and grant-making. The tactic title names a structural maneuver; the description specifies an interpersonal-rivalry deployment.

**Specific Interpretation.** An internal political rival has launched a project, the project hits its declared milestones, and a peer or superior responds not by acknowledging completion but by introducing fresh criteria — quietly raising the bar so the rival's public record reads as a chain of near-misses rather than a chain of wins.

**Generic Interpretation.** An evaluator with control over success definitions exploits the asymmetry between a committed actor (who has already paid the cost of producing toward an old bar) and a redefinable target (whose new bar costs the evaluator little to declare), engineering a perpetual deficit state in the committed party.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the rival-project framing is vivid, the operative mechanism — exploiting sunk effort by changing the metric after delivery — is reusable wherever one party controls the definition of success and another has already invested toward an earlier definition. The same move appears in salary reviews, regulatory enforcement, dissertation committees, and acquisition due diligence. The rival-undermining application is one instance of a broader evaluator-power pattern.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Audiences (sponsors, peers, the rival's team) read the rival's project as chronically underperforming and the rival as a leader who keeps falling short
- **Option Y:** Audiences read the rival as having shipped what was asked and credit the wins as completed
- **Context:** A mid-to-senior leader's high-visibility initiative inside a matrixed organization where success criteria are set in joint forums but interpreted by the actor who owns the next quarterly review packet

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make the rival-fails narrative more attractive on its own merits; it does not promote a competing project or vision. Audiences are not given more reason to want the rival to fail — they are simply given a metric frame in which failure is the only available reading.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Believing the rival is underperforming still carries the same social cost (reputational reassessment, awkward 1:1s, withdrawal of support). The tactic does not reduce the friction of forming the negative view; it just makes the negative view the parsimonious one.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Central operating lever. Each time the rival hits the declared bar, the previously-promised payoff (recognition, budget renewal, promotion candidacy) is retroactively voided by the new criterion. The benefit of the 'rival is succeeding' reading is repeatedly drained — every win is converted, in real time, into 'partial progress against the updated target.'

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Defending the rival becomes increasingly expensive for supporters because each fresh criterion creates a new gap that has to be argued away. Sponsors who publicly backed the rival now look like they are grading on a curve, which carries reputational cost. Loss aversion on the rival's side compounds: having shipped to the old bar, conceding the new bar feels like surrendering work already done.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic is purely anti-rival: it does not need to construct a positive case for any alternative leader or project, only to ensure that the rival's outputs are never read as wins. Acting on both sides of the alternative axis — voiding the benefit of each delivered milestone and raising the cost of continuing to defend the rival — produces a clean 'this leader can't quite finish' narrative without the actor ever having to name a preferred replacement.

**Effectiveness factors.** Works when (a) the actor has standing to author or interpret success criteria after the fact, (b) the criteria are framed in language that admits incremental tightening ('we also need to see…'), (c) the rival lacks a fixed-criteria contract or written charter to point back to, and (d) the audience's attention span is shorter than the cadence of criterion changes — so they remember the latest gap, not the chain of moved bars. Fails when the rival documents the original criteria publicly at acceptance, or when a neutral arbiter (audit, board, customer) holds the metric independent of the actor.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Sunk Cost Fallacy (Arkes & Blumer, 1985) combined with the Hedonic Treadmill effect and Goffman's frame control. The target has already paid the cost of producing against the old bar; abandoning the project to protest the new bar means writing off that investment. The new bar is set just far enough beyond the delivered work that pushing through 'one more cycle' looks rational, while the audience-facing frame is continuously refreshed so that no completed work ever stabilizes as a win.

**Decision Alteration.** The target's choice is reshaped from 'complete and collect the payoff' to 'protect the sunk investment by chasing the new bar.' Quitting feels like wasting the work already done; staying feels like the cheap option even as the cumulative cost mounts. The audience's choice, meanwhile, is reshaped from 'evaluate against a stable contract' to 'evaluate against whatever bar is currently being articulated' — which the evaluator controls.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when success criteria are verbal, ambiguous, or revisable; when the evaluator has unilateral or near-unilateral framing authority; when the audience does not retain a written record of original criteria; and when the target has high switching costs (career commitment, public association with the project, contract structure) that make abandonment expensive.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A PhD candidate in year six of a program where the advisor and committee privately want to extend the candidate's residency as cheap labor on a grant. Each draft addresses the prior round of comments; each new round introduces a new chapter requirement, a new dataset, or a new theoretical engagement.

**Sample X:** The committee continues to find the candidate's dissertation 'not quite ready' for defense

**Sample Y:** The committee certifies the dissertation as complete and schedules the defense

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The committee does not need to make 'not quite ready' more appealing — they only need it to remain the default reading.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. Withholding certification still costs the committee social capital with the candidate; the tactic does not reduce that cost, only justifies the withholding.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Each completed revision is reframed as 'a strong draft, but the field has moved on / we noticed a gap in chapter three' — the perceived completeness of the work is voided at the moment of delivery.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Certifying now would force the committee to defend a moving record of comments and would surrender a working researcher; the cost of saying yes rises with each new criterion they have introduced.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A regional bank operating under a multi-year consent decree from a federal regulator. The bank repeatedly remediates the findings of each examination cycle. Each subsequent examination introduces fresh expectations — new control testing, new documentation standards, new third-party attestations — that were not in the original decree but are framed as 'baseline modern practice.'

**Sample X:** Regulators conclude the firm has not yet demonstrated sufficient compliance with the consent decree

**Sample Y:** Regulators conclude the firm has substantially met the decree and lift the monitoring

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The regulator does not need to make continued enforcement more attractive in itself; it only needs to keep the bank classified as 'in remediation.'

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. Maintaining the consent decree still carries the regulator's own oversight cost; that cost is unchanged.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Each remediation milestone the bank hits is reframed as 'necessary but not sufficient' against the latest articulation of expectations, voiding the closure value of compliance.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Releasing the bank now would require the regulator to defend why earlier-articulated standards are now adequate; having raised the bar repeatedly, the political cost of declaring 'done' rises with each cycle.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any evaluator-target relationship where success criteria are revisable by one party and effort has already been sunk by the other. Documented in corporate performance reviews, regulatory consent decrees, academic gatekeeping, grant renewals, sales-quota redesigns, M&A due-diligence cycles, immigration adjudication, and product-acceptance testing. Less effective in fixed-criteria contractual settings (e.g., construction milestones with arbitration clauses) or in transparent metric environments (e.g., public scoreboards with locked formulas).

### Core Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- A manager who repeatedly tells a direct report they're 'close' to promotion but each cycle introduces a new competency requirement — leadership presence one year, strategic communication the next — keeping them perpetually one calibration shy of band-up.
- A defense procurement office that issues a contract with a fixed performance specification, then during integration testing announces additional cybersecurity, supply-chain, and sustainability requirements that the contractor must meet to be deemed mission-ready.
- A film studio that greenlights a director's project against a script and budget, then introduces successive notes about marketability, casting diversity, and runtime that prevent the picture from being formally locked even after the agreed cut is delivered.
- A venture-stage board that defines a Series B trigger as 'reach $10M ARR with positive unit economics,' then after the founder hits the number introduces logo concentration, gross-retention, and ICP-fit conditions before sanctioning the round.
- An immigration adjudicator processing a green-card application against published criteria who, on each renewed request, requests additional evidence (RFE) under broadening interpretations of 'extraordinary ability,' keeping the applicant in continuous remediation rather than approving or denying.
- A diocese reviewing a parish priest's request for promotion to a larger parish, who declines on the basis that 'we'd like to see one more year' of measurable engagement growth, then redefines engagement to include digital metrics, youth retention, and ecumenical outreach the priest could not have prepared for.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific (anti-rival) reading and the generic (evaluator-power) reading converge on the same lever signature — Y Degradation — but for different reasons. In the specific case, the actor is not trying to win, only to ensure the rival never finishes winning; voiding each completed milestone is sufficient. In the generic case, the evaluator is extracting continued effort from a committed target; voiding completion is how that extraction is sustained. Both lean on the audience's (or target's) inability to retain a stable original bar.

### Strategic Implications

When to use the specific lens: when diagnosing internal political maneuvering in a known rivalry, where the question is 'who benefits from this person never being read as done?' When to use the generic lens: when the same target is being held in a perpetual-deficit state by an asymmetric counterparty (regulator, committee, board, sponsor) and the diagnostic question is structural — who owns the criteria, and what fixes that authority? The specific lens is for naming the move; the generic lens is for engineering the countermeasure (written charters, third-party arbitration, locked criteria at acceptance).

### Resolution Approach

Note distinction from sibling tactic 0458 (DEFINITION OF DONE WARFARE): 0458 contests what 'done' means at the point of delivery — fighting over the interpretation of a single completion event. CRITERIA SHIFTING (0274) operates across a sequence — the rival is allowed to be 'done' on the old terms, but a new terms-set is introduced immediately after, so the win is converted into a stepping stone in a longer race the rival did not know they were running. 0458 is a debate; 0274 is a treadmill. The deepest insight: the tactic's power lives in the temporal seam between delivery and recognition — that brief window in which a completed milestone has not yet been ratified is when the actor inserts the new criterion. Practitioners defending against this should treat ratification of the original criteria, in writing and in front of the audience that will judge, as a non-negotiable acceptance event before work begins.
