# 0279 — THE INDUCED ERROR PROTOCOL

> Deliberately providing a targeted individual with incomplete data, faulty tools, or ambiguous instructions to manufacture a performance failure, justifying their removal or marginalization.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C43 — Decapitation, Bench Erosion & Targeted Purges

**Cited principles:** Sun Tzu, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a 'Protocol' suggesting a bounded HR/operational playbook (manipulating inputs to manufacture a documented performance failure), while the underlying mechanism — sabotaging an adversary's operating conditions so that their resulting failure does the disqualifying work for you — is an ancient strategic move visible from siege warfare to intelligence operations.

**Specific Interpretation.** Within a corporate or institutional setting, a manager or sponsor selectively withholds data, assigns broken or inadequate tools, or issues ambiguous instructions to a targeted employee, then uses the predictable failure as the documented basis for a performance improvement plan, reassignment, or termination.

**Generic Interpretation.** Across any competitive context, an actor with control over an adversary's inputs degrades those inputs so the adversary fails on apparently neutral metrics, allowing the actor to remove them on grounds that look like merit rather than politics.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The HR ritual (incomplete brief, faulty CRM, vague KPI) is the modern uniform on a much older mechanism: structuring the engagement so the target loses 'on their own,' converting a political removal into an evidentiary one. The mechanism is reusable wherever an actor controls another's operating conditions and needs a clean record for the firing.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Leadership and HR sign off on removing or demoting the targeted individual on documented performance grounds
- **Option Y:** Leadership protects the individual or treats the failures as systemic rather than personal
- **Context:** A manager wants a specific direct report (often a resistor, a politically inconvenient holdover, or a successor threat) out of the role, but lacks defensible cause and operates inside a culture that requires due-process documentation before termination.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make removal intrinsically more attractive on its merits — it does not surface new strategic value in firing the target, only manufactures the paper trail. The benefit of removal pre-exists; the tactic only legitimizes acting on it.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the central operating lever. By manufacturing documented failures (missed numbers, project breakdowns, customer complaints), the tactic supplies HR-grade evidence that strips the political, legal, and reputational cost out of firing the target. The same removal that would look vindictive on Monday becomes 'we had no choice' by Friday.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Defending the target becomes a less attractive position for peers and skip-levels once the failure record is on paper — keeping them looks like protecting a known underperformer rather than backing a competent colleague. The tactic quietly erodes the value of being their advocate.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Protecting the individual now carries direct cost: leadership must override documented evidence, the protector inherits accountability for any future failure, and HR/Legal flag the deviation as risk. The cost of inaction (keeping them) becomes visible in the same record that justifies action.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic does not need to argue that removal is good (no B(X)↑) — it only needs to make removal cheap and protection expensive. By engineering the failure rather than the case for firing, the perpetrator converts a political decision into an administrative one, where the documented record itself does the persuading.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on the perpetrator's control over the target's inputs (briefs, tools, access, instructions), plausible deniability that the failures were caused (not engineered), an organizational culture that trusts documented metrics over qualitative defense, and the absence of a strong sponsor who can reframe the failures as systemic. Fails when the target documents the input deficiencies in real time, when peers experiencing the same conditions succeed (revealing the targeting), or when an investigator notices the pattern of degraded inputs.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross) combined with Sun Tzu's principle of 'winning the battle before it is fought' — observers reliably attribute failure to the actor's character or competence rather than to the engineered conditions, and the engagement is structured so the target's defeat is already baked in before the visible contest begins.

**Decision Alteration.** Observers and decision-makers are presented with what appears to be a clean evidentiary record of the target's failure. Because attribution defaults to person rather than situation, they conclude the target deserves the consequence. The actor never has to argue for the removal — the manufactured record argues for it, and the actor merely executes on the 'obvious' conclusion others draw.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when the actor has covert control over the target's operating conditions, when failure is measured on visible outputs rather than examined inputs, when the institutional culture privileges documented outcomes over context, and when no investigative actor has both the standing and the incentive to audit the conditions that produced the failure.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A holding-company CEO wants a regional GM (who has independent board relationships) replaced with a loyalist. Over two quarters, the CEO selectively delays capital allocation approvals, reroutes the GM's best account managers to other regions, and changes the regional KPI definitions mid-year without recalibrating targets.

**Sample X:** The board approves removing a regional general manager for missing financial targets

**Sample Y:** The board keeps the regional GM and questions corporate's role in the underperformance

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The CEO does not argue that the loyalist would do better — that argument is unwinnable against the GM's prior record. The tactic deliberately bypasses making a positive case for replacement.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Two quarters of documented misses convert what would have been a political firing (board fight) into a numbers-driven dismissal that the board cannot easily contest without auditing the corporate-level inputs.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Board members who liked the GM personally face the prospect of being seen as protecting an underperformer, eroding the social value of their defense.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Keeping the GM after documented misses creates board-level fiduciary exposure — any future underperformance becomes the protector's responsibility, not just the GM's.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** The Prime Minister wants to oust a coalition-partner MP from a sensitive ministry without breaking the coalition. The PM's office routes incomplete intelligence briefings to the MP's ministry, slow-walks civil service appointments, and assigns the ministry an ambiguous mandate that overlaps two other portfolios. When the predictable policy failure surfaces, the PM 'reluctantly' accepts the MP's resignation.

**Sample X:** A coalition government MP loses their cabinet portfolio after a public policy failure

**Sample Y:** The MP retains the portfolio and blames coalition partners or civil servants

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The PM never publicly argues the MP should be removed — doing so would fracture the coalition. The benefit case is left entirely implicit; the failure is treated as self-disqualifying.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The visible policy failure makes removal a face-saving move rather than a coalition-breaking power play — the coalition partner cannot defend an MP whose ministry just produced a documented fiasco.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Defending the MP marks the coalition partner as out of touch with public accountability, eroding their own standing with the electorate.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Keeping the MP transfers ownership of the failure from the MP to the coalition partner leader, making future failures their political liability rather than the MP's.

### Versatility

**Level:** moderate

**Explanation.** Highly effective wherever an actor controls another's operating inputs and the surrounding culture grades on outputs rather than auditing inputs — corporate management, government cabinet politics, military command (assigning impossible objectives to remove a rival), academic departments (course assignments designed to drive enrolment failure). Less effective in transparent environments with strong forensic culture (regulated industries, courts, structured peer-review systems) where input quality is itself auditable, and inapplicable in flat or peer structures where no single actor controls the target's inputs.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: assigning a rival VP a stretch P&L with hidden cost commitments inherited from the prior leader, then citing the deteriorating margin as cause for replacement
- Government: routing critical intelligence around a politically inconvenient cabinet minister so their portfolio produces visible policy mistakes
- Sales: giving an underwanted senior account executive the worst territory, smallest support team, and lowest-quality lead list, then citing missed quota at the next review
- Military: assigning a promotion-threatening officer to a command with insufficient logistics or hopeless objectives, allowing the operational failure to terminate their career advancement
- Academic: a department chair allocating a tenure-track rival the highest-load teaching schedule and least-promising graduate students, then citing weak research output and student evaluations at tenure review
- Family business: a founder-CEO setting up a child-successor's pilot division with ambiguous reporting lines and conflicting board mandates, allowing the resulting confusion to justify reverting to the founder's preferred successor

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific HR-protocol reading and the generic attribution-engineering reading converge on the same strategy classification (Resistance Triple, 14) because both describe a tactic that operates by making removal cheap and defense expensive rather than by promoting the merits of removal. The tactic's signature is the deliberate absence of B(X)↑: the actor never argues for the firing because the manufactured record argues for it. This makes it cluster with — but distinct from — REPUTATION ASSASSINATION (which degrades who the target IS) and COMPETENCE DEMOLITION (which directly attacks demonstrated competence). The Induced Error Protocol attacks neither identity nor demonstrated competence; it attacks the conditions under which competence would be visible.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's strategic power lies in its evidentiary cleanliness. Unlike defamation or open political attack, it generates documents the perpetrator can point to without ever speaking the political motive aloud. This makes it the preferred removal vehicle when due-process exposure (litigation, board scrutiny, regulatory review) is high. The corresponding fragility is the input record: every degraded brief, every misrouted resource, every ambiguous instruction is itself documentary evidence in the opposite direction — which is why competent targets who suspect they are being set up shift their effort from output performance to input documentation. When to use the specific lens: Use the specific lens when the practitioner needs to recognize the protocol unfolding in real time — the early indicators (briefs that arrive late or incomplete, tools that mysteriously break, KPIs that shift mid-period, peers in similar roles operating under different conditions) are operationally specific and require pattern-recognition at the HR/operational level. When to use the generic lens: Use the generic lens when analyzing removal patterns across contexts (why does this CEO's exits cluster in the same way the prior administration's cabinet removals did?) or when designing institutional defenses — the attribution-engineering mechanism is the same regardless of the surface uniform, so input-audit mechanisms designed for corporate HR can be ported to ministerial accountability or academic tenure review.

### Resolution Approach

The Induced Error Protocol teaches that the most defensible removals are the ones where the perpetrator never visibly attacks — they only structure the engagement so the target's defeat becomes a fact rather than a claim. Practitioners on both sides should remember that the contest is decided at the input layer, not the output layer: by the time the failure shows up in performance reviews, the game is already over. The countermove is not to perform harder against degraded conditions but to make the conditions themselves visible while they can still be corrected.
