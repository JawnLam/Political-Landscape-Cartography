# 0600 — THE ATTRITION BY ERROR CAMPAIGN

> Undermining a rival initiative through a series of small, seemingly accidental administrative errors, minor delays, and 'misunderstandings' that cumulatively cause significant damage without any single traceable act of sabotage.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C63 — Active Sabotage, Pipeline Poisoning & False-Flag Operations

**Cited principles:** Williamson, Darley, Latané

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a specific organizational mode (administrative errors, delays, 'misunderstandings' inside a process pipeline) but the underlying mechanism — degrading a rival's position through a distributed series of individually-deniable micro-acts — is a timeless pattern of low-intensity conflict visible in bureaucratic obstruction, regulatory friction, supply-chain interference, and historical 'work-to-rule' actions.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads as an internal bureaucratic obstruction campaign against a specific rival project — operations, finance, IT, or compliance functions slow-walking the rival's tickets, paperwork, and clarifications until the project's schedule, budget, and reputation collapse without any clean fingerprints.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads as the universal pattern of degradation-via-distributed-friction: keep each act below the threshold at which it could be challenged, but accumulate the costs until the target is no longer viable. The signature is that the damage is real but the attribution is impossible.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The administrative-error vocabulary is one expression, but the deeper mechanism — staying under the accountability threshold by atomizing harm into noise-floor increments — generalizes wherever a target can be slowed through legitimate channels and where the cost of investigating each incident exceeds the cost of absorbing it. The corporate clerical version is a special case of a much older pattern.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Other stakeholders withdraw active support, resources, and political capital from the rival initiative and let it die of its own accumulating problems
- **Option Y:** Stakeholders continue backing the rival initiative, absorbing the mounting delays and rework, and defend its track record
- **Context:** A rival initiative is moving through a multi-function organization (finance approvals, procurement, IT provisioning, compliance sign-offs, HR requisitions). The actor cannot openly oppose it but has direct or indirect control — via allies in those functions — over the small-grained administrative decisions on which the initiative depends

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does nothing to make withdrawing support more attractive in any positive sense. It does not offer alternative initiatives, rewards for defection, or upside framing for stepping back. Its entire operating logic is degrading Y, not promoting X — the actor wants the rival to be abandoned, not replaced with a celebrated alternative.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Stepping away from the rival initiative is not made easier by this tactic — sponsors who want to stay loyal face exactly the same social and political costs of abandonment as before. The tactic operates entirely on the Y side of the ledger.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

As the initiative accumulates delays, rework, and 'mysterious' problems, its reputational benefit erodes — it becomes 'the project that's always behind,' 'the one that can't get anything right.' Sponsors who staked credibility on backing it now find the credibility return diminishing. The benefit of Y degrades through nothing the rival did wrong, but through the cumulative noise around it.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the core operating lever. Every miscoded purchase order, every 'oh sorry, we used the old spec,' every approval that takes three weeks instead of three days, every meeting that has to be rescheduled because the invite went to the wrong distribution list — each is a small real cost (rework, schedule slip, opportunity cost, manager attention) that is individually within the normal organizational error rate and therefore unchallengeable. Aggregated, they impose a crushing tax on the rival initiative.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic produces the desired choice shift not by giving sponsors a reason to back something else, but by making continued sponsorship progressively unaffordable in attention, schedule, and reputational terms. Because every individual act is plausibly an honest mistake, no defender can mount a counter-attack — they have nothing to point at. The rival initiative is starved of momentum and dies as a 'troubled project,' which is exactly the frame the actor wants for it.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires distributed allies or sympathetic actors across the functions the rival depends on (finance, IT, procurement, legal, HR); requires that the normal organizational error rate be high enough that each act stays under the detection threshold; requires patience — the tactic only works on timescales of months. Fails when the rival sponsor is senior enough to demand a root-cause investigation, when error logs are centralized and visible, or when one actor lacks discipline and produces an obvious 'tell' that breaks the plausible-deniability frame.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Williamson's Transaction Cost Economics combined with Diffusion of Responsibility (Latané & Darley). Each act is engineered to fall below the threshold at which the cost of investigating it would exceed the cost of absorbing it; meanwhile, because the harms are distributed across many actors and functions, no individual is accountable for the aggregate damage. The mechanism exploits the universal organizational fact that friction is normal — and that defenders cannot investigate everything that goes wrong.

**Decision Alteration.** Shifts the target's choice not by changing what they want, but by making the path to what they want continuously more expensive in real terms (time, money, attention, political capital). Because the costs accumulate from below the accountability radar, the target experiences the degradation as ambient bad luck or systemic dysfunction rather than as a coordinated attack — which means they cannot fight back, only absorb. Eventually the cost of continuing exceeds the value of finishing, and they walk away believing the project was 'just unlucky' or 'cursed.'

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best in large, multi-function organizations or distributed systems where (a) the target depends on many gatekeepers for routine approvals, (b) a meaningful baseline error rate exists so additional errors do not stand out, (c) the actor has either direct control or quiet allies in the critical functions, and (d) defenders lack the time or political authority to demand a systemic audit of why this one project always has problems.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A retail chain's national procurement function, allied to a rival regional VP, miscodes the pilot's reorder forms, sends fixtures to the wrong stores, books logistics two weeks late, and 'forgets' to include the pilot in regional sales-flash distribution. Each error is filed with apologies and corrected; the pilot's same-store-sales numbers nonetheless underperform expectations and the regional manager's case for expansion quietly collapses

**Sample X:** Senior allies of a regional manager quietly let her flagship store-format pilot fail by withdrawing further capital commitments after the third quarter of 'operational issues'

**Sample Y:** Senior allies continue defending and resourcing the regional manager's pilot through the eighteen-month evaluation window despite mounting friction

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The tactic does not promote a replacement format or sweeten the experience of withdrawing — defection from the pilot earns no positive narrative.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. Senior allies who want to keep backing the pilot are not given any easier off-ramp; they simply face mounting evidence that it 'isn't working.'

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The pilot's reputational benefit (proof that the regional manager can execute a strategic format play) is eroded by the accumulation of operational 'problems,' independent of the underlying retail concept's merits.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each miscoded order, late shipment, and missed reporting cycle imposes a real cost — lost sales days, manager attention burned on cleanup, finance write-offs — that taxes the pilot's P&L and makes continued sponsorship more expensive.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A regulated industry coalition has placed sympathetic appointees and supportive contractors throughout the supporting infrastructure the agency depends on: the state IT services group runs its case-management upgrades behind schedule, the central HR office takes nine months to fill enforcement vacancies, the attorney general's office assigns inexperienced staff to its referrals. The agency's case rate drops, its conviction rate slips, its budget defenders run out of ammunition. No single act is corrupt; the agency simply 'can't seem to get its act together'

**Sample X:** The state legislature defunds an environmental enforcement agency after a sustained pattern of 'procedural irregularities' damages its case rate

**Sample Y:** The legislature continues funding the agency at its established level given its formal mandate

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. Defunding the agency is not made attractive in itself — there is no celebration of the alternative regulatory regime.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. Legislators who want to defend the agency face the same political costs as before — they just have less to defend with.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The agency's value proposition to its political defenders (high-profile wins, deterrent effect) erodes as its case statistics decline through no visible fault of its leadership.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Every delayed IT upgrade, every unfilled vacancy, every misassigned referral imposes accumulated drag on enforcement output, making the cost of continued political sponsorship — measured in legislative attention and credibility — progressively higher.

### Versatility

**Level:** moderate

**Explanation.** Highly effective in any environment with (a) routine baseline friction high enough to camouflage additional errors, (b) multi-function dependencies the target cannot route around, and (c) distributed allies or sympathetic gatekeepers in those functions. Less effective in small, transparent organizations where every administrative act is visible to the affected sponsor, in systems with centralized error-tracking and post-mortem cultures, or under sponsors senior enough to demand a coordinated audit. Versatility is therefore real but bounded — it requires structural cover that does not exist everywhere.

### Application Examples

- A corporate procurement function, allied to a rival division head, repeatedly 'misunderstands' the spec for a competing product line, ordering wrong components and triggering rework that bleeds the launch's margin
- A government agency's legal department, sympathetic to industry concerns, takes the maximum statutory time on every regulatory drafting review for an enforcement initiative its director opposes, killing the rulemaking schedule through procedural correctness
- A university registrar's office, controlled by faculty hostile to a new interdisciplinary program, repeatedly mis-routes student enrollments, scheduling clashes, and grade submissions until the program's student-experience metrics deteriorate to the point of cancellation
- A military supply chain officer, loyal to a rival commander, miscodes ammunition shipments to a deployed unit, producing nuisance shortages that erode the unit's operational reputation without ever crossing into prosecutable negligence
- A diplomatic mission's consular section quietly slow-walks visa processing for a rival country's trade delegation through accumulated 'documentation issues' until the planned commercial summit loses momentum and is postponed indefinitely
- A non-profit's grant management office, captured by a rival board faction, routinely files reimbursement requests against the executive director's pet program just late enough to trigger funder concerns, gradually eroding the program's funding pipeline

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The signature of this tactic is the asymmetry between damage and attributability. The harm is real, measurable, and cumulative; the attribution surface is zero. Each act is individually defensible as 'normal organizational friction,' so no defender can find a target to fight. This distinguishes Attrition by Error from its cluster siblings sharply: THE FALSE FLAG OPERATION manufactures a visible act and misattributes it; THE 'GOLD PLATING' SABOTAGE adds visible scope to a rival's project; THE STALL AND STEAL stretches one decision while preparing a substitute. Attrition by Error refuses to produce any single act worth pointing at — its entire signature is the absence of a signature. It is closer to entropy than to attack.

### Strategic Implications

Practitioners should recognize that this tactic is structurally invisible to defenders who pattern-match on dramatic moves. The defense is not to litigate individual incidents (which is the trap) but to instrument the system: centralized error tracking by project, root-cause analysis across functions, and political escalation framed around the aggregate cost-rate rather than any specific incident. Once a sponsor can credibly point to '47 administrative errors in 6 months against this one initiative versus a baseline of 8,' the plausibility-deniability frame collapses and the campaign becomes vulnerable to counter-investigation. Conversely, for an actor running such a campaign, the discipline is to keep the rate just barely above baseline — high enough to do damage, low enough that no audit can statistically isolate the targeting.

### Resolution Approach

Classify as 'specific' when analyzing a bounded bureaucratic obstruction inside an identifiable organization, where the levers operate through identifiable administrative functions and the rival sponsor is named. Classify as 'generic' when the lens is the broader pattern of distributed-friction conflict — visible historically in work-to-rule labor actions, regulatory capture-by-friction, supply-chain harassment, and any setting where degradation can be smuggled in below the accountability threshold. Both readings yield the same Y Degradation strategy, but the specific lens helps practitioners plan defense, while the generic lens helps them recognize the pattern across domains where the vocabulary differs but the mechanism is identical.
