# 0550 — THE 'COMPETENCE DEMOLITION'

> Systematically assigning tasks outside an individual's expertise and then documenting their failures to build a case for their removal or demotion.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C43 — Decapitation, Bench Erosion & Targeted Purges

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description anchors in a specific HR-mechanics sequence (assign-then-document) typically deployed against a named individual inside a particular organization, yet the underlying mechanism — engineering observable failure to manufacture a record that converts a subjective dislike into an objective-looking case — is a reusable pattern of evidence fabrication through situational engineering.

**Specific Interpretation.** A manager or sponsor who wants a particular subordinate removed but lacks legitimate cause systematically rotates them through assignments outside their demonstrated skill set, then files the resulting underperformance into the personnel record as proof of unfitness for the role they actually held competently.

**Generic Interpretation.** An influencer manufactures the evidentiary basis for a desired adverse decision by controlling task-assignment in ways that pre-determine observable failure, exploiting observers' tendency to read failure as dispositional rather than situational.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the tactic is most visible in HR-driven removals, the operational core — controlling the assignment so the failure produces itself, then letting observers' attribution biases do the political work — applies wherever an actor controls task allocation against a target. It surfaces identically in prosecutorial overcharging, in academic committee assignments designed to bury rivals in service work, in military postings to no-win commands, and in NGO board work used to discredit founders. The mechanism is reusable; the HR context is one venue.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Senior leadership and HR approve the removal or demotion of the targeted employee, citing the documented record of failed assignments
- **Option Y:** Senior leadership retains the employee in their current role, treating the assignment pattern as a development problem rather than a competence problem
- **Context:** A mid-to-senior manager wants a tenured, well-networked subordinate out — often because the subordinate is a political rival, a holdover from a prior regime, or someone whose continued presence constrains the manager's reorganization plans — but lacks the standing or evidence to remove them through direct means under existing HR policy

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make removal itself more appealing on its merits; it does not argue that the role would be better filled by a specific replacement or that organizational performance would visibly improve. The benefit side of removal is left to the decision-maker's own calculus. The whole apparatus operates on the alternative side — making retention indefensible — not on making removal attractive.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The accumulated paper trail substantially reduces the political and procedural cost of acting against the target. HR-driven removals require defensible cause; the documented failures convert what would have been a contested, capital-spending decision ('we want them out') into a routine performance-management decision ('the record speaks for itself'). The decision-maker gets to act without owning the choice.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The core operating lever. Each documented failure systematically dismantles the perceived value of retaining the employee — their prior reputation, their institutional knowledge, their network, their tenure all become discounted against the visible record of recent underperformance. The tactic does not argue against the person; it builds a file that argues against them, and the file outlasts memory of context.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Retention becomes politically costly because doing nothing now looks like complicity with documented incompetence. Decision-makers who keep the employee become personally exposed if anything else goes wrong — they were warned, the file shows it. The tactic weaponizes the documentation chain itself: ignoring the paper trail is a fireable offense for the person ignoring it.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The orchestrator deliberately avoids amplifying X's benefit — selling the removal directly would invite scrutiny of motives and surface the political nature of the move. Instead, the tactic operates entirely on degrading the retention case (B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑) while quietly lowering the procedural cost of acting (C(X)↓) by pre-loading the case file. The result is a decision that appears forced by evidence rather than chosen by anyone — the orchestrator's fingerprints are on the assignments, not on the removal.

**Effectiveness factors.** Depends on (a) the orchestrator having unilateral or near-unilateral authority over the target's task assignments, (b) an HR or governance regime that treats documented performance as quasi-objective evidence, (c) observers who lack visibility into the assignment-to-skill mismatch and therefore read failures as dispositional, (d) sufficient time horizon to accumulate a pattern before the target catches on, and (e) the target's inability to credibly refuse assignments or escalate the mismatch without appearing insubordinate. Fails when the target has independent access to skip-level sponsors, when assignment rationales come under scrutiny by an outside party, or when the target documents the mismatch in real time and forces the record to include the setup.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross) combined with Goffman's frame control. Observers systematically over-attribute failure to dispositional traits ('they couldn't handle it') and under-attribute to situational engineering ('they were set up to fail'). The orchestrator exploits this asymmetry by controlling the situational variables invisibly while making the dispositional reading the only frame available to decision-makers. Confirmation bias compounds the effect — once a 'struggling' narrative is established by the first documented failure, subsequent failures are read as pattern-confirmation rather than as the predictable output of continued mismatch.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic alters decisions not by changing what decision-makers want but by changing what they appear forced to do. It manufactures the evidentiary record that converts a subjective preference into an obligatory-looking action. Decision-makers who would have hesitated to act on the orchestrator's bare preference act readily on the file the orchestrator has built — they are responding to evidence, not to politics, and that framing distance protects them from the social and institutional cost of the decision.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best in organizations with formal performance-documentation regimes (annual reviews, PIPs, written assignments), with hierarchical task-allocation authority concentrated in the orchestrator, with weak skip-level access for targets, and with decision-makers who value the appearance of process-driven decisions. Requires time — the tactic is patient by design and fails if rushed into a single setup-and-strike.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A newly-appointed agency director inherits a deputy from the prior administration whose policy views and external network make direct dismissal politically expensive. The director progressively reassigns the deputy from their established regulatory portfolio to lead unfamiliar technology-modernization and inter-agency budget initiatives, then files each missed milestone and stakeholder complaint into the personnel record.

**Sample X:** A federal agency director removes a long-tenured deputy by citing a year of documented failed assignments in policy domains the deputy had no prior background in

**Sample Y:** The director retains the deputy and continues to deal with their inherited authority and policy disagreements

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The director never argues publicly that removing the deputy will produce better policy outcomes; doing so would invite scrutiny of policy motives. The case rests entirely on the deputy's record, not on the merits of removal.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The accumulated personnel file converts what would have been a politically explosive dismissal of a holdover into a process-conformant performance-based action that career HR and Inspector General review can sign off on without political controversy.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The deputy's deep regulatory expertise, institutional memory, and stakeholder relationships are systematically devalued in the record because the new assignments produce no opportunities to demonstrate them; the file shows a deputy who 'couldn't deliver,' not a deputy whose deliverables were redirected away from their strengths.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Retaining the deputy after a documented year of missed milestones would expose the director to Inspector General questions and Congressional oversight critique — 'why was a non-performing senior career official kept in role?' — making the cost of inaction higher than the cost of action.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** An ambitious dean cannot remove a tenured full professor whose published critiques of the dean's reform agenda carry weight with the faculty senate. Over three years, the dean appoints the professor to chair an accreditation self-study, then a multi-departmental ethics review committee, then an inter-institutional consortium working group — none of which intersect with the professor's research expertise. The dean's annual reviews note slipped deadlines, missed deliverables, and stakeholder complaints, building a record that frames the professor as 'in decline' and clears the path for negotiated early retirement.

**Sample X:** A university dean engineers the early retirement of a tenured professor by loading them with administrative service assignments outside their disciplinary expertise

**Sample Y:** The professor remains on faculty with their tenured protections and continues to publicly critique the dean's curriculum reforms

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. The dean never argues that the professor's departure will improve the department; tenure norms make any such argument socially costly. The pressure is built entirely on the negative record.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — An early-retirement negotiation backed by three years of documented administrative underperformance becomes a routine HR transaction rather than a politically explosive tenure fight that would mobilize the faculty senate against the dean.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The professor's scholarly reputation, citation record, and influence with colleagues are progressively eclipsed in institutional perception by their visible failure to manage the assigned committees — colleagues who once respected them increasingly see someone 'past their peak,' devaluing the cost of losing them.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The accumulated administrative-failure record provides ammunition to anyone who might want to challenge the professor's standing on a future tenure committee, departmental governance role, or grant review — keeping them on faculty without acting on the record becomes a slow leak of institutional credibility for the department itself.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any hierarchical setting where (a) one party controls task assignment for another, (b) performance gets documented in a way that outlasts memory of the assignment context, and (c) decision-makers value the appearance of evidence-driven action. This includes corporate management chains, federal civil service, university faculty governance, military command postings, professional services partner tracks, hospital department politics, and religious-order administration. Less effective in flat organizations, gig/contract relationships where the target can simply decline mismatched work, and contexts where peer review of assignments is structural (e.g., union shops with grievance procedures over task assignment).

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A division SVP rotates a politically inconvenient director through three consecutive turnaround assignments in unfamiliar product lines, building a record that supports a 'leadership effectiveness' downgrade at the next talent review.
- Government: A new agency administrator details a career senior executive from their longtime policy domain into emergency-management coordination roles, then cites their unfamiliarity-driven stumbles as basis for a non-disciplinary reassignment under SES rules.
- Academic: A department chair assigns a rival full professor to chair the curriculum review, the assessment committee, and the search committee simultaneously, then documents the resulting deliverable slippage as evidence of declining academic citizenship.
- Military: A combatant commander posts a problematic but well-connected colonel to a series of joint-task assignments outside their service specialty, building a record of underwhelming fitness reports that supports passing them over for brigadier general selection.
- Professional services: A managing partner steers an unwanted senior associate onto deals in unfamiliar industry verticals where they lack the relationship base, then cites their inability to originate work as the basis for a non-partnership track decision.
- NGO / nonprofit: An executive director assigns a founder-board-member to lead a major-gifts fundraising campaign outside their program expertise, documenting the campaign's shortfall to support a 'governance refresh' that retires them from the board.
- Religious institutions: A bishop assigns a theologically dissenting senior priest to a series of administrative chancery roles requiring canon-law expertise they lack, building a record that justifies a quiet reassignment to a remote parish.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both the specific HR-mechanics reading and the generic situational-engineering reading converge on the same lever signature (C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑, Resistance Triple) because the tactic's identity is precisely its refusal to operate on the B(X)↑ lever. The orchestrator never argues for the removal on its merits — that would expose intent. The tactic's power lies in making the case look like it built itself. This is what separates COMPETENCE DEMOLITION from THE INDUCED ERROR PROTOCOL (0279) in the same cluster: the induced error is a single staged failure used as catalyst, while competence demolition is a slow, cumulative, file-building campaign whose patience is itself the disguise. It also distinguishes from THE PROXY DEMOTION (0225), which displaces through structural reorganization; here, the target remains formally in role while the record under them is hollowed out.

### Strategic Implications

For the orchestrator, the tactic is high-yield because it externalizes responsibility — the documented failures, not the orchestrator's preferences, drive the decision, providing political cover for everyone in the decision chain. For the target, the tactic is uniquely difficult to counter because each individual assignment is defensible in isolation ('we need someone senior on this'); only the pattern reveals intent, and by the time the pattern is visible the record is built. The counter-tactic is to refuse the frame in real time: log each mismatched assignment with a written note about skill-fit at the time of assignment, copying skip-level sponsors, so that any future invocation of the resulting record must address the contemporaneous mismatch documentation. The tactic also has a characteristic failure mode for the orchestrator — if the assignment pattern is ever surfaced in a hostile context (litigation, audit, journalist), the deliberateness of the mismatch becomes evidence of bad faith rather than evidence of poor performance, inverting the entire record.

### Resolution Approach

Apply the specific lens when analyzing an actual ongoing removal campaign — focuses attention on the assignment-by-assignment sequence, the HR documentation mechanics, and the target's available counter-moves. Apply the generic lens when teaching the underlying pattern, when scanning for analogous moves in non-HR contexts (prosecutorial charging strategies, committee assignments in legislatures, posting decisions in officer corps), or when designing organizational safeguards — peer review of cross-domain assignments, mandatory skill-fit memos, mandatory exit interviews that ask about assignment history — that target the situational-engineering mechanism rather than any single documented incident.

## Competence Demolition Dynamics

**Mechanism Signature.** The defining move is the deliberate, repeatable assignment of work outside demonstrated competence, paired with the same documentation regime used for legitimate performance management. The two halves are required together: assignment without documentation is just bad management; documentation without engineered assignment is normal performance review.

**Escalation Pattern.** First-stage assignments are typically defensible-looking stretch opportunities ('this will broaden you'). Second-stage assignments compound the mismatch by adding scope or stakes. Third-stage assignments are the public-failure assignments — visible to skip-level leadership, with measurable deliverables — which produce the file's headline entries.

**Counter Measures.** Real-time skill-fit memos at the moment of assignment, copied to skip-level sponsors; written requests for paired support (subject-matter experts on the team) that, if denied, become part of the record; explicit declination of assignments framed as 'I can take this if X, Y, Z; I cannot take this without them' — converting a future failure into a foreseen risk the assigner accepted.

**Exit Criteria.** From the orchestrator's perspective, the campaign concludes when the file is thick enough that an independent reviewer (HR, an Inspector General, a board governance committee) would sign off on adverse action without further inquiry — typically 12-36 months of accumulated documentation across 3-6 mismatched assignments.
