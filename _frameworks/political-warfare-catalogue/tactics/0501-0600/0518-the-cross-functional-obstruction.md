# 0518 — THE 'CROSS-FUNCTIONAL' OBSTRUCTION

> Creating cross-functional teams with deliberately conflicting mandates, ensuring they become paralyzed by infighting and fail to challenge the existing power structure.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C55 — Gatekeeping & Access Control

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Olson, Pfeffer, Machiavelli

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a specific organizational form ('cross-functional team') and a specific intent ('protect the existing power structure'), but the underlying mechanism — deliberately engineering structural conflict inside a group so that it cannot act coherently against the convenor — is a generalizable defensive move that predates and outlives the cross-functional team artifact.

**Specific Interpretation.** An incumbent executive or coalition chartering a cross-functional working group, task force, or steering committee whose members are drawn from departments with structurally opposed mandates (e.g., Sales vs. Risk, Engineering vs. Compliance, Growth vs. Finance), staffed so that no single member can produce a unilateral recommendation and so that any consensus would require concessions each member's home department forbids — guaranteeing that the team's output is either deadlocked, watered-down, or perpetually 'still in deliberation.'

**Generic Interpretation.** A defensive divide-and-rule move in which an incumbent power deliberately constructs (or co-opts) a multi-actor body charged with reviewing or constraining that power, but staffs and mandates it such that its members' incentives are mutually exclusive. The body absorbs the political legitimacy of 'collective review' while being structurally incapable of producing collective action against its convenor.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The cross-functional team is a 2000s-era management artifact, but the move — engineering paralysis-by-conflict in a body that nominally exists to constrain you — is ancient (Roman tribunate vetoes, the U.S. Senate filibuster, Cabinet 'collective responsibility' procedures, UN Security Council vetoes among rivals). The mechanism is Machiavellian divide-and-rule applied prophylactically inside one's own institution, and reads more cleanly at the generic level.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The existing power structure remains unchallenged because the cross-functional team produces no coherent recommendation
- **Option Y:** The cross-functional team produces a unified recommendation that challenges, constrains, or reorganizes the existing power structure
- **Context:** A senior executive (or executive coalition) faces pressure to convene a review body — often demanded by the board, regulators, a new CEO, or a high-profile incident — that could plausibly recommend curbs on the executive's authority, budget, or scope. Instead of resisting the review (which would look defensive), the executive proposes a cross-functional team and personally curates its membership and charter.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make the existing power structure more attractive on the merits. It does not advance a positive case for the incumbent arrangement, run a PR campaign for it, or add new benefits to it. The incumbent simply survives by attrition of the challenge — its appeal is unchanged. The absence of this lever is part of the tactic's signature: it is a defensive move, not a sales move.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Maintaining the status quo carries the same operational cost as before. The tactic does not reduce the cost of incumbency — it does not, for example, deliver any concessions, restructuring, or efficiency gains that would make the existing arrangement cheaper to defend. The status quo is preserved by sabotaging the alternative, not by making itself easier to sustain.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

A cross-functional team that produces nothing — or produces a milquetoast lowest-common-denominator document — visibly reduces the perceived benefit of the entire 'reform the power structure' project. Stakeholders who hoped for a substantive recommendation see only deadlock, and conclude (incorrectly but conveniently) that 'reform is hard,' 'the issues are complex,' or 'the experts couldn't agree' — diminishing future appetite for the challenge.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. The tactic raises the cost of mounting a coherent challenge by guaranteeing internal warfare within the very body chartered to mount it. Each member's home department punishes concessions to rival departments; producing a unified recommendation requires every member to accept reputational and political damage at home; the team's meetings burn calendar weeks per participant; the chair must referee fights that turn personal. The cost of producing Y rises from 'agree on a document' to 'agree on a document that nobody's home department will let them sign.'

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The choice shift is produced not by promoting the status quo but by making the only available reform mechanism — the cross-functional team — incapable of generating output. By staffing the body with members whose mandates conflict structurally, the convenor ensures that the cost of producing a unified recommendation exceeds any plausible benefit to the individual members, and that the resulting deadlock degrades the perceived value of the entire reform exercise. The status quo wins by default while the convenor wears the badge of 'we tried — we even chartered a cross-functional team.'

**Effectiveness factors.** Works best when: (1) the convenor has unilateral authority over team composition and charter scope; (2) the participating departments have genuine, well-documented mandate conflicts so the deadlock looks organic rather than engineered; (3) there is no external timeline forcing a decision (a regulator with a hard deadline, a board with a quarterly review date, or a crisis with an obvious clock breaks the tactic); (4) the convenor can credibly position themselves as 'above' the deadlock — usually by chairing or by appointing a chair too junior to force consensus. Common failure modes: a strong external chair imposed by the board; a single team member willing to write a dissenting report and leak it; a regulator who demands deliverables rather than process.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Olson's Logic of Collective Action combined with Schelling's coordination problem — when a group's members have heterogeneous and mutually-exclusive payoff structures, they cannot produce a collective good (here, a unified recommendation) even when each member individually wants one. Layered on top is Machiavelli's divide et impera as articulated by Pfeffer in organizational power theory: an incumbent's most durable defense is not to suppress challenge but to ensure that any body authorized to mount a challenge is internally fractured along pre-existing fault lines.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not directly persuade any actor to prefer the status quo. Instead, it raises the transaction cost of producing the alternative so high that the alternative never coheres into a proposable object. Stakeholders who would have supported a clear reform proposal never get one to support — they get a process that is visibly underway and visibly inconclusive, and over time their attention migrates elsewhere. The convenor wins by default, having never had to argue the merits.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when (a) the incumbent has charter-design authority over the review body, (b) the relevant constituencies have genuine and observable mandate conflicts that make the deadlock plausibly organic, (c) the review body's success criteria are vague ('explore options,' 'develop recommendations') rather than specific ('deliver a binding decision by Q3'), and (d) the meta-process lacks a forcing function (no deadline, no automatic escalation if the body fails to report).

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A Defense Secretary, under congressional pressure to address inter-service redundancy in a major aviation program, charters a joint review board with equal representation from Air Force, Navy, Marines, and Army — knowing that each service's core doctrine on the platform's role is irreconcilable with the others, and that any joint recommendation would require one service to accept demotion of its mission relative to a rival.

**Sample X:** The Pentagon's procurement authority over a specific weapons platform remains with the incumbent service branch

**Sample Y:** A joint inter-service review board recommends transferring procurement authority to a unified joint command

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The incumbent service's claim to the platform is not strengthened on the merits; its position is preserved by the board's failure to recommend an alternative, not by any new argument for the status quo.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The cost of running redundant procurement programs across services is unchanged — indeed it persists, which is precisely the inefficiency the review was meant to address.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — When the joint board issues a 35-page report of 'considerations for future study,' the apparent benefit of unification — the simplicity and savings that motivated the review — looks chimeric, and congressional appetite for forcing the issue erodes.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each service representative knows that recommending transfer to a rival service would end their own promotion track. The personal political cost of producing any joint recommendation that disadvantages one's home service is career-terminating, so no such recommendation is produced.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** The current-generation patriarch, facing pressure from younger cousins demanding modernization, agrees to form a family governance committee with representation from each of the four branches of the founding family — knowing that the branches have decades-old disputes over the original inheritance distribution, that any recommendation favoring 'professionalization' would require one branch to cede a board seat to a non-family executive, and that no branch will accept that demotion before the others.

**Sample X:** A founding family retains operational control of a third-generation private company

**Sample Y:** A family governance committee recommends professional outside management for day-to-day operations

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The patriarch's operational control is not framed as objectively better; he is not running a campaign for his own competence. His position survives because the committee cannot agree on a replacement model.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The costs of family-only management — succession risk, governance gaps, talent ceiling — are not reduced. They persist as the visible price of the deadlock, but the patriarch absorbs them rather than paying any reform cost.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — After eighteen months of inconclusive meetings, the cousins who pushed for 'professionalization' come to see the entire reform agenda as a quagmire — the apparent benefit of outside management is buried under accumulated inter-branch grievance, and the appetite for pushing it again wanes.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any branch that endorses outside management before extracting concessions from the rival branches loses its negotiating leverage forever. The cost of being the first mover is locking in inferior terms for one's own descendants, so every branch waits — and the committee never moves.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable in any institutional context where (a) an incumbent has authority to design or shape the membership and charter of a body chartered to review their position, (b) the candidate members come from constituencies with pre-existing structural conflicts, and (c) the body's deliverable can plausibly be 'recommendations' rather than 'decisions.' Found in corporate boards (committees designed to deadlock), legislatures (committees of jurisdiction designed to bury bills), international bodies (UN Security Council vetoes among rival permanent members), federations (inter-service joint commands), regulatory bodies (multi-stakeholder advisory committees), and family enterprises (governance councils across rival branches). Less effective in unitary command structures where no review body exists, or where an outside party (judge, regulator, activist investor) can impose a chair and a deadline.

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A CEO under board pressure to review compensation governance charters a committee composed of the heads of HR, Finance, Legal, and Compliance — knowing each function's stake in compensation policy is irreconcilable, so no unified recommendation emerges and existing pay practices persist.
- Government: A legislative leader refers a popular but inconvenient bill to a joint committee with representatives from four committees of jurisdiction (Ways and Means, Energy, Commerce, Foreign Affairs), each with veto power over the others' priorities, ensuring the bill never emerges in markup.
- Diplomacy: A great power encourages the creation of a multilateral coordination body that includes its principal rivals as voting members, knowing that the rivals' irreconcilable interests will block any binding action critical of the convenor.
- Academic: A university president, asked to address grade inflation, charters a faculty senate committee with representatives from STEM, humanities, professional schools, and arts — each with diametrically opposed grading philosophies — and accepts the resulting three-year deadlock as 'the faculty's prerogative.'
- Family business: A controlling shareholder agrees to a governance review committee with representation from each family branch knowing the branches' historical disputes will prevent any recommendation displacing him.
- NGO/activism: A targeted corporation invites all of its critics — labor, environmental, indigenous-rights, consumer-protection groups — into a single 'stakeholder dialogue' whose internal disagreements over priorities ensure no unified demand reaches the company's leadership.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both readings reveal the same operational signature: the tactic works not by defending the status quo but by ensuring the challenge never coheres. The specific lens (cross-functional teams) and the generic lens (engineered structural deadlock as defensive divide-and-rule) converge on Strategy 6 (Y Degradation) — both raise the cost of producing the alternative and degrade its perceived benefit, but neither acts on the X side at all. This is the inverse signature of most influence tactics in this catalogue: where most tactics sell something, this tactic sells nothing — it preserves position by sterilizing the only available challenge mechanism. Within Cluster C55 (Gatekeeping & Access Control), this tactic differs from the sibling Gatekeeper Neutralization (which co-opts a single individual), Administrative Capture (which captures process), and Chief of Staff as Gatekeeper (which controls access flow): here the 'gatekeeping' is done by the very body chartered to challenge — the challenger is gatekeeping itself, by design.

### Strategic Implications

The deepest practitioner implication is that the tactic's effectiveness depends entirely on charter-design authority and forcing-function absence. An incumbent who can compose the membership and write the mandate can almost always engineer deadlock; an incumbent who cannot (because the board, a regulator, or a court has imposed the charter) cannot run this play. The corollary for those seeking to bypass the tactic: do not negotiate over the substance of a cross-functional review until you have negotiated the charter — specifically, demand (a) a single accountable chair with authority to force votes, (b) a binding deliverable date with automatic escalation if missed, and (c) a minority-report mechanism so dissenters can publish even when consensus fails. These three structural features defeat the tactic; their absence almost guarantees its success.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a particular review body and asking whether its composition is organic or engineered — look for evidence that the convenor controlled membership selection, that the mandates conflict in observable ways, and that the deliverable is described in process language ('explore,' 'review,' 'consider') rather than decision language ('recommend by date X with binding effect'). Use the generic lens when analyzing the broader institutional move — when an incumbent agrees to be 'reviewed' but quietly shapes the review's structure, the question is not whether the review will work but which fault lines have been activated. Treating the cross-functional team as the unit of analysis misses what is actually being done; treating engineered structural deadlock as the unit of analysis reveals the tactic's full lineage from the Roman tribunate through the modern board committee.
