# 0536 — THE 'ADJACENCY' STRATEGY (ENCIRCLEMENT)

> Expanding influence by systematically taking control of weakly defended functions or processes that are adjacent to a rival's domain, gradually encircling and isolating them before a direct confrontation.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C91 — Encirclement, Jurisdictional Creep & Cannibalization

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Pfeffer, Sun Tzu

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title borrows directly from military doctrine (encirclement, the indirect approach) but the described mechanism — capturing weakly defended adjacent functions to isolate a rival — is the organizational instantiation of a positional logic that transcends any single context. Title points outward to a general strategic principle while description anchors inward to a rival's organizational domain.

**Specific Interpretation.** An ambitious executive or department head expands their portfolio by quietly absorbing orphaned, neglected, or weakly governed functions adjacent to a rival's empire — vendor management, a metric, a reporting line, a process — until the rival's domain is structurally surrounded and dependent, at which point a direct challenge becomes feasible at low cost.

**Generic Interpretation.** A positional-power doctrine: rather than attack a defended center, claim the unclaimed periphery around it. The target loses by attrition of context — surrounded by territory that now belongs to the rival, the defender's authority shrinks even if their nominal jurisdiction does not.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The mechanism — indirect approach via uncontested adjacent ground — is reusable across military campaigns (Sun Tzu, Liddell Hart), geopolitical sphere-building, platform-business adjacency expansion (the classic 'commoditize your complement' logic), and intra-organizational empire-building. The org-political context is one instance of a much older positional doctrine, so the generic reading is more fundamental.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Acquiesce to the actor's gradual expansion into adjacent functions until structural encirclement makes formal challenge to the rival's domain politically inevitable
- **Option Y:** Successfully defend the rival's domain and its adjacencies as an integrated sphere of authority
- **Context:** An organizational arena with multiple loosely-governed adjacent functions surrounding a rival's established domain (e.g., a senior VP whose team owns a flagship product, with various supporting functions — analytics, vendor management, governance committees, regional liaisons — under ambiguous or weak ownership)

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not work by selling the actor's expansion as positively desirable. Each adjacent capture is framed as low-stakes housekeeping ('someone needs to own this') rather than as a benefit play. The whole signature of encirclement is that it advances without triggering an evaluative frame — the moment B(X) is overtly amplified, the territorial grab becomes legible and inviteable resistance prematurely.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. The actor only takes ground that is weakly defended — orphaned committees, neglected metrics, vendor relationships no one wants to manage. Acquiescence becomes the path of least resistance precisely because each individual step appears trivial, helpful, and cheap to allow.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

As adjacent territory falls to the actor, the rival's domain loses the interface relationships, data flows, and coordination authority that gave it broader strategic value. The rival still owns the formal asset but increasingly owns it in isolation — the perceived benefit of holding the central position erodes because the position no longer connects outward.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Every successful adjacency capture raises the cost of subsequent defense. The rival must now litigate boundaries the actor has already established, dispute precedents already set, and explain why the actor — who already runs four surrounding functions — should not also run the fifth. By the time direct challenge comes, the rival defends from a salient where every flank is exposed.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The actor's territorial expansion is made cheap to permit (each step is small and unglamorous), while the rival's central position is steadily devalued (loses its adjacencies and connective tissue) and made progressively costlier to defend (each new front opens from already-conceded ground). The deliberate absence of B(X)↑ is the tactic's signature — overt promotion would surface the underlying ambition and trigger coordinated resistance before encirclement is complete.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effective when the organization has poorly-defined boundaries around the rival's adjacencies, the rival is occupied with operational matters rather than scanning the perimeter, third parties (legal, finance, vendor management) are willing to accept new counterparties without inquiry, and the actor has the patience to expand over many quarters rather than push for visible gains. Fails when the rival reads territorial maps actively, when adjacent function owners themselves resist transfer, or when a single premature direct challenge surfaces the cumulative pattern before encirclement is structurally decisive.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Sun Tzu's indirect approach (later codified by Liddell Hart as 'the strategy of indirect approach') combined with Schelling's logic of focal points and incrementalism — the doctrine that decisive position is won by occupying ground the opponent has not thought to defend, with each individual move calibrated to fall below the threshold that would trigger coordinated response. Closely related to what Pfeffer calls 'strategic positioning' in organizational power, and to the salami-tactics literature on incremental territorial gains.

**Decision Alteration.** Each individual decision the target faces is structured to look local, small, and concessive — yielding a neglected committee, a vendor relationship, a process. The cumulative consequence (encirclement) is invisible at the level of any single choice. By the time the structural picture becomes legible, the position to defend has already become untenable. The mechanism inverts loss aversion by making each loss feel too small to fight while the aggregate loss is decisive.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where the contested terrain has many seams and orphans (loosely-owned functions, ambiguous jurisdictions, neglected processes); where the target is reactive rather than strategic in their perimeter scanning; where third-party gatekeepers (procurement, HR, finance) accept low-friction transfers; and where the actor can sustain a multi-period campaign without needing visible early wins.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A military theater where one ambitious field commander controls forces in territory adjacent to a rival commander's fortress city, and quietly builds out the supply, signal, and basing infrastructure surrounding it under cover of routine operational improvements

**Sample X:** Allow a regional commander to consolidate logistics, communications relays, and forward depots across the adjacent valleys to a rival's stronghold

**Sample Y:** Recognize the regional commander's adjacent build-up as preparatory encirclement and reinforce the rival stronghold's perimeter before the noose closes

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active in this configuration — the build-up is deliberately presented as routine logistics, not as strategic positioning

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Each piece of adjacent infrastructure is justified as ordinary operational improvement (better roads, faster comms, depot consolidation), making approval cheap and refusal look obstructionist

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — As the rival's fortress is steadily isolated from supply and reinforcement routes, its value as a strongpoint diminishes — it becomes a fortified position with nowhere to project from

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Defending the rival's stronghold now requires litigating with the adjacent commander over previously-conceded ground; reinforcement convoys must traverse territory the actor controls

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A digital ecosystem where the dominant platform owner systematically acquires or builds the small adjacent services that the rival marketplace depends on — not the marketplace itself, but everything around it, until the rival operates on rented infrastructure they no longer control

**Sample X:** Permit a dominant platform to acquire adjacent payments, identity, advertising, and analytics services that complement the rival's core marketplace

**Sample Y:** Defend the rival marketplace as a self-contained competitive offering with independent control of its surrounding services

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Each adjacent acquisition is announced as user-experience improvement or ecosystem investment, not as competitive positioning against the rival

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Regulators and customers accept each acquisition individually because each is small relative to the platform's core business and serves obvious utility

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The rival marketplace's independence becomes nominal — its identity layer, payments, analytics, and ad inventory all flow through infrastructure owned by the encircling platform

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any future move by the rival now requires renegotiating with the platform on multiple fronts simultaneously; sovereignty has been quietly traded away in a series of individually-trivial integrations

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** The indirect-approach logic generalizes to any contest where (a) territory has multiple seams and orphans, (b) the target is reactive rather than perimeter-scanning, and (c) the actor can sustain a multi-period campaign. Applies in military strategy (Cannae, Schlieffen Plan, German invasion of France via the Ardennes), geopolitical sphere-building (Russian peripheral influence operations, Chinese Belt and Road logic), platform-business adjacency expansion, internal corporate empire-building, and even academic department politics. Lower effectiveness when the contested terrain is tightly defined, the target is strategic about perimeter, or institutional gatekeepers resist transfer.

### Core Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Military: A field army secures the road network, river crossings, and supply depots surrounding an enemy fortress before any siege is declared, so that when the formal investment begins the fortress is already strategically untenable.
- Diplomacy: A great power signs trade, basing, and security agreements with each of the small states bordering a rival regional power, until the rival's sphere of influence has been quietly absorbed without any direct confrontation.
- Corporate empire-building: A SVP volunteers their team to absorb the analytics function, then vendor management, then governance committees adjacent to a rival SVP's product line — and only after all surrounding functions report up through them does the question of who should run the product itself become live.
- Platform business: A dominant operating-system vendor steadily integrates browser, search, identity, payments, and cloud services around a rival's flagship application, until the rival operates on infrastructure entirely owned by the platform.
- Academic politics: A department chair builds joint appointments, shared courses, and cross-listed seminars with every adjacent program to a rival department, gradually making the rival department's resources, students, and faculty interchangeable with their own.
- Activism: A reform coalition contests school boards, county prosecutor races, and election supervisor positions surrounding a contested statewide office, building infrastructure and bench depth before challenging the headline seat directly.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both readings converge on Strategy 14 (Resistance Triple) — the diagnostic absence of B(X)↑ is what makes this an encirclement tactic rather than a frontal expansion. The actor specifically does not amplify their own benefit because doing so would surface the underlying ambition prematurely. This distinguishes the tactic from its cluster siblings: BYPASS OPERATION goes around a rival to reach a third party; SPHERES OF INFLUENCE VIOLATION asserts authority into the rival's claimed jurisdiction directly; CANNIBALIZATION displaces a rival's function by replacement; ADJACENCY/ENCIRCLEMENT is unique in expanding around the rival without ever touching the center until the center is structurally indefensible.

### Strategic Implications

The practitioner takeaway is timing. Encirclement is a multi-period campaign whose value compounds — none of the individual moves are decisive, and any single move evaluated in isolation looks innocuous or even helpful. This creates two strategic vulnerabilities for defenders: first, the inability to point at any single act as the casus belli (defenders look paranoid if they object early, irrelevant if they object late); second, the temptation for the actor to break discipline and attempt a direct strike before the perimeter is complete, which surfaces the pattern and consolidates opposition. For practitioners playing defense, the only reliable countermove is active perimeter scanning — treating each request for adjacent function transfer as a strategic question, not an operational one.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing actual organizational positioning — mapping which adjacent functions are weakly held, which executive's portfolio is quietly expanding around whose, where the next adjacency capture is likely to land. Use the generic lens when designing or counter-designing the campaign as a whole — recognizing that the doctrine is positional rather than transactional, and that defense requires re-framing each individual concession as part of a connected territory rather than a standalone judgment.
