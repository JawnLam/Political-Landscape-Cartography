# 0566 — THE 'SUCCESS SILOING'

> Ensuring that the credit for successful collaborations is attributed solely to the favored faction, preventing rivals from gaining recognition.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C69 — KPI Gaming, Criteria-Shifting & Failure Dilution

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description names a specific organizational pattern (managing the optics of a joint deliverable so one faction monopolizes credit), but the underlying move — selective attribution of shared success to engineer asymmetric recognition — is a generic political principle that operates anywhere reputation is a tradeable currency.

**Specific Interpretation.** A leader who controls the post-mortem narrative of cross-functional initiatives ensures that announcements, internal communications, performance reviews, and external case studies name the favored faction as the driver while rendering rival contributors as auxiliary or invisible.

**Generic Interpretation.** The universal pattern of capturing the recognition output of any collaborative production process and routing it disproportionately to one contributor, starving rivals of the reputational return on their cooperation.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Credit is a scarce, zero-sum-ish resource in any setting where reputation drives future opportunity allocation. The mechanism — controlling the attribution stage of a shared output — is reusable across scientific co-authorship, military command, political coalitions, and corporate joint projects. The factional corporate framing is just one instance of the wider attribution-capture pattern.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Senior stakeholders and decision-makers attribute the success of cross-functional wins to the favored faction and elevate its members
- **Option Y:** Senior stakeholders recognize the rival faction's substantive contribution and elevate its members proportionally
- **Context:** A leader oversees a portfolio of cross-functional initiatives where the favored faction and a rival faction must collaborate on shared deliverables. The leader controls the narrative apparatus — steering committee presentations, success stories in town halls, performance review write-ups, the post-implementation case study — and uses that control to systematically front the favored faction in every retelling.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The entire mechanism manufactures benefit for the favored faction: visible credit becomes promotion fodder, bonus justification, headline assignment selection, and external speaking opportunities. Each successful collaboration is converted directly into reputational capital that compounds over future cycles.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not make it easier or cheaper to be the favored faction — they still must do real work on the collaboration. The siloing operates only on the post-production attribution stage; it doesn't reduce upfront effort or risk for the favored side.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. The rival faction does the work but is denied the recognition return — their contributions appear in private review documents at best, never in the narratives that drive promotion and external standing. Collaboration ceases to yield reputational ROI for them.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Rivals pay the standard cost of collaboration (time, political risk-sharing, defending the joint plan against skeptics) without the recognition payoff, making the all-in cost of participation a net loss. Repeated cycles also damage the rival's standing — they appear to deliver less while in fact delivering equally.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic engineers asymmetric returns on the same collaborative work. By controlling the attribution layer rather than the production layer, the leader converts the rival's labor into the favored faction's reputational fuel. The rival's effort is captured; their recognition is starved; over multiple cycles they appear to be lower performers despite identical contribution. Note the diagonal: B(X)↑ and B(Y)↓ are not independent moves — they are two sides of the same credit-allocation decision, which is what gives this tactic its elegance and deniability.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires the perpetrator to control the narrative chokepoints: who presents to the executive committee, who drafts the case study, who writes the joint performance reviews, who briefs the press. Effectiveness collapses if rivals have independent access to senior stakeholders or if measurement systems track contribution at individual level rather than initiative level. Sustained success siloing also tends to erode genuine collaboration over time — once rivals recognize the pattern, they withdraw effort or refuse to participate, killing the joint output the tactic depends on for its raw material.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Goffman's Impression Management combined with Pfeffer's organizational power dynamics — specifically, the principle that reputational capital is allocated through narrative control rather than objective contribution measurement. Also draws on the fundamental attribution error: observers assign causal credit to whichever actors are made most salient in the success story, even when the underlying work was distributed.

**Decision Alteration.** Audiences who evaluate contributors (for promotion, funding, future assignments) base their judgments on the publicly available account of who did what. By monopolizing that account, the actor causes evaluators to allocate scarce rewards as if the favored faction were the sole or primary contributor. The decision isn't 'who deserves credit' — the decision is 'who appears in the story,' and the actor controls the story.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works wherever (a) the output of work is collaborative and hard to disaggregate at the contribution level, (b) recognition flows through narrative channels the actor controls (announcements, case studies, post-mortems), and (c) reputation translates into future resource allocation. Strongest in environments where evaluators are too distant from the work to verify attribution independently.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A multilateral diplomatic negotiation where Norwegian back-channel diplomats did substantial early shuttling but the U.S. team controls the press conference, the White House Rose Garden ceremony, and the official communiqué. The U.S. team allows Norway a polite footnote-level mention while structuring the visible narrative around U.S. leadership.

**Sample X:** The press release names the U.S. State Department as the architect of the successful peace deal

**Sample Y:** The joint statement names both the U.S. and Norwegian back-channel mediators as co-architects

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The U.S. team accumulates diplomatic prestige usable in future negotiations, domestic political wins, and historical legacy.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — the U.S. team still bore the full cost of negotiation; the tactic operates only on credit distribution post-deal.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Norway's investment of years of quiet shuttle diplomacy yields no reputational return, weakening the case for future Norwegian inclusion in major mediations.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Norwegian diplomats must explain to their own parliament and public why their substantive effort produced invisible standing, raising the domestic political cost of having participated.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** An academic research breakthrough where a postdoc did the experimental design and a collaborating lab provided the critical reagent and analytical method, but the senior PI controls submission, authorship order negotiations, the press release through the university communications office, and the conference keynote invitations. The senior PI takes corresponding-author position and frames the work as 'my lab's discovery' in all public-facing narratives.

**Sample X:** The senior PI is listed as sole corresponding author on the breakthrough paper

**Sample Y:** The postdoc and the collaborating lab share corresponding-author standing reflecting actual contribution

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Sole corresponding authorship converts directly into grant renewal probability, tenure committee evidence, and citation impact that compounds across the PI's career.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — the PI's lab still bore real research costs; the tactic operates at the publication and dissemination stage only.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The postdoc loses the corresponding-authorship marker that drives faculty hiring decisions; the collaborating lab loses the standing that would have justified their own grant continuation.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The postdoc spent years on the work and emerges from the project with reduced market value relative to peers who were not similarly captured.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Operates in any setting where collaborative work produces a single legible output and recognition flows through narrative channels — corporate joint ventures, scientific collaborations, diplomatic negotiations, military joint operations, legislative coalition wins, film and creative production, open source contribution, and consulting engagements with multiple advisors. Less effective in environments with strong individual-contribution measurement (e.g., individual sales quotas) or transparent contribution tracking (e.g., open source commit logs).

### Application Examples

- A corporate CEO presenting a successful product launch as 'my division's work' in earnings calls while a partnering business unit's contribution disappears from the narrative.
- A general taking sole credit for a coalition military operation in the official after-action report, ensuring allied forces appear as supporting elements rather than co-equals.
- A senior academic listing their name first on a discovery announcement while the graduate student who designed the experiment is buried in the acknowledgments.
- A lobbying firm presenting a successful regulatory outcome to its client as solely its work, omitting the activist coalition whose grassroots pressure made the regulator move.
- A film director claiming an 'auteur' narrative for a movie's critical success while the screenwriter, cinematographer, and editor are reduced to credit-roll footnotes.
- A startup founder positioning themselves as the sole visionary behind a successful pivot in the company's investor narrative, erasing the early employee whose insight drove the change.
- A consulting partner taking ownership of a successful client engagement at the firm level, presenting it to internal partner committees as their account while the associate who did the analytical work is named only on internal HR forms.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Success siloing reveals that in collaborative work, the contest is rarely over the work itself — it is over the attribution layer that converts work into reputation. The favored faction and the rival may produce identical outputs, but only the favored faction's outputs are visible to the audiences that allocate future resources. Within Cluster C69 (KPI Gaming, Criteria-Shifting & Failure Dilution), this tactic is the credit-side mirror of FAILURE DILUTION: where failure dilution spreads bad outcomes across many shoulders to protect a faction, success siloing concentrates good outcomes onto few shoulders to elevate a faction. Together they form a complete asymmetric-attribution toolkit. Distinct from THE 'SUCCESS EMBARGO,' which suppresses recognition of any success (often to delay attribution until the optimal political moment), success siloing actively redirects recognition — the story is told, just not honestly.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic produces compounding advantages: each cycle of siloed credit makes the favored faction look stronger in the next allocation decision, which earns them the next plum collaboration, which they again take credit for. Over a few cycles this becomes self-justifying — the favored faction is, in performance review terms, genuinely the high-performing group, because all the visible wins are theirs. The deeper cost is structural: rivals learn that collaboration with the favored faction is a sucker's game, and either withdraw effort, refuse cooperation, or defect to compete openly. The leader who sustains success siloing across many cycles ends up commanding a faction that can no longer collaborate with anyone — they've poisoned the well they depended on for raw material. The tactic is also a strong signal to senior observers paying attention: a leader who keeps producing a faction that 'does everything well' may be revealing more about the attribution machinery than about the faction.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing a single organizational pattern — e.g., a particular VP who routinely fronts their own team in cross-functional wins — and when designing counter-measures within that organization (independent reporting lines, contribution-level metrics, neutral post-mortem facilitators). Use the generic lens when teaching the pattern, when analyzing it across domains (diplomatic, academic, creative), or when evaluating reputation-allocation systems at the structural level. The generic frame also helps identify the same mechanism in non-corporate settings where it might be missed because it doesn't look like 'office politics' — it looks like a press release, an authorship line, or a Nobel committee citation.
