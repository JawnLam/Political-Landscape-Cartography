# 0579 — THE 'CATCH-22' POLICY

> Establishing contradictory or mutually exclusive internal policies, ensuring that non-compliance is inevitable and providing a pretext for selective enforcement against targeted individuals or groups.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C13 — Policy Weaponization, Workaround Reclassification & Catch-22 Engineering

**Cited principles:** Foucault

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title evokes a specific named cultural archetype (Heller's novel) and the description anchors the tactic in 'internal policies' of an organization, yet the underlying move — engineering unavoidable rule violations to license discretionary punishment — is a general principle of disciplinary power that long predates and outlives any single workplace instance.

**Specific Interpretation.** A deliberate act of policy architecture inside an organization: writing two or more rules that cannot all be satisfied simultaneously (e.g., 'all decisions must be approved by Legal' AND 'decisions must be made within 24 hours' AND 'unauthorized delays are misconduct'), guaranteeing that any employee — and especially a targeted one — can be found in technical violation whenever enforcement is convenient.

**Generic Interpretation.** The abstract pattern of constructing a normative system whose internal contradictions make universal compliance impossible, thereby converting the question of 'who is in violation' (everyone) into the question of 'who will be enforced against' (chosen targets). The tactic shifts power from rule-following to rule-interpreting.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The organizational-policy framing is the modern habitat, but the mechanism — manufacturing universal guilt to enable selective punishment — is the same move used in totalitarian legal codes, immigration enforcement regimes, professional licensing boards, and tax codes. The 'internal policy' is just the modern surface; the underlying disciplinary architecture is the durable insight.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Submit to the rule-maker's discretionary authority — keep one's head down, stay aligned with the policy-owner, accept that protection from enforcement is the policy-owner's gift
- **Option Y:** Operate independently and rely on the formal policy as a stable contract — assume that following the rules in good faith protects against sanction
- **Context:** An organizational setting where a policy architect (HR head, compliance chief, COO, department lead) has authored or curated a body of internal policy and seeks to keep specific individuals or groups perpetually vulnerable to enforcement while preserving the appearance of neutral, rule-based governance

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not market the benefits of submission as such. It does not say 'align with me and you'll be rewarded.' The benefit of X (safety from enforcement) is implicit and conditional, never positively asserted. The whole point is to keep the carrot invisible so the stick does the work.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Submission and quiet alignment with the policy-owner become the path of least friction — discretionary forbearance is granted to those who cause no trouble. The cost of X (loyalty, deference, self-censorship) is reduced relative to what it would be under a fairly-enforced regime, because the target can always be 'forgiven' for violations as long as they remain useful or quiet.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic doesn't argue against the benefits of independent good-faith compliance; it simply makes that posture impossible to maintain. The independence (Y) still has all its original appeal — the move is to make it lethal, not unattractive.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core of the tactic. By engineering contradiction into the policy fabric, every independent actor is permanently in technical violation, and the cost of independence (Y) becomes the ever-present threat of discretionary sanction — discipline, demotion, termination, or criminal referral can be triggered at any moment by the policy-owner with full procedural cover.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The Catch-22 policy works by collapsing the formal/informal gap in the target's choice calculus. Formally, all employees should follow policy; informally, no one can. The contradiction shifts the meaningful decision from 'shall I comply?' to 'shall I be useful to the enforcer?' By making independence costly (universal liability) and submission cheap (discretionary mercy), the tactic produces a Cost Contrast without ever needing to argue for the merits of obedience.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) sufficient authorial control over the policy stack to install the contradictions in the first place, (b) enforcement discretion concentrated in or close to the architect, (c) procedural legitimacy of the policies — they must read as neutral, not as obviously rigged, and (d) a workforce or governance culture that respects formal rules enough to be coerced by them. Fails when an external audit, regulator, or court reads the policies in aggregate and rules the contradictions themselves a due-process violation; also fails when targets call the bluff publicly and force universal enforcement, exposing the selectivity.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Foucault's Disciplinary Power, combined with the criminology concept of Selective Enforcement (Bittner, Skolnick) — the productive use of universal nominal guilt to create asymmetric real vulnerability. The cultural archetypes are Kafka (The Trial — opaque, indeterminate guilt) and Heller (Catch-22 — formally contradictory rules), but the operational principle is Foucauldian: power flows not from prohibition but from the architecture of surveillance and the conditions under which transgression is noticed.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic converts every actor inside the system from a presumptive-rule-follower into a presumptive-violator-at-the-discretion-of-the-enforcer. This re-baselines the target's decision frame: independent action is no longer a neutral default but a standing liability, and securing the enforcer's tolerance becomes the dominant strategic imperative. Decisions formerly evaluated on merit are now evaluated on enforcement risk.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective when (a) the rule-maker controls both authorship and enforcement, (b) the rules are voluminous and technical enough to obscure the contradictions from casual reading, (c) appeals to higher authority either don't exist or share the rule-maker's interests, and (d) the cost of testing the rules (job, license, freedom) is high enough that targets self-censor preemptively.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A junior associate at a law firm where the partnership has written conflicting policies on billable-hour minimums, mandatory pro bono time, and prohibitions on after-hours work — making it mathematically impossible to satisfy all three. Partners use the contradictions to keep associates permanently in technical breach.

**Sample X:** Comply with the explicit will of the senior partner, regardless of the letter of policy

**Sample Y:** Insist on following written firm policy as a stable contract regardless of partner pressure

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active in this configuration — submission is not advertised as beneficial, only as safe

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Going along with what the partner wants in any given week becomes the path of lowest enforcement friction, because the policy contradictions ensure technical violation either way

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Good-faith policy compliance still carries its formal benefits — the move doesn't argue against the benefits, just makes the posture untenable

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Standing on written policy now means standing on whichever clause the partner chooses to ignore, leaving the associate exposed to the unmentioned clause as grounds for negative review, lost bonus, or partnership-track removal

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A small-business owner in a country with a tax code so internally contradictory that no firm can fully comply (e.g., rule A requires monthly VAT remittance, rule B prohibits remittance without certified accountant sign-off, rule C requires the accountant's certification to be filed quarterly). Tax authorities use the contradictions to selectively audit and shut down politically inconvenient businesses while ignoring identical violations by aligned ones.

**Sample X:** Maintain a posture of quiet alignment with the regime — comply visibly, do not call attention

**Sample Y:** Run a small business strictly by the published rules and rely on the law as a shield

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — not active — the regime does not need to advertise the rewards of loyalty; their absence of enforcement speaks loudly enough

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Visible alignment with regime priorities lowers the operational cost of doing business, because audits and inspections quietly bypass aligned firms

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Good-faith compliance still has its theoretical legal protections — the tactic does not attack the principle of rule-of-law, only its application

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Operating independently means being permanently exposed to selective audit, sudden freezing of accounts, or criminal referral — any of which can be triggered at the regime's discretion based on real technical violations the firm cannot avoid

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable in any normative system where (a) one actor controls both rule-authorship and rule-enforcement, and (b) the targets cannot easily exit the system. This includes corporate policy regimes, civil service codes, tax and regulatory law, immigration enforcement, professional licensing boards, military justice codes, school disciplinary codes, religious legal systems, and HOA/co-op governance. Less effective in genuinely adversarial legal systems with independent judicial review of the policy fabric itself, or in markets where targets can easily exit to a competitor.

### Core Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate HR writing remote-work, in-office presence, and travel-coverage policies whose combined requirements no employee can simultaneously satisfy, then using the contradictions to dismiss specific employees for cause
- Government compliance regimes for NGOs that require both pre-approval of foreign donations and prohibition on declining donations from approved sources, making receipt-as-violation and refusal-as-violation both available pretexts for shutdown
- Academic tenure codes that require both 'collegiality' and 'rigorous critical engagement with colleagues,' allowing tenure committees to deny on either ground depending on the candidate's politics
- Military regulations on commander discretion and on adherence to standing orders that contradict in combat conditions, leaving any junior officer subject to court-martial whenever the chain of command chooses to act
- Religious community bylaws that require both deference to elders and direct conscience-based action, allowing elders to discipline dissenters under whichever clause is convenient
- Immigration enforcement regimes in which the documentation required to remain in compliance contradicts the documentation required to obtain the documentation, leaving every non-citizen permanently deportable at administrative discretion

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both analyses converge on Cost Contrast (Strategy 8), and that convergence is itself the insight: the Catch-22 tactic is genuinely the same move at the specific and generic level. What differs across scale is only the unit of governance — a single department's policy stack, a corporate code, a national legal regime. The tactic distinguishes itself from siblings in cluster C13 by its constructive ambition: where THE TECHNICALITY TRAP (0195) exploits found loopholes, THE COMPLIANCE TRAP (0357) drowns targets in process volume, and THE WORKAROUND AS INSUBORDINATION (0493) retroactively reclassifies tolerated behavior, the Catch-22 Policy is the prospective design of impossibility. It is policy-as-trap, authored on purpose.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's deepest power is that it disguises discretionary rule by patronage as rule-of-law. Targets feel they are being treated 'by the book' even as the book has been engineered to produce whatever outcome the enforcer wants. This makes resistance harder than under naked authoritarianism, because the target's own respect for procedural legitimacy becomes the cage. Practitioners deploying this tactic should understand that its half-life shortens dramatically the moment a sophisticated observer — auditor, journalist, judge, board investigator — reads the policy stack in aggregate rather than clause by clause. Defenders should refuse to litigate any single violation in isolation and instead force visibility of the contradictory structure as the actual harm.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing a single policy stack inside a known organization — when the work is to identify which contradictions exist, who authored them, and whom they're aimed at. Use the generic lens when teaching the pattern, comparing across regimes, or counseling targets who need to understand that their predicament is not a personal failure of compliance but a structural feature of the system they're in. Counter-tactics differ accordingly: the specific reading suggests legal/audit interventions; the generic reading suggests exit, coalition-formation among co-targeted actors, and external publicity of the structural contradiction itself.
