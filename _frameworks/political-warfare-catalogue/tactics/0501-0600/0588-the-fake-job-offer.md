# 0588 — THE 'FAKE JOB OFFER'

> Arranging for an external recruiter to offer a seemingly attractive but ultimately non-existent job opportunity to a rival, destabilizing their focus and potentially leading them to signal disloyalty.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C41 — Termination Tactics, Departure Engineering & Resignation Politics

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Akerlof, Festinger

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title names a concrete bait-and-switch maneuver involving a fabricated recruiter and a non-existent role, but the operative mechanism — dangling a plausible exit option to destabilize a target's focus and surface their latent disloyalty — generalizes to any context where a fake outside alternative can be used to provoke self-incriminating behavior.

**Specific Interpretation.** A political operator covertly arranges an external recruiter to dangle a fabricated, attractive job opportunity in front of an internal rival, with the aim of distracting them, eroding their performance, and ideally drawing them into observable acts of disloyalty (clandestine interviews, sharing confidential information, badmouthing leadership) that can later be used against them.

**Generic Interpretation.** An influence pattern in which a fabricated alternative is dangled in front of a target to disrupt their commitment to the current arrangement and induce them to reveal compromising information, preferences, or intentions — a sting operation deployed against attention and loyalty rather than against assets.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the staged-recruiter delivery vehicle is corporate, the underlying mechanism — using a plausible-but-counterfeit outside option as both a distraction device and a behavioral lie-detector — is reusable across intelligence, sales counter-espionage, marital surveillance, and any domain where induced exposure of disloyalty is the goal. The corporate framing is one delivery channel; the deception architecture is the tactic.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Rival becomes destabilized by the fake offer — divided focus, leaked confidences, observable interview activity, or visible exit-prep behavior — giving the actor evidence of disloyalty and a justification to marginalize or terminate them
- **Option Y:** Rival remains focused, loyal, and politically intact, continuing to compete for resources, promotions, and influence on a level playing field
- **Context:** A senior actor faces an internal rival who is competent enough to be threatening but cannot be moved against on performance grounds; the actor needs a pretext, and the rival's own response to a tempting outside lure can be engineered into one

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The actor does not make the rival's downfall intrinsically more attractive — there is no positive case being built. The actor's preferred outcome arrives entirely through the rival's induced self-sabotage, not through any benefit enhancement on the actor's side.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The mechanism does not lower the cost of the rival's destabilization to the actor — if anything, running a staged recruitment scheme is operationally expensive and exposure-risky. The tactic's payoff is independent of cost reduction on the X axis.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The fake offer manufactures dissatisfaction with the rival's current role by presenting a more glamorous, better-paid, or higher-status fabrication for comparison. The current job's perceived benefits shrink relative to the phantom alternative — even after the offer evaporates, the contrast leaves residual discontent that erodes the rival's engagement and loyalty.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Engaging with the fake offer — taking calls, attending interviews, sharing strategic information to seem credible, badmouthing the current employer to the 'recruiter' — generates discoverable evidence of disloyalty. The rival's continued tenure (Y) becomes politically expensive once that evidence exists in the actor's hands, transforming a stable position into a precarious one.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic works without any X-side promotion because the actor's preferred outcome — the rival's removal or marginalization — is delivered by the rival's own induced behavior, not by the actor advocating for it. Both Y-side levers fire intensely: the fake offer simultaneously devalues the current role through contrast (B(Y)↓) and converts continued tenure into a politically discoverable liability through manufactured disloyalty evidence (C(Y)↑). The rival is engineered to dismantle their own position.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires a trustworthy external accomplice (recruiter or shell firm) whose role-play withstands the rival's verification attempts; depends on the rival being ambitious enough to take the bait but not so well-connected that they cross-check the offer through industry contacts; thrives when the rival has visible career frustrations the offer can exploit. Fails when the rival is unusually loyal, when the staged recruiter leaves a paper trail, or when the rival discloses the approach transparently to leadership (the textbook counter-move that flips the trap).

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Akerlof's Information Asymmetry combined with Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance — the actor exploits the target's inability to verify the counterfeit alternative while engineering a behavioral commitment (interview attendance, confidential disclosure) whose dissonance with the target's stated loyalty produces observable signals the actor can harvest. Schelling's notion of the credible decoy and classical honey-trap logic from intelligence tradecraft are adjacent frames.

**Decision Alteration.** Rather than directly persuading the target toward any choice, the tactic introduces a phantom option into the target's decision set, knowing the target's response to that phantom will reveal preferences and produce behaviors that compromise their existing position. The target's decision environment is corrupted at the input stage: they are reasoning about a counterfeit, and their reasoning leaves a trace.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works when the target cannot easily verify the alternative (closed industries, opaque hiring processes, isolated targets), when the target has latent dissatisfaction the bait can activate, when the actor controls observation of the target's response, and when the rules governing the current relationship treat disloyalty signals as sanctionable. Requires plausible deniability infrastructure — a delivery channel that cannot be traced back to the actor.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An intelligence service suspects an officer of leaking but lacks proof. They construct a fictitious foreign handler who approaches the target with a fabricated recruitment pitch, observing whether the target reports the contact (loyal) or engages with it (disloyal) — a classic dangle operation.

**Sample X:** Counter-intelligence officer obtains evidence that the suspected mole is willing to sell classified material to a foreign service

**Sample Y:** Suspected mole continues operating undetected within the agency, with no actionable evidence of disloyalty on file

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active — the agency does not need to make exposing the mole more attractive; the exposure happens through the mole's response to the bait.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — running the dangle is operationally expensive and risky; cost reduction is not the mechanism.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The fabricated foreign offer makes the mole's current cover position feel constraining and undercompensated by comparison, accelerating the disloyalty the agency wants to surface.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any engagement with the dangle — even a single recorded meeting — converts the mole's continued tenure from a state of plausible deniability into a state of documented treason, ending the cover.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A senior pastor of an independent church suspects a charismatic deacon of preparing to split the congregation. Through a friendly bishop in a distant denomination, the pastor arranges for the deacon to be quietly approached about a 'lead pastor opening' at a (fictitious) new church plant, monitoring whether the deacon shares membership data or speaks against the current leadership during the courtship.

**Sample X:** Pastor obtains proof the deacon is willing to leave with the donor list and start a rival congregation

**Sample Y:** Deacon remains in good standing, retains influence over donors, and continues to be a potential succession threat

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active — the pastor does not need to advocate for the deacon's removal; the deacon's response generates its own justification.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active — orchestrating the fake call requires considerable trust-network expenditure and risks exposure.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The fictitious lead-pastor role with autonomous authority makes the deacon's subordinate position feel artificially limiting, surfacing ambitions that were previously dormant.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — If the deacon shares donor contacts or attempts to recruit congregants during the courtship, those acts become firing offenses under church governance — turning a stable subordinate role into an indefensible position.

### Versatility

**Level:** moderate

**Explanation.** Generalizes to any context where (a) the target's commitment to a current arrangement can be tested by a plausible outside lure, (b) the actor can construct a credible counterfeit and observe the target's response covertly, and (c) the target's disloyalty signals carry institutional consequences. Found in intelligence tradecraft (dangle operations), corporate counter-espionage, religious and political succession contexts, and personal surveillance (suspicious-spouse private investigators). Less applicable where the target can easily verify alternatives (transparent labor markets, dense networks), where institutional rules treat outside conversations as legitimate (academia, sales-rep markets), or where the cost of exposure if the sting is discovered exceeds the payoff of confirming disloyalty.

### Core Strategy

### Application Examples

- Intelligence agency dangle operation: a fabricated foreign-service approach used to test a suspected mole and convert suspicion into prosecutable evidence
- Corporate counter-espionage: HR partners with a friendly executive search firm to test whether a suspected leaker will share trade secrets to land a fabricated competitor role
- Religious succession politics: a senior cleric arranges a fictitious distant-parish opening to surface whether a rival junior cleric is recruiting congregants for a future split
- Family business succession: an incumbent matriarch uses a cousin's fake 'business partnership offer' to test whether a rival heir will leak family financials to outsiders
- Political campaign discipline: a campaign manager has a fictitious rival super-PAC approach a suspected leaker on staff, monitoring whether they share internal strategy memos
- Marital surveillance: a private investigator runs a 'honey trap' approach against a suspected unfaithful spouse to convert suspicion into legally-admissible evidence for divorce proceedings
- Diplomatic vetting: a host country's intelligence service arranges a fake business opportunity for a foreign diplomat to test whether they will breach assignment protocols for personal enrichment

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

This tactic belongs to the class of 'induced self-exposure' moves — tactics where the actor manufactures a scenario in which the target's own behavior generates the evidence needed to act against them, eliminating the actor's need to make a direct case. It is the corporate-political descendant of the intelligence dangle operation, sharing operational DNA with sibling C41 tactics like THE 'EXTERNAL RECRUITER' PLOY and THE 'FAKE RECRUITMENT' PLOY, but distinguishing itself by the specific objective: not to extract the rival (departure engineering) but to convert the rival into a documented disloyalty case (evidence engineering). The mechanism is doubly elegant — the actor never has to argue for the rival's removal because the rival, in chasing the phantom, builds the case themselves.

### Strategic Implications

Understanding this tactic reframes how loyalty signals should be read in organizations: when a rival 'mysteriously' becomes the target of an attractive but unverifiable outside approach, that itself is intelligence about who in their political environment wants them gone. For practitioners, the key strategic insight is that the tactic's power lies in the target's information asymmetry — they cannot verify the offer before responding to it, and their response is captured before they can retract it. This makes it most dangerous precisely against ambitious, frustrated, network-isolated targets, and least effective against targets who treat all unsolicited approaches as potential sting operations and disclose them upward as a matter of routine. The tactic also illuminates a broader principle: the most effective political moves are those where the target's own behavior provides the actor's justification, leaving no fingerprints on the trigger.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing a particular corporate suspicion case — focus on the recruiter delivery vehicle, the rival's verification networks, the legal exposure of fabricated representations, and the institutional rules that make disloyalty actionable. Use the generic lens when teaching the broader principle of induced self-exposure or when designing counter-measures — counter-measures generalize across domains (verify approaches through independent channels, disclose unsolicited offers upward as policy, treat verification-resistant offers as suspect by default), while specific-lens responses are context-bound. The two lenses converge on the same strategy classification (Y Degradation), confirming that the tactic's signature is not in its delivery but in its targeting: it always attacks the alternative's standing rather than building up the preferred outcome.
