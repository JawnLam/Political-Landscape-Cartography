# 0532 — THE 'GHOST PROJECT'

> Continuing to work on a project that has been officially canceled, often using discretionary resources, in the hope of reviving it or using its outputs elsewhere.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#5 X Optimization** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓` · **Cluster:** C46 — Setting Up to Fail (Impossible Mandates, Glass Cliffs & Burner Initiatives)

**Cited principles:** Arkes & Blumer, Arkes, Blumer, Samuelson & Zeckhauser, Samuelson, Zeckhauser

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description is anchored in a specific corporate situation (an officially canceled project, discretionary resources, hopes of revival) but the underlying maneuver — preserving an option that authority has formally closed — is a generic pattern of covert optionality preservation visible across military, scientific, bureaucratic, and family domains.

**Specific Interpretation.** A subordinate or team champion whose pet project has been killed by leadership keeps the work alive in the seams of their normal workload, banking artifacts (designs, prototypes, datasets, code) so that when the political weather shifts the project can be re-presented as 'we already have something ready.'

**Generic Interpretation.** The broader pattern of refusing to fully accept an authority's negative decision by carrying the suppressed option forward in low-visibility form, converting a closed question into a dormant option that can be reopened when the cost-benefit calculus changes for the decider.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Although the description reads as a corporate skunkworks scenario, the deeper move — keeping a suppressed option alive against an authoritative 'no' so it can be re-floated under new conditions — recurs in military shadow programs, suppressed scientific research, vetoed legislation re-drafted as something else, and family business ideas a successor nurses until the patriarch dies. The corporate skunkworks is one instance of a much older pattern of bureaucratic shadow-work.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Leadership eventually revives the canceled project or absorbs its outputs into a different initiative
- **Option Y:** The cancellation stands and the project's intellectual capital decays into nothing
- **Context:** A mid-level technical leader's pet initiative has been officially killed in a portfolio review, but the leader continues to advance it on the margins of their team's discretionary time, building reusable artifacts in the hope that a future strategic shift, an executive change, or an adjacent initiative will create an opening to resurrect or repurpose the work.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

By the time a revival window opens, X is no longer 'fund a speculative idea' but 'adopt a demonstrably working prototype with sunk effort already absorbed.' The accumulating artifacts increase X's apparent benefit because the project arrives at the next decision point with maturity it should not yet have.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core of the tactic. The ghost work systematically eats the cost of future revival — code is written, IP is preserved, team knowledge stays warm, dependencies stay current. When the decision is re-asked, X is cheap to greenlight because most of the expensive work has already been paid for off-books.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not attack the cancellation decision or its rationale. The ghost project operator typically accepts the surface verdict and avoids public criticism of leadership's call — that's precisely why the work has to go underground. Attacking Y would invite scrutiny and end the ghosting.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — Not active

The tactic does not raise the cost of letting the cancellation stand. There is no burning platform constructed around Y; the actor lets Y persist as the official truth precisely so the work can continue unnoticed. The whole maneuver depends on Y feeling settled to everyone except the ghoster.

### Strategy

**#5 X Optimization** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates entirely on the preferred-choice axis: it keeps X alive and lowers the activation energy for resurrecting it, without engaging the cancellation itself. The asymmetry is deliberate — touching Y would draw fire from the authority that killed the project. Instead the ghoster bets that future conditions will revisit X on their own, and ensures that when they do, X is the cheapest, most credible option on the table.

**Effectiveness factors.** Depends on the actor having genuine discretionary slack (people, budget margin, unaccounted time), on cancellation rationale being context-dependent rather than principled (strategy reversed → opening; principle reversed → no opening), on leadership turnover or strategy oscillation cycles long enough to create a revival window, and on the actor's ability to keep ghost work invisible to the auditor side of the organization. Fails when the cancellation was a final principled rejection, when discretionary slack disappears under cost pressure, or when the ghosting is detected — at which point the actor loses credibility for having defied a direct decision.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Sunk Cost Effect (Arkes & Blumer) combined with Status Quo Bias (Samuelson & Zeckhauser) — operating in reverse on a future decider. The tactic manufactures the conditions under which the decider, when next presented with the choice, will see X as the path of least resistance because the costs are already absorbed and abandoning the now-mature artifact would itself feel like a loss.

**Decision Alteration.** It does not try to alter the current decider's mind about the current 'no.' Instead it alters the choice architecture facing the next decider (which may be the same person under new circumstances) by ensuring that when revival is reconsidered, X arrives pre-funded, pre-staffed, and pre-de-risked. The decision shifts from 'should we fund this speculative thing?' to 'should we throw away this nearly-done thing?' — a much harder no.

**Optimal Conditions.** Thrives in slack-rich environments with discretionary resources (R&D shops, knowledge-work teams with billable-time fungibility, academic labs, government research arms), in organizations with cyclical strategy revisions or expected leadership turnover, and in domains where artifacts compound in value over time (code, designs, datasets, models) rather than perishing. Less effective in tightly-audited operational environments or where the cancellation was based on enduring principle rather than transient priority.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A weapons-program manager whose system has been defunded continues subcomponent development under unrelated R&D line items, maintaining the engineering team and the integration knowledge, betting that the next strategic threat assessment or administration change will create a justification to resurrect the capability under a new name.

**Sample X:** A Cold War missile concept formally killed by Congress is later fielded as part of a successor program

**Sample Y:** The cancellation succeeds in eliminating the capability from the arsenal entirely

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — When the next national security review opens, the resurrected program shows up not as a paper concept but as integrated hardware with a tested subsystem and a coherent team, dramatically raising the perceived feasibility of the capability.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The covert continuation collapses the future restart cost — engineers stay on payroll under cover assignments, classified knowledge does not diffuse out of the building, and tooling is preserved, so the revival requires only a funding line rather than a full re-establishment.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The program manager does not lobby against the original cancellation or attack Congress's judgment; doing so would draw oversight that would terminate the ghost activity.

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — No effort is made to make defunding more painful — the manager wants the official decision to feel as concluded as possible to the appropriators while the work continues out of sight.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Career civil servants in a federal agency whose proposed rule was withdrawn after political pushback continue refining the technical analysis, building the evidentiary record, and maintaining the inter-agency relationships, so that when administration priorities shift the rule can be re-proposed within months rather than the years it would take to rebuild from scratch.

**Sample X:** A regulatory rule rejected by the current administration is re-proposed and adopted under a successor administration

**Sample Y:** The rejected rule stays dead and the policy direction is permanently abandoned

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — When the policy window reopens, the rule arrives with a mature evidentiary record, current stakeholder mapping, and refined technical drafting, making it look more rigorous and less risky than a freshly-proposed alternative.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The continued back-channel work eliminates the years of re-mobilization that would otherwise be required, allowing the rule to be ready for proposal within an administration's narrow window of political will.

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Civil servants are careful not to publicly relitigate the political withdrawal of the original rule — doing so would breach civil-service norms and politicize the technical staff.

- C(Y)↑ ✗ — No campaign is mounted to make the abandoned policy direction look worse over time; the technical staff lets the political verdict stand while quietly preserving the option.

### Versatility

**Level:** moderate

**Explanation.** Works wherever an actor has discretionary slack and where decisions are revisited on cycles — corporate R&D, military programs, scientific research, regulatory policy, academic department initiatives, family-business succession ideas. Less applicable in highly audited, tight-budget operational environments (manufacturing lines, audited financial functions) and in one-shot decisions that will not be revisited. Also constrained where cancellation rationale was principled rather than tactical — a project killed because it violated values stays killed, while a project killed because of this quarter's priorities lives on as a ghost.

### Core Strategy

**#5 X Optimization** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓`

### Application Examples

- A pharmaceutical scientist whose compound was deprioritized in portfolio review keeps the synthesis pathway warm using slack from approved projects, so that when a competitor publishes adjacent activity the compound can be re-greenlit as a fast-follow rather than a restart.
- Career intelligence officers continuing analytical work on a target deprioritized by the current administration, so that the analytical product is ready the day a new administration restores the target to the priority list.
- An NGO program officer whose advocacy campaign was paused for funder-relationship reasons continues building the coalition and updating the position papers, ready to relaunch within weeks of a funder leadership change.
- A graduate student whose dissertation direction was vetoed by an advisor continues the work on personal time, producing a draft sufficient to seek a co-advisor or transfer programs without losing two years of progress.
- A family-business heir whose modernization proposal was rejected by the founder builds out the operational plan and supplier relationships on the side, holding it in reserve for the inevitable succession transition.
- A municipal planning office whose redevelopment proposal was killed by a city council vote continues refining the design and securing community input, knowing that a new council two years out may reopen the question and the office wants to be ready with a polished plan rather than a stale one.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The Ghost Project's signature is the deliberate non-engagement with the cancellation decision itself. Unlike sibling tactics in the Set-Up-to-Fail cluster which engineer failure of a rival's mandate, this tactic accepts failure on the surface and works only on optionality preservation underneath. It is the asymmetric mirror of the Burner Initiative — where a Burner is launched publicly to die, the Ghost is killed publicly to live. The tactic also differs from sunk-cost-driven persistence (Staw's escalation of commitment) because the ghoster is not throwing more resources at a losing project from conviction; they are quietly preserving option value at low marginal cost while waiting for the environment to change.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic exposes a structural weakness in formal cancellation as a control mechanism: in slack-rich knowledge organizations, authority can stop a project on paper but cannot easily stop the work in the brains and laptops of the people who believed in it. This means leaders who want a decision to be truly final must either remove the slack (reorganize, defund, reassign the people) or address the underlying judgment of the believers, not merely the project. From the ghoster's side, the tactic is patient and cheap but carries a low-probability, high-severity downside: if detected, it brands the actor as someone who defied a direct decision, often more damaging than the project's original rejection. Compared to direct re-litigation of the cancellation, ghosting trades upside (no chance of immediately reversing the decision) for downside protection (no immediate political fight) — a classic optionality trade that suits actors with long time horizons and low risk tolerance for open conflict.

### Resolution Approach

Read the tactic as specific when the analytical question is about a particular cancellation event and what its champion will do next within that bounded organization. Read it as generic when the question is about how formal decisions get quietly preserved as future options across any institutional system. The generic frame travels better and reveals the deeper insight: in any organization with slack and cyclical strategy, an authoritative 'no' is rarely final — it is a temporary suppression of an option that may surface again when the surface conditions shift, and the apparent stability of cancellation decisions is partly an artifact of which ghosts happen to be running underneath.
