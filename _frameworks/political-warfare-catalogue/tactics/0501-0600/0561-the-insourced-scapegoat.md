# 0561 — THE 'INSOURCED SCAPEGOAT'

> Blaming internal shared services (e.g., IT, HR) for the failure of transformation initiatives, diverting attention from the strategic flaws of the leadership.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C26 — Scapegoating, Blame Architecture, Expendable Allies & Ghost Sponsorship

**Cited principles:** Cialdini, Festinger, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a specific corporate configuration — transformation initiatives plus internal shared services (IT, HR, Finance) as the named blame target — but the underlying move is the broader scapegoating pattern of routing accountability to a structurally weak internal function. The specific frame names a particular victim class; the generic frame names the principle by which that class was chosen.

**Specific Interpretation.** Leadership of a stalling transformation publicly attributes its failure to shared-services execution gaps ('IT couldn't deliver the platform,' 'HR couldn't recruit the talent,' 'Finance's systems blocked the rollout'), shielding the strategic premise of the transformation itself from scrutiny.

**Generic Interpretation.** Failure is routed to an internal function that is structurally unable to mount a political defense — cross-cutting, diffusely accountable, and lacking a single charismatic owner who can credibly counter-narrate. The 'shared services' category is contingent; the structural-weakness criterion is general.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The mechanism — picking a scapegoat whose structural position prevents effective counter-narration — reproduces across many domains beyond corporate transformations (central banks blamed for political-fiscal failures, back-office bureaus blamed for diplomatic incidents, anesthesiology blamed for surgical complications). The shared-services framing is a vivid instance of the general principle that scapegoats are chosen by political defensibility, not by causal contribution.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Board and senior stakeholders accept the narrative that the transformation's underperformance is an execution problem owned by shared services, and continue backing the original strategic direction (and its leadership)
- **Option Y:** Board and senior stakeholders interrogate the strategic premise of the transformation itself and hold the sponsoring leadership accountable for the design, not just the delivery, of the change
- **Context:** A multi-year, board-sanctioned transformation (digital, operational, cultural) is missing milestones twelve to eighteen months in. The CEO or transformation sponsor needs the board's next-stage commitment but cannot survive a strategic-premise review.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

This tactic does not make the transformation itself look better — there are no new claims about its strategic merit, no fresh upside being painted. The narrative move is purely about where failure lands, not about adding affirmative value to the preferred course. Sponsoring leaders rely on the original strategy's residual credibility, not on enhancement.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Continuing to back the transformation becomes cheaper for stakeholders because the perceived remediation is bounded and operational — 'fix IT delivery,' 'replace the HR lead,' 'unblock Finance's systems' — rather than a wholesale strategic reset. The cost of staying the course is reframed from 'admit the strategy was wrong' to 'support a leadership-driven course-correction on execution.'

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Interrogating the strategic premise loses appeal once the diagnosis appears settled. If the board accepts that shared services is the bottleneck, then strategic re-examination looks like overreach — questioning the architects when the carpenters are the ones who botched the build. The tactic moves the inquiry one floor down.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

A board member or executive who insists on probing the strategy itself must now contradict an operational diagnosis offered by named leadership, and risks appearing either out of touch with the specifics or politically motivated. They must also implicitly defend the shared-services functions, which are by design diffuse and hard to defend on individual merits — a thankless political posture.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration is diagnostic. The tactic does not affirmatively boost the transformation (B(X)↑ stays inactive) — it cannot, because the transformation is visibly underperforming and any new bullishness would invite scrutiny. Instead it makes continued backing structurally cheaper, makes strategic interrogation feel unwarranted and unrewarding, and makes that interrogation politically expensive to pursue. This is the classic 'wear down the alternative without selling the preferred' posture of Resistance Triple, fitted to a defensive-incumbent situation.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effectiveness depends on (a) shared-services functions actually having delivery gaps the leadership can point to with plausibility — fully invented failures collapse under technical review; (b) the board lacking independent technical advisors who can adjudicate strategic-versus-execution attribution; (c) the shared-services leaders being politically junior to the transformation sponsor and thus unable to counter-narrate without appearing insubordinate; (d) the absence of a strong COO or independent transformation office whose role is precisely to call out this misattribution. Fails when an audit committee or independent reviewer is empaneled with cross-functional credibility, or when a shared-services leader has board-level allies and refuses to absorb the blame quietly.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross 1977) operating in tandem with Cialdini's Authority heuristic and Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance reduction. The audience under time pressure attributes failure to the most visible operational actor rather than to structural or strategic causes; the sponsoring authority's diagnosis is taken as informed; and the audience reduces dissonance about their own prior endorsement of the strategy by accepting an execution-level account that preserves their earlier judgment.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not argue that the strategy was right. It substitutes the question. The audience's choice shifts from 'was this the right course?' to 'who failed to execute the course?' — and the answer is pre-loaded. Because the substituted question is narrower, easier to answer, and emotionally lower-stakes, audiences default to it; the original question never gets re-asked. Strategic accountability dissolves into operational housekeeping.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where (i) the scapegoat function is cross-cutting and diffuse — touching everything, owning nothing cleanly; (ii) the function's leadership ranks below the strategy's sponsor and has weak board access; (iii) the audience values continuity and is averse to the disruption of a strategy reversal; (iv) the function lacks an external constituency (customers, regulators, suppliers) who would push back; (v) there is real execution imperfection on which the narrative can plausibly hang.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A national e-government transformation, championed by a powerful minister, is two years behind schedule. Press coverage focuses on the central IT agency's procurement failures. The minister appears before a parliamentary committee.

**Sample X:** Parliament accepts that a stalled national digitization program failed because the central IT agency mis-procured the platform, and renews political backing for the responsible minister

**Sample Y:** Parliament examines whether the digitization program's underlying policy design — its scope, timeline, and governance model — was flawed from inception, and holds the minister accountable

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The program itself is not re-sold; its strategic claims are left untouched and rely on residual political endorsement

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Continued backing becomes a vote for 'fixing procurement at the IT agency,' a bounded technical remediation, rather than for the minister's full policy design

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Investigating policy design looks redundant when the IT agency has been publicly named — the diagnosis appears settled and further inquiry looks politically motivated

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — A parliamentarian pressing the strategic question must contradict the minister's operational account and defend an agency that, by design, cannot articulate its own contribution clearly

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A hospital network's two-year clinical pathway transformation has produced no measurable outcome improvements. The CMO presents to the board, attributing the gap to pharmacy turnaround times and lab integration delays.

**Sample X:** A hospital board accepts that a failed clinical pathway redesign was caused by the central pharmacy and lab services' inability to adapt their workflows, and continues backing the chief medical officer's redesign approach

**Sample Y:** The board interrogates whether the CMO's clinical pathway model itself was poorly suited to the patient population and care environment

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The pathway model's clinical merits are not reasserted with new evidence — the tactic relies on the model's pre-existing credibility

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Staying the course means commissioning a 'pharmacy and lab integration sprint,' an operational fix that costs far less political capital than a clinical-strategy reversal

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Re-examining the clinical model loses appeal when an operational cause has been named — board members are not clinicians and prefer the simpler, named explanation

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — A board member pressing the clinical question must take on the CMO's professional authority while defending support functions that have no clinical voice of their own

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** The pattern reappears wherever an authoritative sponsor is accountable for a complex initiative and a cross-cutting internal function can plausibly absorb operational blame. Seen across corporate transformations, government program failures, hospital and university administrative reforms, military procurement, and large NGO program rollouts. Less effective in environments with independent oversight bodies (inspectors general, audit committees with technical staffing) whose role is precisely to reattribute causation. Also less effective where the scapegoat function has strong external constituencies — for example, blaming HR for a transformation failure is harder when employee outcomes are tracked by a board people-committee, and blaming IT is harder when customer-facing systems made the failure visible to regulators.

### Core Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate transformation: A CEO blames the IT function for the failure of a digital transformation, deflecting from a flawed strategic bet on a technology platform that never matched the firm's operating reality
- Government program failure: A cabinet minister attributes the collapse of a benefits-system overhaul to the central government IT shared service, shielding the policy design from review by the Public Accounts Committee
- Military procurement: A combatant command blames the central logistics agency for a failed expeditionary rollout, sparing the operational concept from after-action interrogation
- Academic restructuring: A university provost attributes the failure of a curriculum reform to the registrar's office and central IT, leaving the pedagogical strategy intact in the faculty senate's review
- NGO program collapse: A program director blames the central monitoring-and-evaluation team for a country-program shutdown, deflecting from a flawed theory of change that the donor would otherwise have to revisit
- Hospital reform: A chief medical officer blames pharmacy and laboratory services for a stalled clinical redesign, preserving the clinical model and the CMO's authority over it

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

What unifies the specific and generic readings is that the tactic chooses its scapegoat by political defensibility, not by causal contribution. Shared services are useful in the corporate frame for the same reason central agencies are useful in the governmental frame and lab/pharmacy services are useful in the hospital frame: they are cross-cutting, diffusely owned, structurally junior to the sponsor of the failing initiative, and lacking the kind of single charismatic head who could mount a credible counter-narrative. The Resistance Triple configuration is itself diagnostic — the tactic conspicuously does not boost the preferred course (B(X)↑ is absent), because doing so would re-open the strategic question the tactic is trying to close. It works precisely by making the strategic question feel already-answered. Distinguishing this from the closely related 'INSOURCING BLAME / OUTSOURCING CREDIT' (0085): that tactic is the broader policy of routing all internal failure to internal parties while externalizing credit; the 'Insourced Scapegoat' is the narrower operational move of selecting a specific structurally-weak internal function as the named blame target during a particular failure event.

### Strategic Implications

For practitioners on offense, the tactic's discipline is in restraint: do not over-claim. The narrative must point to real execution gaps in the named function, not invent them, because the tactic's whole leverage rests on plausibility. For practitioners on defense — whether a board member, an independent reviewer, or the leader of the scapegoated function — the counter-move is to refuse the substitute question. The defensive ask is not 'did the IT function under-deliver?' (the answer will usually be partly yes) but 'is the strategic premise sound on its own terms, evaluated before execution?' Empaneling a review whose terms of reference explicitly separate strategic-design assessment from execution assessment denies the tactic its core mechanism. Shared-services leaders who anticipate this tactic should also build, in advance, written records of the scope, constraints, and dependencies the sponsoring strategy imposed on them — quiet documentation now becomes the evidence base later.

### Resolution Approach

Apply the specific lens when analyzing a board-level confrontation over a stalled corporate transformation, where the named victim class (IT, HR, Finance shared services) and the named beneficiary (the transformation sponsor) are the operative actors. Apply the generic lens when the underlying question is about scapegoat-selection criteria — why this function and not another? — which is a question that recurs across governments, militaries, hospitals, and NGOs whenever a high-profile initiative needs a place for its failure to land.
