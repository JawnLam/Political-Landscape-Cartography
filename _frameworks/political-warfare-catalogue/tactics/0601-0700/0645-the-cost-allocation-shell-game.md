# 0645 — THE 'COST ALLOCATION' SHELL GAME

> Continuously changing the methodology for allocating overhead costs to artificially deflate the expenses of favored projects and inflate the costs of rival initiatives.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C05 — Data Manipulation & Metric Engineering

**Cited principles:** Kahneman, Tversky, Akerlof

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic is named in the specific vocabulary of managerial accounting (overhead allocation methodology) but its mechanism — continuously reshuffling the rules by which a shared cost is attributed in order to flatter favored work and burden rival work — is the generic move of weaponizing discretionary measurement. The accounting frame is the carrier, not the engine.

**Specific Interpretation.** A finance or controllership actor with authority over allocation methodology (activity-based costing keys, headcount drivers, square-footage drivers, IT charge-back rules) repeatedly revises those keys so that the favored project's P&L looks lean and the rival's looks bloated, governing the investment, prioritization, and kill decisions that follow.

**Generic Interpretation.** The abstract pattern is rule-revision arbitrage: where two activities draw on a shared resource and the apportionment formula is discretionary, the actor who controls the formula controls the apparent cost — and apparent cost, not real cost, is what governance committees compare.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The accounting setting is incidental. The same maneuver operates whenever there is a shared resource pool (overhead, server capacity, scientist time, regulatory burden, carbon budget) and a discretionary attribution rule. The cluster siblings (CRITERIA SHIFTING, GASLIGHTING BY METRICS, DEFINITION OF DONE WARFARE, DEPRECIATION POLITICS) confirm this is one instance of a broader family in which the actor's edge comes from owning the measurement rule rather than from operating on the underlying activity.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Approve continued funding, prioritization, and protection of the favored project on the basis of its (artificially deflated) cost profile
- **Option Y:** Approve continued funding of the rival initiative on the basis of its (artificially inflated) cost profile
- **Context:** A capital allocation or portfolio review committee inside a multi-division firm where corporate or shared services costs (IT, facilities, finance, legal) are pushed down to project P&Ls through allocation keys controlled by the CFO's office, and where method changes are routine enough to pass without external audit scrutiny

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not touch the favored project's revenue case, strategic narrative, or capability claims. Its sponsors still have to argue for X on its merits — the maneuver only ensures those merits are not eaten by a heavy cost line. Operating this lever would require a different tactic (a re-pitch, a roadmap reframe), not a methodology change.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the core operating move. Reweighting overhead drivers — shifting from headcount-based to revenue-based allocation, narrowing the cost pool, redrawing the activity hierarchy — surgically subtracts shared cost from the favored project's books. The deflation is not a side effect; it is the engineered output, and it is the lever doing most of the work.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The rival's outputs, customer value, and strategic claims are unchallenged. The tactic operates entirely on the cost side of the ledger — the rival can keep producing whatever it produces and the same numerator survives intact. This is what distinguishes the maneuver from a direct attack on the rival's mission.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The shared overhead has to land somewhere. When the methodology shift pulls cost off the favored project, that cost reappears on the rival's books — the rival now carries a heavier corporate burden it did not incur, eroding its margin, ROI, and payback period exactly when governance compares the two. The tactic structurally couples the two effects: deflating X requires inflating Y.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** Because shared costs are by definition zero-sum within a pool, any methodology change that lightens one project's burden automatically heavies another's. The tactic exploits this conservation law to drive both levers with a single instrument — the allocation rule itself — without ever having to argue about merit, value, or strategy. Governance committees comparing project P&Ls then see a cost differential that looks like business reality but is methodological artifact.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) discretionary control of the allocation methodology, typically by the CFO's office or a controllership function; (b) plausible technical justification for each revision (ABC refinement, driver accuracy, GAAP-neutral) so changes do not trigger audit flags; (c) governance reviewers who compare period-to-period project costs without normalizing for methodology changes; (d) sufficient frequency of revision that no single change is large enough to provoke investigation but the cumulative effect is decisive. Fails when an independent FP&A function maintains restated baselines, when affected project owners have allocation transparency rights, or when an internal auditor instruments method change logs.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Akerlof's Information Asymmetry combined with Tversky/Kahneman's Framing Effect, operating through Goodhart's Law (when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure). The actor who controls the measurement convention controls the perceived cost; perceivers treat the framed number as the underlying reality because the cost of independently re-deriving it is prohibitive.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not attempt to change minds about substance. It alters the numbers in front of the decision-maker, knowing that comparative decisions (kill/keep, fund/defund, prioritize/deprioritize) are made by reading the numerator and denominator off whatever schedule is presented. By owning the schedule, the actor makes the rival look expensive and the favorite look cheap without ever making an argument either party could rebut on merits.

**Optimal Conditions.** Any decision system where (a) a shared resource pool is apportioned among competing claimants by a formula, (b) the formula's parameters are technically defensible under multiple alternative rule-sets, (c) one actor has procedural authority to revise the formula, and (d) reviewers compare outputs without standardizing on a fixed methodology. The opacity of the technical justification — accounting, statistical, algorithmic — is itself part of the protection.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An OMB or agency-internal budget exercise where shared infrastructure costs (IT, legal counsel, regional office overhead) are pushed down to program P&Ls; the comptroller controls the cost-driver methodology and adjusts it across cycles in ways that systematically advantage the administrator's priorities

**Sample X:** A federal agency's preferred regulatory program survives a budget review with an apparently modest enforcement cost

**Sample Y:** A rival program in the same agency appears bloated and is targeted for cuts

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The favored program's policy case, statutory authority, and outputs are untouched

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Shifting from a per-program headcount key to a transaction-volume key lifts shared cost off the favored program if it runs lean staffing on high-volume cases

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The rival program's mission impact, beneficiary count, and statutory standing remain unchallenged

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The same methodology change loads the conserved overhead onto the rival program, which now appears to carry an outsize share of agency infrastructure

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A research university's departmental review where shared core-facility costs (electron microscope hours, computational cluster time, technician support) are charged back to PI grants through internal recharge rates; the department chair, who is also a PI with active projects, controls the recharge methodology

**Sample X:** A senior researcher's preferred lab program continues to receive instrument time and graduate-student lines

**Sample Y:** A junior PI's program is squeezed out of shared facility access on apparent cost grounds

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The scientific case for the favored research is not bolstered — only its apparent affordability

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Reclassifying instrument depreciation into a flat departmental overhead rather than per-hour usage frees the chair's high-utilization projects from variable charges

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — The junior PI's research quality and grant prospects are not directly attacked

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The flat overhead now falls disproportionately on lower-utilization junior labs that, under per-hour charging, would have paid less; their grant budgets show inflated facilities costs

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any organizational context with a shared-cost pool and discretionary allocation authority — corporate ABC/overhead, government program budgeting, university recharge centers, cloud chargeback among engineering teams, hospital department cost accounting, joint-venture cost-sharing formulas, carbon allowance allocation among business units. Less effective in flat-structure organizations without shared pools, in startups where there is no overhead to allocate, and in jurisdictions where allocation methodology is set externally (e.g., regulated utility cost recovery overseen by a PUC).

### Application Examples

- A bank's CFO repeatedly retunes the funds-transfer-pricing methodology so the CEO's strategic priority business line shows attractive net interest margin while a rival division's appears unprofitable.
- A defense contractor's program executive shifts indirect cost pools (engineering overhead, G&A) between major programs across reporting cycles so cost-plus contracts the firm wants to expand look efficient at proposal time.
- A hospital system's finance office adjusts the methodology for allocating shared service costs (medical records, billing, malpractice insurance) so the service line the CMO wants to grow shows margin while a rival service line appears to lose money per case.
- A cloud platform team rewrites the chargeback formula for shared infrastructure (load balancers, observability stack, security tooling) so the leadership-favored product team's unit economics look strong while a rival team's apparent infrastructure spend balloons.
- A diversified industrial conglomerate's headquarters changes the basis for allocating corporate overhead from revenue share to asset share between annual planning cycles, advantaging the asset-light division the chairman favors over the capital-intensive division he wants to divest.
- A multinational NGO reallocates shared country-office costs (security, compliance, expat support) between program lines so the donor-favored thematic program reports impressive cost-per-beneficiary while a competing program appears administratively heavy.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The shell game belongs to a distinct family of tactics — alongside its cluster siblings CRITERIA SHIFTING, GASLIGHTING BY METRICS, and DEFINITION OF DONE WARFARE — in which influence is exercised not by arguing about reality but by owning the rules that translate reality into numbers. What distinguishes this tactic within that family is the conservation property: because overhead pools are zero-sum, every act of deflation is simultaneously an act of inflation elsewhere, so a single methodological change drives both Cost Contrast levers without the actor ever having to author a second move. The 'shell game' label is exact: the pea (the cost) is always under one of the cups, and the actor controls which cup.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic's power and its vulnerability are the same thing — methodology changes leave a paper trail in the general ledger and the controllership's own change logs. It works only as long as no one normalizes prior-period comparisons or audits method-change frequency. Practitioners on the defending side should demand restated baselines whenever methodology changes (the same discipline GAAP imposes on accounting policy changes for external reporting) and should escalate any pattern of changes whose net effect consistently advantages a single sponsor. Practitioners considering the tactic should understand it is a wasting asset: each revision is forgivable in isolation, but a multi-year pattern becomes self-evidently engineered once anyone bothers to plot the directional bias.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing or defending against a concrete allocation dispute — the active_levers framing focuses attention on whether the methodology change is genuinely producing both deflation of one P&L and inflation of another, which is the diagnostic signature. Use the generic lens when the analyst sees the same maneuver in a non-accounting context (regulatory burden allocation, capacity attribution, chargeback rules in any shared-resource system) and needs to recognize that the rule-revision-arbitrage pattern is the same animal in different clothes. Both lenses produce the same strategy classification (Cost Contrast, 8), which is itself a finding: the tactic's signature is mechanically consistent across domains because the conservation property is mechanically consistent.
