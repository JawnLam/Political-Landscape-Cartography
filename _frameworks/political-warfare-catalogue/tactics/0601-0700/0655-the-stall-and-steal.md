# 0655 — THE STALL AND STEAL

> Intentionally paralyzing a rival's innovative project through bureaucratic delays until one's own faction can duplicate the idea, launch it first, and claim the credit.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C63 — Active Sabotage, Pipeline Poisoning & False-Flag Operations

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Akerlof

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a specific organizational dynamic — a rival's innovation project deliberately paralyzed via process while the saboteur's faction races to first-mover status with the stolen concept — but the underlying mechanism (asymmetric time-arbitrage in idea markets, where procedural control becomes the medium of intellectual appropriation) generalizes far beyond corporate R&D into any rivalrous domain with sequential gatekeeping.

**Specific Interpretation.** An intra-organizational sabotage tactic in which a politically positioned actor uses bureaucratic chokepoints (review boards, legal clearances, compliance gates, resource approvals) to freeze a rival's project while a parallel team within the actor's faction reverse-engineers the idea and ships first, then collects the credit, budget, and career capital the original team had built.

**Generic Interpretation.** A general influence pattern of weaponized procedural delay coupled with covert parallel execution — the actor controls Y's clock while privately running X to completion, exploiting the fact that in idea-based competition the first credible mover claims the conceptual territory regardless of who originated it.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Although the named context (rival innovation project, faction duplication, credit-claiming) reads as classic intra-org politics, the defining mechanism — using control over the alternative's timeline to manufacture one's own first-mover advantage — is a reusable strategic primitive that recurs in venture capital, patent prosecution, legislative drafting, scientific publication, and military procurement. The procedural sabotage is incidental to the deeper move: time-arbitrage on attributable origination.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Adopt and credit the saboteur's faction's version of the innovation, which has shipped first and appears mature
- **Option Y:** Wait for the original rival team to complete their stalled project and credit them as the originator
- **Context:** A corporate environment where two factions are competing for the strategic mandate around a new initiative (product line, technology, business model). The saboteur sits on, or has influence over, the approval gates the rival must clear, while the saboteur's faction is positioned to execute a parallel build.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

By launching first with a polished version, the saboteur's faction makes their iteration appear as the definitive instantiation of the idea — leadership sees a working artifact attached to their team, not a stalled proposal attached to the rival. The benefit of choosing X is inflated by the visible product and the narrative of execution velocity.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not reduce the inherent cost of adopting the saboteur's version. Whatever the project costs, it costs. The mechanism operates on relative timing and credit attribution, not on the absolute economics of the preferred choice.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core of the tactic. The rival's stalled project loses its central benefit — originator credit — the moment the saboteur's faction ships. What was 'visionary' becomes 'derivative' or 'late.' Even if the rival eventually completes a superior version, the conceptual territory has been claimed; their work now reads as a follow-on, not a foundation.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Bureaucratic delay directly inflicts cost on the rival team: burn rate without progress, team morale erosion, sponsor patience evaporation, opportunity cost of locked engineers, and the political tax of appearing unable to deliver. Each cleared gate produces a new one. The cost-loading is the active sabotage mechanism — the engine that buys the time the steal requires.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic operates almost entirely on the alternative — grinding the rival's project under procedural cost while gutting its claim to originator benefit — while doing only modest positive promotion of the saboteur's faction's version (the launch itself does the talking). C(X)↓ is conspicuously absent: the actor still has to actually build the thing. This three-lever asymmetric attack on Y is the signature of Y-Focused Triple.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (a) genuine procedural authority or strong informal influence over the rival's clearance path, (b) parallel execution capacity hidden well enough that the duplication isn't discovered mid-build, (c) plausibly deniable rationales for each delay ('compliance concern,' 'resource review,' 'strategic alignment question'), and (d) an audience that rewards shipping over originating. Fails when the rival team has independent escalation paths to leadership, when the duplication is detected and reframed as theft, when the rival's idea is so distinctive that the copy is obviously derivative, or when the organization has strong norms around attribution and intellectual property within its walls.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Schelling's First-Mover Focal Point combined with Akerlof's Information Asymmetry — the tactic exploits the cognitive convention that whoever produces the first visible, working instantiation of an idea becomes the canonical referent for that idea, regardless of provenance. Asymmetric control over the alternative's tempo creates an information environment where the audience never sees the originator's mature work, so the copy becomes the only version they can evaluate.

**Decision Alteration.** The audience's choice between attributing the idea to the originator versus the appropriator is rigged at the input stage rather than the deliberation stage. By the time the decision is made, only one viable artifact exists. The decision feels like a comparison but is in fact a non-choice — the appropriator's version is the only thing on the table, and the originator's version exists only as an unrealized claim, which the audience systematically discounts.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best in domains with (a) gate-based progression that one actor can influence, (b) reward systems weighted toward visible deliverables over documented originality, (c) audiences with limited bandwidth to investigate provenance, (d) ideas that are graspable enough to be re-implemented without the originator's tacit knowledge, and (e) sufficient distance between the gatekeeper role and the executing faction to maintain plausible deniability.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A large pharma's licensing committee, controlled by allies of the internal biologics group, has repeatedly returned the biotech partner's IND-enabling package with new questions while the in-house group quietly stood up a parallel program using insights from the disclosure

**Sample X:** Crediting a pharmaceutical company's in-house biologics group with the discovery of a novel antibody platform that they recently 'launched'

**Sample Y:** Crediting a smaller licensed-in biotech partner whose original disclosure documents have been sitting in regulatory review for fourteen months

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The in-house program's shipped milestones (assay validation, animal data, CMC progress) make their version look like the real, executable platform — the audience sees deliverables, not provenance

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The cost of running the in-house program is unchanged; the tactic doesn't make the work cheaper, only earlier-credited

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The biotech partner's claim to platform origination is hollowed out — when their data eventually clears review, it reads as a confirming follow-on to the in-house group's 'breakthrough'

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each round of regulatory questions burns the biotech's cash runway, frustrates their investors, and erodes the deal's perceived value, sometimes forcing renegotiation on terms that strip out originator economics

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A junior legislator from the minority introduced an innovative regulatory framework on AI accountability. The bill went to a committee chaired by a senior majority leader, who scheduled no hearings and requested successive rounds of analysis from the legislative counsel's office. Meanwhile, the chair's staff drafted a near-identical bill, which was introduced under the chair's name and fast-tracked through a different committee they also controlled

**Sample X:** Passing a legislative bill drafted by the majority leadership's staff, with the leadership's name attached as primary sponsor

**Sample Y:** Passing a substantively identical bill from a junior member of the opposing caucus whose original draft has been stuck in committee for nine months

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The chair's bill arrives with the procedural backing of leadership, the analytical polish of staff resources, and the legitimacy of having moved through 'proper' channels — it looks like the mature, governable version

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The political and substantive cost of passing the bill is unchanged; the tactic doesn't lower the price of action, only redirects credit

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The junior legislator's claim to authorship is publicly displaced — even sympathetic commentators end up describing the chair's version as 'the bill' on AI accountability, and the junior member's version disappears into procedural limbo

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Sustained committee inaction inflicts a reputational cost on the junior member ('can't move legislation'), exhausts their staff's bandwidth on a bill going nowhere, and burns down the political window in which AI accountability had narrative momentum

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any domain where (a) ideas are the unit of competition, (b) some actor controls the progression timeline of others' work, and (c) attribution is determined by visible first-mover status rather than documented origination. This covers corporate R&D, scientific publication races, patent prosecution and licensing, legislative drafting, defense procurement, venture capital deal structuring, academic departmental politics, and intra-agency policy turf battles. Less effective where attribution is contractually or legally fixed at origination (peer-reviewed publication with priority dates, patent applications with filing-date priority), or where the originator can route around the gatekeeper.

### Core Strategy

### Application Examples

- Corporate R&D: a division head sitting on a portfolio review committee repeatedly defers a sister division's emerging-tech proposal while staffing a parallel skunkworks that ships first under their own brand
- Legislative process: a committee chair stalls a junior member's high-profile bill via referral to fiscal and legal review while their own staff drafts a substantively identical version that moves through a friendly committee
- Academic science: a journal editor sends a competing lab's submission through multiple revision rounds while a paper from the editor's collaborator on the same finding is accepted in another venue, becoming the citable first publication
- Government procurement: a program office buries a small contractor's unsolicited proposal in technical questions while initiating a similar program of record with a preferred prime contractor, effectively appropriating the architecture
- Venture capital: a fund stalls term-sheet negotiations with a portfolio target while quietly seeding a parallel startup with the same thesis, founded by an executive-in-residence on the fund's payroll
- Diplomacy: a chair-nation of a multilateral working group stalls a smaller state's policy proposal through translation and consultation rounds while introducing a near-identical resolution under its own flag at a parallel forum

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific and generic readings converge on Y-Focused Triple, which is itself the tactic's signature: this is not a story about promoting one's own work but about controlling the alternative's clock and stripping its origination claim. The cluster siblings illuminate the same family — the Innovation Blockade attacks Y without the parallel steal, Gold Plating Sabotage attacks Y by overloading rather than delaying, and the Patent Blockade attacks Y through legal rather than procedural mechanisms. The Stall and Steal is distinctive within the cluster because the procedural delay is purposive and time-bounded — it exists to buy a window for the parallel build, not to permanently destroy the rival's project. The delay ends precisely when the steal lands.

### Strategic Implications

Practitioners face this tactic far more often than they recognize, because each individual delay looks legitimate on its own terms — every gate has a real rationale, every question is technically reasonable, every escalation is plausible. The pattern only becomes visible when the parallel build surfaces, and by then the originator's defensive options have collapsed: protesting now looks like sour grapes, the new version is shipping, and the audience has moved on. The structural countermeasure is not to fight individual delays but to invest early in provenance infrastructure — dated written disclosures circulated to neutral witnesses, parallel paths around the gatekeeper, public commitment of the idea before the steal can outrun the originator. The realpolitik observation: where attribution is contestable, control over the alternative's tempo is more valuable than the merit of one's own work.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when diagnosing or defending against an active intra-organizational pattern — the named actors, the named gates, the named parallel team — because intervention requires identifying the procedural chokepoints and routing around them. Use the generic lens when designing institutions or contracts that aim to make this class of move structurally harder — provenance registries, priority-date conventions, blind review, fixed-clock evaluation windows, and conflict-of-interest separation between gatekeepers and competing executors. The two lenses serve different actors: the operator needs the specific diagnosis to survive the current play, while the institution-designer needs the generic frame to prevent the next one.
