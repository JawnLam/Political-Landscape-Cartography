# 0676 — THE 'FAKE RECRUITMENT' PLOY

> Launching a highly visible external search for a role currently occupied by an internal resistor, undermining their authority and signaling their imminent replacement.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C41 — Termination Tactics, Departure Engineering & Resignation Politics

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Schelling, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic names a concrete organizational maneuver (a visible external search for a specifically-occupied role) but the operative mechanism — manufacturing a credible signal of imminent replacement to collapse a resistor's standing — is a portable pattern. The title locks the move into HR/recruitment vocabulary; the mechanism transcends it.

**Specific Interpretation.** An executive or HR principal posts, publicizes, or sources for a role currently filled by an internal incumbent who has been blocking a desired direction, using the public search itself — not its outcome — to delegitimize the incumbent and shake loose their de facto veto.

**Generic Interpretation.** Any costly, visible signal that a chosen actor's position is about to be filled by someone else, deployed not to actually replace them but to degrade their authority in the present moment so they fold, exit, or are abandoned by their own coalition.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The recruitment apparatus is the costume; the operating mechanism is credible-replacement signaling as a status-degradation play. The same pattern shows up in cabinet shuffles leaked to the press, leaks of vendor RFPs aimed at a sole-source incumbent, and parallel-track diplomacy meant to spook a counterparty — none of which are 'recruitment' but all of which do the same work. The mechanism is what travels.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The internal resistor concedes on the contested issue, softens their veto, takes a graceful exit, or quietly stops blocking
- **Option Y:** The internal resistor continues to occupy the role and resist the desired direction
- **Context:** An executive sponsor cannot fire a senior internal blocker outright — cause is weak, the person has tenure or political capital, or termination would be expensive — and instead launches a visible external search for that exact role, framed as 'succession planning,' 'building bench strength,' or 'exploring the market.' The blocker's reports, peers, and counterparties all see the listing.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

Nothing about the maneuver makes capitulating positively attractive — there is no carrot, no offer, no upgraded narrative for the resistor who folds. The tactic is engineered to make resistance unsustainable rather than to make compliance appealing, which is part of why it preserves deniability ('we never asked them to do anything').

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Once a public search exists, a quiet resignation or step-aside becomes a face-saving move ('I'd already been thinking about my next chapter') rather than a defeat. The tactic constructs an exit ramp the resistor can take without admitting they were pushed, lowering the personal and reputational cost of giving way.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The role itself begins to bleed value the moment a search is public — direct reports stop investing in long-horizon initiatives, peers route around the incumbent, vendors slow their relationship-building, and the incumbent's calendar quietly empties of consequential meetings. Resistance from a lame-duck seat is worth less every day the search stays live.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Continuing to occupy the role becomes humiliating in a way pure obstruction is not. Every day the resistor shows up while their replacement is being interviewed compounds a public loss of face, signals to their network that they have been discounted, and forces them to perform authority they manifestly no longer have. Their family, recruiters, and own coalition begin treating them as someone on the way out.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration is the signature of a tactic that cannot afford to look like a direct attack on the target's merits. Promoting concession (B(X)↑) would require articulating a positive case for the resistor's surrender, which would expose the sponsor's hand; instead, the play quietly opens an exit door (C(X)↓), simultaneously hollows the disputed seat (B(Y)↓), and loads the social cost of staying onto every passing day (C(Y)↑). The resistor is degraded into stepping aside rather than persuaded into doing so.

**Effectiveness factors.** Works when the sponsor can credibly fund and staff an actual search (so the signal is not dismissed as theater), when the incumbent has reputational skin in the game (mid- to late-career professionals are more vulnerable than founders or owner-operators), and when third parties — board, key clients, direct reports — can plausibly read the search as legitimate succession. Fails when the incumbent has hard contractual protection, when they own client or technical relationships that don't transfer to a successor, when they can flip the narrative ('they're trying to push me out for raising concerns about X'), or when the search runs so long without a hire that it reveals itself as a bluff.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Schelling's costly signaling and credible commitment, fused with Goffman's frontstage impression management — and reinforced by Pfeffer's analysis of authority as a continuously-maintained social fiction. A position of power is sustained by others' belief that it will persist; a credible, expensive public signal that it will not persist collapses the belief, and with it the power, before any actual replacement occurs.

**Decision Alteration.** The tactic does not change the target's preferences; it changes the social ledger of every third party the target depends on. Once peers, subordinates, and counterparties privately conclude the target is being phased out, they begin to ration the deference, information, and cooperation that constitute the target's effective authority. The target then faces a degraded role from which to resist, while a quiet exit becomes the only face-saving move left.

**Optimal Conditions.** Hierarchies and markets where positions are publicly named and successions are publicly observable; environments with active gossip channels that propagate the signal faster than the target can counter it; targets whose authority depends more on consent than on contract; and sponsors who can pay the real cost of the signal (an actual search, an actual RFP, an actual parallel negotiation) credibly enough that observers cannot dismiss it as bluff.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A consumer brand under pressure on margins wants to bring its longstanding ad agency to heel. Rather than open a hostile negotiation, the CMO commissions a highly public agency review, briefs three rivals, and lets the trade press cover it. The incumbent reads the trades the same morning the brand's procurement team calls about the renewal.

**Sample X:** The incumbent agency quietly accepts a steep fee cut and a constrained scope of work to retain the account

**Sample Y:** The incumbent agency holds its pricing and pushes back on the client's creative direction

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — No additional inducement is offered for capitulation — the agency is not being courted, it is being measured against rivals

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Accepting the cut while a public review is live can be framed internally as a strategic decision to defend the relationship, not as a capitulation

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Holding the line is worth less when the client has already shown the door to three competitors — the relationship value the agency was protecting has already been depreciated

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — A push-back while a public review is running risks the account, and the trade-press coverage means losing it would be career-damaging for the named partner

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A prime minister governing in coalition is being blocked by a small partner on a flagship piece of legislation. Rather than threaten the partner directly, the PM's office discreetly briefs lobby journalists that exploratory talks are underway with a rival opposition party about a 'confidence and supply' arrangement. The story runs in three weekend papers.

**Sample X:** The coalition partner agrees to support a contentious bill in exchange for minor face-saving amendments

**Sample Y:** The coalition partner blocks the bill on principle

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The partner is not offered new policy concessions or ministerial positions — there is no positive bargain, only a deteriorating one

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Accepting a face-saving amendment lets the partner's leader claim a win to their base rather than an outright surrender

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The leverage of blocking the bill evaporates once it becomes credible that the PM has another path to a majority

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Continuing to block risks the partner's expulsion from government and the loss of all ministerial portfolios their MPs currently hold

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Operates anywhere a position derives value from being acknowledged as durable — corporate roles, government contracts, vendor relationships, coalition seats, marriage, alliance treaties, sole-source supply arrangements. Less effective where positions are protected by hard law (tenured judges, lifetime appointments, ownership stakes) or where the target has nowhere to lose face because their constituency does not consume the signal (a founder reporting only to themselves cannot be lame-ducked by a search).

### Core Strategy

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A new CEO commissions a 'capability assessment' by an external consultancy that interviews candidates for every direct report's role, framed as benchmarking but read by every executive as a search
- Government: A White House leaks that the president is 'considering options' for a cabinet position whose occupant has publicly disagreed with policy, accelerating the secretary's resignation without a firing
- Sales/Procurement: A purchasing department issues a public RFP to a sole-source incumbent's competitors not because it intends to switch but to extract a price concession before contract renewal
- Diplomacy: A small state quietly opens talks with the rival of its current security patron, ensuring the talks are visible, in order to renegotiate the terms of its existing alliance
- Academia: A dean creates a search committee for a 'co-director' of an institute run by a powerful but obstructionist senior faculty member, signaling diluted authority without removing the chair
- Family business: A founder publicly engages an executive search firm to find a 'COO to professionalize operations' as a way of telling their adult child running the business that the role is now contested

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both lenses converge on the same lever configuration (Resistance Triple) and the same diagnostic feature: the absence of B(X)↑. This is not a sales tactic — it never tries to make the target's surrender attractive. Recognizing that absence is the way to tell this play apart from its cluster siblings: the 'Fake Job Offer' (which seduces the target out, B(X)↑ heavy), the 'Preemptive Surrender Offer' (which buys cooperation with severance, B(X)↑ + C(X)↓), and the 'External Recruiter Ploy' (which recruits the target away rather than around them). The Fake Recruitment Ploy is the cluster's coldest member because it works through public degradation of a seat, not private inducement of a person.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic only functions because authority in most organizations is a Schelling equilibrium maintained by mutual belief among third parties. The sponsor never has to confront the target; the third parties — the resistor's own reports, peers, and customers — do the degradation work the moment they update their belief about the target's future. This means the tactic's real cost is not the search itself but the signal's credibility budget: every public move that doesn't culminate in an actual decision burns credibility for the next time. Practitioners who use it routinely find the same observers discount their signals on subsequent rounds, eroding the very mechanism that made it work.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when actually staring at a recruitment posting and trying to read intent — the choice architecture (who is being signaled to, what the listing's wording protects, how long the search will plausibly stay 'live') yields actionable detail. Use the generic lens when looking at a non-HR move that smells the same way — a leaked RFP, a parallel-track negotiation, a public 'review of options' — because recognizing it as a Resistance Triple via credible-replacement signaling lets you apply countermoves from the broader pattern: force the bluff (demand a timeline), poison the signal's credibility, or convert the public threat into a private bargain on terms more favorable than capitulation.
