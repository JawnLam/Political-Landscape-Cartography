# 0613 — THE 'A-TEAM' HOARDING

> Systematically allocating the highest-performing individuals exclusively to favored initiatives, while deliberately staffing rival projects with mediocre or difficult personnel to engineer performance disparities.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#15 Full Spectrum** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C47 — Strategic Incompetence Deployment & Talent Contamination

**Cited principles:** Pfeffer, Sun Tzu, Ross

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description blends a specific corporate-political scenario (favored vs. rival initiatives within a firm, staffed asymmetrically) with an ancient and broadly transferable principle of strategic resource concentration — loading elite assets into a chosen front while starving (or actively poisoning) the rival front. Both readings illuminate the tactic.

**Specific Interpretation.** Analyzes the talent-allocation decision a sponsor or operating chief makes when two internal initiatives compete for organizational resources, where staffing choices are the lever used to determine which initiative 'wins' on apparent merit.

**Generic Interpretation.** Analyzes the general pattern of engineering visible performance disparities between two parallel efforts by stacking one with elite resources and burdening the other with weak or toxic resources, then letting attribution bias do the political work.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While the corporate 'favored vs. rival initiatives' framing is the modern setting, the core mechanism — concentration of elite assets on the front you want to win plus deliberate degradation of the rival front — is Sun Tzu's principle applied to organizational life and reappears wherever a decision-maker controls both sides of a comparison. The portability of the mechanism makes the generic reading more fundamental.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Stakeholders judge the favored initiative as the superior, viable, fundable path and the rival initiative as the weaker bet to wind down
- **Option Y:** Stakeholders judge both initiatives on a level staffing playing field and let intrinsic merit decide resource allocation
- **Context:** A division head or sponsor controlling staffing decisions across two internal initiatives that will be compared on outcome metrics by a governance body (steering committee, board, executive team) before the next budget or roadmap cycle

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Concentrating the highest performers on the favored initiative materially raises its delivery velocity, output quality, and visible wins — the actor is not just claiming X is better, they are engineering it to actually perform better within the comparison window

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

A-players reduce execution friction, escalations, and rework cycles for X — fewer fires for the sponsor to fight, fewer cross-functional dependencies that stall, less management overhead per unit of progress

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Starving the rival initiative of top talent caps its achievable output regardless of its underlying strategic merit; the benefits that would have materialized under competent staffing simply never become visible to the governance body

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

'Mediocre or difficult personnel' is the operative phrase — actively loading the rival with friction-generators (toxic peers, low performers, chronic complainers) does not merely deprive Y of upside but injects active drag: interpersonal conflicts, missed deadlines, escalations that consume sponsor attention and damage Y's reputation independent of its merits

### Strategy

**#15 Full Spectrum** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** All four levers genuinely operate because the actor controls both sides of the comparison and exercises that control on both the asset side (A-players to X) and the liability side (difficult personnel to Y). This is one of the rare configurations where Full Spectrum is warranted: the tactic's defining move is the double-loading — concentration AND contamination — and either alone would be a different, lesser tactic.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires the actor to have unilateral or near-unilateral staffing authority across both initiatives, a governance body that evaluates initiatives on output metrics without auditing inputs, a sufficient performance distribution in the talent pool to make differential staffing consequential, and a comparison window short enough that the contaminated initiative cannot recover or that no one can demand a re-staffing. Most likely to fail when an HR analytics function tracks staffing quality, when displaced A-players or burdened B-team leaders escalate openly, or when the contaminated initiative happens to draw a project lead skilled enough to neutralize the loaded deck.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer's Resource Dependence Theory combined with Fundamental Attribution Error (Ross). The tactic exploits observers' tendency to attribute outcome differences between two efforts to the efforts' intrinsic merit (dispositional attribution) rather than to the structural conditions — staffing, support, resourcing — under which each effort was executed (situational attribution). Sun Tzu's principle of concentration of force ('When ten to one, surround them') is the strategic ancestor.

**Decision Alteration.** Shifts the decision-maker from a merit judgment to a rigged comparison they don't perceive as rigged. By making X visibly outperform Y on observable metrics during the evaluation window, the actor lets the decision-maker reach what feels like an evidence-based conclusion — when in fact the evidence was manufactured upstream through resource allocation. The decision feels objective; it isn't.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best where one party controls the staffing of both compared options, where outcomes are evaluated on output metrics without input audits, where the talent distribution is heterogeneous enough that A-versus-mediocre allocation produces visible performance gaps, and where the evaluation window is short enough that structural causes don't surface.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A mid-cap technology company where the CEO controls all engineering hiring and reassignment decisions and is positioning to consolidate strategic authority away from the product organization headed by an internal rival

**Sample X:** The CEO's preferred 'platform modernization' initiative ships visible wins each quarter and earns continued board investment

**Sample Y:** The competing 'edge-product expansion' initiative championed by a rival SVP stalls and is wound down at the next strategic review

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Highest-rated engineers and the strongest product manager are quietly transferred onto the platform initiative during a 'reorg,' raising its actual shipping velocity

- C(X)↓ ✓ — A-team staffing reduces escalations to the CEO and to dependent teams, so the platform initiative consumes less of the executive bandwidth that would otherwise constrain it

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Edge-product expansion is left with junior engineers and a first-time product manager, capping its ability to deliver the differentiated outcomes its business case depended on

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Two known low-performers and one disruptive senior engineer are reassigned onto the edge-product team, generating the interpersonal conflicts and missed milestones that become the public reason for winding it down

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A defense procurement environment in which a service-branch acquisition chief controls assignment of senior program managers and lead engineers across two competing development programs evaluated by Congressional oversight

**Sample X:** The Pentagon's preferred next-generation fighter program produces successful test results and locks in multi-year funding

**Sample Y:** A competing UAV-swarm program loses funding after a series of public failures and is folded into the larger program

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The most decorated program managers and the most experienced systems engineers are assigned to the favored fighter program, raising its probability of producing the demonstrable wins Congress will see

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Strong leadership on the fighter program reduces the number of crisis reviews, GAO findings, and inter-service disputes that would otherwise consume the acquisition chief's political capital

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The UAV-swarm program is staffed with managers passed over for promotion and engineers nearing retirement, ensuring its technical ceiling is far below what the underlying technology could otherwise achieve

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — A program manager with a known history of contractor disputes is placed on the UAV-swarm program, producing the missed milestones and contractor escalations that become the documented case for cancellation

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any context with parallel competing efforts, a single allocating authority controlling resources to both, and an evaluation body judging outcomes without auditing inputs. Spans corporate strategy, defense procurement, academic department politics, political-campaign field operations, sports franchise management, NGO grant programs, and family-business succession trials. Less effective in environments with independent staffing authority for each initiative, strong input audits (HR analytics, governance committees that track staffing quality), or short enough decision cycles that re-staffing remedies are visible.

### Application Examples

- A pharmaceutical division head assigning star clinical leads to the molecule the head champions for the next pipeline review while loading a rival molecule's trial with a project manager known for missed deadlines
- A university dean staffing the favored interdisciplinary institute with star faculty granted teaching releases while filling a rival department's faculty lines with adjuncts and toxic colleagues facing tenure denial
- A presidential campaign manager deploying top field organizers to the candidate's preferred primary states while seeding rival in-house factions' state operations with inexperienced staff and internal critics
- A general allocating elite combat units to the front the high command wants to advance while assigning logistically broken or undisciplined units to a secondary front the command wants quietly abandoned
- A foundation program officer routing the strongest grantees and most experienced evaluators to a favored grant portfolio while assigning weak grantees and a problem evaluator to a competing portfolio the officer wants to phase out
- An NFL general manager stacking the offensive line and surrounding the franchise quarterback with elite skill players while leaving a rival rookie quarterback behind a thin line with a journeyman receiving corps, then citing comparative performance as evidence for the franchise decision

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The tactic's defining feature is that the actor controls both sides of a comparison and rigs both sides — concentrating elite resources on X and deliberately contaminating Y — then lets observers reach the 'merit' conclusion the staffing has predetermined. Within its sibling cluster, it sits as the mirror image of the bad-apple seeding and incompetence-cultivation tactics: those tactics degrade a single target, while A-Team Hoarding always operates in pairs because the comparative outcome is the political product. The doubled action — stacking AND seeding — is what distinguishes Full Spectrum here from the simpler single-front resource plays.

### Strategic Implications

Because all four levers operate, the tactic produces uncommonly durable political results: the decision-maker's conclusion is anchored not in rhetoric but in observable performance data, which is difficult to contest after the fact. The vulnerability is upstream — at the staffing decision itself — and is visible only to actors who think to audit inputs rather than outputs. Governance bodies that ask 'who was on each team and how were they chosen' before asking 'how did each team perform' neutralize this tactic; bodies that ask only the second question are systematically deceived by it. The human cost is unusually high: the contaminated initiative's team members are made to be the visible explanation for a failure they were structurally set up to deliver, and their careers carry the marks the staffing rig produced.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing a single observed staffing decision across competing initiatives within a known organization — the question is whether a particular comparison has been rigged and what governance intervention would surface it. Use the generic lens when teaching the broader pattern, designing input audits to prevent it, or recognizing it across very different domains (military allocation, campaign field operations, sports staffing) where the same Full Spectrum mechanism operates under different surface vocabulary.
