# 0618 — THE 'TIGER TEAM' ASSAULT

> Deploying a specialized, high-authority 'Tiger Team' to take over a critical problem, bypassing the existing hierarchy and undermining the authority of the local leadership.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C51 — Coalition Building, In-Group Cartels & Committee Stacking

**Cited principles:** Goffman, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description names a specific organizational artifact (a 'Tiger Team' — a defined construct from operations and crisis management) but the underlying move is a generic pattern of installing a parallel authority structure to override an incumbent one under the cover of urgency. The 'critical problem' frame is a context-specific anchor; the mechanism of authority-substitution-via-specialized-override is reusable wherever hierarchies can be circumvented by appeals to exigency.

**Specific Interpretation.** A senior sponsor commissions a small, high-authority, cross-functional team — branded as elite and time-limited — to seize operational control of a problem domain currently owned by a line leader. The Tiger Team's mandate explicitly bypasses normal reporting lines, and its very existence publicly signals that the incumbent leader has been judged inadequate to the moment.

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents the broader pattern of authority substitution by exigency: framing a situation as a crisis requiring exceptional measures, then installing a parallel decision-making structure whose mere existence delegitimizes the displaced authority. The specialized vehicle (task force, czar, special prosecutor, troika, war cabinet) varies; the structural move — overriding routine hierarchy via emergency-credentialed alternates — does not.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While 'Tiger Team' has corporate-operational provenance, the substantive mechanism — using an exigency frame to install a parallel authority that displaces an incumbent without firing them — recurs in government (czars, special prosecutors), military (joint task forces overriding service chains), and political life (war cabinets, blue-ribbon commissions). The reusable principle is more fundamental than the specific organizational vehicle, so generic is the deeper read.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** The organization endorses and resources the Tiger Team's takeover of the problem domain; the incumbent line leader cooperates with — or at least does not openly oppose — the new structure
- **Option Y:** The incumbent line leader retains operational ownership of the problem; the organization works the issue through the existing hierarchy and rejects the parallel structure
- **Context:** A line leader (a divisional VP, a country manager, a program director) owns a domain where results have slipped, are politically embarrassing, or are too slow for a senior sponsor's timeline. The sponsor lacks clean grounds — or political appetite — to fire the leader outright, so installing a credentialed override team becomes the substitute for direct action

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The Tiger Team is staged with deliberate prestige markers — handpicked talent, executive sponsorship, expedited resourcing, direct reporting to the top — making the takeover route look like the organization's serious answer to a serious problem. Endorsing it lets stakeholders be seen aligning with decisive action rather than defending a sluggish status quo

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Cooperating with a Tiger Team that has been parachuted into one's domain is operationally and politically expensive for the incumbent — loss of face, reduced staff loyalty, future hiring difficulty, and the implicit admission of failure. The tactic does not lower the cost of acquiescence; it relies on the alternative being more costly still

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The Tiger Team's very chartering says, on the record, that the existing hierarchy has not produced results. Every day the line leader retains formal ownership while the Tiger Team works the problem, the leader's authority is hollowed out — the option of 'leave it with the line' now looks like organizational denial, not stewardship

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

A line leader who openly resists the Tiger Team appears to be protecting turf during a crisis the sponsor has publicly named. The leader is forced to argue that the problem is less urgent than the sponsor says — a position that, if wrong, ends careers, and if right, looks defensive. Peers and direct reports observing the contest discount the leader's future political weight accordingly

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration concentrates pressure on the alternative (continued line ownership) while burnishing the override route. The sponsor does not need to lower the cost of acceptance — they need only make resistance career-suicidal and continued ownership organizationally embarrassing. The benefit of the Tiger Team route is talked up via prestige staging; the line leader is then placed in a contest they can only lose with dignity by yielding

**Effectiveness factors.** Works when the sponsor has the standing to publicly name a 'crisis,' when the Tiger Team can be stocked with credible operators (not obvious loyalists), and when the line leader lacks board-level protection. Fails when the 'crisis' frame collapses under scrutiny (problem turns out to be ordinary), when the Tiger Team underdelivers and exposes the sponsor, or when the displaced leader has enough institutional capital to make their resistance read as principled rather than defensive

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer's structural power thesis combined with Goffman's stigma / spoiled-identity dynamics. Pfeffer holds that power flows from structural position and the ability to define problems; chartering a parallel authority is a direct act of structural power, redefining who 'owns' the issue. Goffman supplies the second mechanism — the displaced incumbent is publicly marked as the one whose competence required override, a status that adheres regardless of the eventual operational outcome

**Decision Alteration.** Shifts the target's choice from 'continue managing my domain' versus 'cede authority' into a forced contest between 'cooperate with the override and salvage dignity' versus 'fight the override and be branded as the obstacle to a sponsor-named crisis.' The frame removes the option of business-as-usual: by the time the Tiger Team exists, the prior status quo has already been adjudicated as failure. The incumbent's only remaining variable is whether to exit gracefully or be seen losing

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in environments where (a) a senior sponsor can credibly invoke exigency, (b) parallel structures are culturally legitimate (consulting cultures, military, federal government, large multinationals), (c) the incumbent is politically inconvenient to fire directly, and (d) sufficient organizational slack exists to staff a high-quality override team without visibly stripping other priorities

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A multinational industrial firm where the APAC region has missed three consecutive quarterly targets. The APAC president is a long-tenured insider with strong board relationships, so a direct firing would trigger a destabilizing succession fight at the board level

**Sample X:** The board approves the CEO's appointment of a Transformation Office that reports directly to the CEO and assumes decision rights over the Asia-Pacific region's restructuring

**Sample Y:** The board defers to the existing APAC regional president to lead the restructuring through normal channels with quarterly check-ins

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — The Transformation Office is staffed with named external operators and former turnaround specialists, making it look like the organization is finally getting serious about APAC after years of soft tolerance

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Standing up the office is expensive — relocations, premium compensation, opportunity cost of pulled internal talent. The tactic does not reduce this cost; it justifies it via the crisis frame

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Letting the APAC president keep leading after three missed quarters now reads as patience-as-paralysis; the benefit of incumbent continuity has been spent

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any board member who blocks the Transformation Office now owns the next missed quarter personally, and the APAC president's defense of his own tenure looks like an executive protecting himself rather than the business

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A federal administration under media pressure on overdose deaths, where the HHS Secretary is a respected veteran with strong Senate ties and cannot be moved without cost. The President needs to be seen acting now without a confirmation fight

**Sample X:** The President signs an executive order creating a White House Opioid Response Coordinator with cross-agency authority over HHS, DOJ, and DEA workstreams on the crisis

**Sample Y:** The administration leaves opioid response to the existing HHS Secretary and the standing interagency working group

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Appointing a high-profile coordinator generates immediate press coverage of presidential action — the symbolic benefit lands before any operational result is required

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Inter-agency authority overlays produce real coordination friction and turf battles; the tactic does not eliminate these, only legitimates them under the crisis banner

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Continuing to route response through the HHS Secretary now signals insufficient urgency, regardless of what the Secretary is actually doing — the political value of incumbent stewardship is depreciated by the coordinator's mere existence

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any Cabinet officer who openly resists the coordinator's authority becomes the public face of bureaucratic obstruction to a presidentially named crisis — a stance that is rarely survivable in a media cycle

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable wherever (a) hierarchies exist, (b) exigency can be credibly invoked, and (c) a sponsor has authority to charter parallel structures. Crosses corporate (transformation offices, war rooms), government (czars, special prosecutors, blue-ribbon commissions), military (joint task forces overriding service chains), academic (presidential task forces overriding deans), and religious (apostolic visitations overriding diocesan bishops). Less effective in flat organizations or partnerships where 'parallel authority' has no structural meaning, and in cultures where naming a crisis itself carries unacceptable loss-of-face costs (some East Asian corporate contexts)

### Application Examples

- Corporate: a private equity sponsor installing a 100-day plan team that reports to the deal partner and overrides the portfolio company CEO on every working-capital decision
- Government: appointing a special prosecutor who absorbs jurisdiction from a U.S. Attorney's office that is judged too compromised or too slow on a politically charged matter
- Military: standing up a Joint Special Operations task force with direct lines to combatant command, displacing the service component commander who would normally own the area of operations
- Academic: a university president convening a 'rapid response committee' on antisemitism or campus safety that reports to the president and bypasses the standing dean of students
- Diplomacy: a head of state dispatching a personal envoy to a negotiation, sidelining the resident ambassador whose access has produced no movement
- Activism: a national civil-rights organization parachuting a senior litigation team into a local chapter's case, displacing local counsel judged insufficiently aggressive
- Religious: the Vatican commissioning an apostolic visitation of a diocese, with investigators reporting to Rome and the local bishop reduced to formal cooperation

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The Tiger Team Assault is structurally an act of authority substitution disguised as problem-solving. The operational output of the team is almost secondary to the political output of its existence — chartering it is itself the move. Within the cluster, it is the offensive twin of THE 'REVOLUTIONARY GUARD' CREATION (which builds a loyal cadre to protect a leader) and a hierarchical cousin of THE 'HOSTILE TAKEOVER' OF A COMMITTEE (which captures an existing body rather than building a new one). What distinguishes the Tiger Team from these siblings is that it leaves the displaced authority nominally in place, drawing power away without ever requiring a vote, a firing, or a public confrontation

### Strategic Implications

The tactic externalizes the political cost of confronting an incumbent onto the incumbent themselves: the sponsor never had to fire anyone, and any damage to the displaced leader can be attributed to the crisis, not to the sponsor's hand. This is why the tactic is preferred when an incumbent has board protection, civil-service tenure, or strong external relationships — it accomplishes what direct removal cannot. The corresponding vulnerability for the sponsor is that the Tiger Team must produce visible results; if it underdelivers, the manufactured crisis frame collapses backward onto the sponsor, who must then explain both why the alarm was sounded and why their hand-picked override failed. Counter-play for the incumbent is rarely open resistance — which the tactic is specifically designed to make suicidal — but rather rapid public co-sponsorship of the Tiger Team's existence while quietly working to be the one credited with its results

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing concrete instances of override-team chartering: who is the sponsor, who is being displaced, what 'crisis' is being named, and what protective relationships make direct action impossible. Use the generic lens when studying the broader pattern of authority substitution by exigency across institutional types, or when designing organizational guardrails (charter-and-sunset clauses, standing oversight of task force creation) that constrain the tactic's misuse. The two lenses converge on the same strategic configuration — Y-Focused Triple — because the tactic's signature is precisely its asymmetry: it punishes the alternative far harder than it reduces the cost of compliance
