# 0678 — THE 'REVERSE POACHING' AGREEMENT

> Secretly colluding with friendly external organizations to not hire talent leaving the company, limiting the exit options for dissatisfied employees.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C53 — External Actors as Political Instruments (Consultants, Vendors, Validators)

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The title and description anchor in a specific employment-context move (a non-poach pact between peer firms aimed at trapping disgruntled employees), but the underlying mechanism is the generic collusive suppression of an actor's exit market — a pattern that recurs wherever competitors quietly agree not to compete for someone's defection.

**Specific Interpretation.** A bounded corporate manoeuvre in which executives, HR leaders, or founders covertly arrange with peer companies, recruiters, or industry friends to decline applications from their departing staff, narrowing the realistic external labour market and thereby making resignation a dead-end gamble.

**Generic Interpretation.** A generalised cartel-style restraint on an alternative: the actor cannot foreclose the alternative directly, so they coordinate with the gatekeepers of that alternative to foreclose it on the actor's behalf — converting a competitive exit market into a closed loop.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The employment context provides the most legible illustration, but the operative principle — quietly buying off the destinations to which someone could defect — generalises to vendor pools, capital markets, university placements, and political coalitions. The mechanism (collusive obstruction of the alternative) is what's reusable; the labour-market dressing is one instance of it.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Stay at the current employer, swallow grievances, and accept whatever terms management offers
- **Option Y:** Resign and pursue employment at a peer firm in the same industry or geography
- **Context:** A mid-to-senior employee at a firm in a concentrated industry (e.g., a specialist VP at one of four regional banks, a partner at a boutique consultancy in a tight peer set, an engineer in a small geographic tech cluster) becomes dissatisfied — over pay, scope, ethics, or treatment — and begins quietly testing the external market while the employer becomes aware and activates back-channel agreements with peer-firm leaders to ensure the employee's CV is quietly binned wherever they apply.

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The current employer does nothing to make staying more rewarding — no raise, no promotion, no expanded mandate. The tactic deliberately avoids the cost of actually improving the X-side offer; that's its appeal to the perpetrator. The benefit of staying is unchanged in absolute terms and only appears better by comparison to a foreclosed alternative.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

Staying is not made easier or less costly — in many cases the very grievances that triggered the exit search are still active. The friction, indignity, or underpayment of the current role persists. The tactic operates by closing the back door, not by sweetening the front room.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The headline benefit of resignation — a clean move to a comparable peer firm — is structurally erased. Even if interviews happen, offers do not materialise; the apparent payoff of leaving collapses into 'a long, fruitless job search of unknown duration.' The employee may still pursue distant or downmarket roles, so the benefit isn't zeroed, but the most attractive proximate destinations stop functioning as real options.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the operating core. Leaving now carries the hidden cost of an invisible blacklist: lost income during an extended search, reputational damage as rejection patterns become legible to one's network, the psychological cost of repeated unexplained ghosting, and the possibility of having to relocate, downshift industries, or accept a lower-tier employer. The employee bears the full cost of a market failure they cannot see, name, or prove.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration is signature Y Degradation: the employer makes no investment in the X-side (no retention bonus, no improved terms, no career conversation) and instead operates exclusively on the alternative — quietly hollowing out the perceived value of resignation while loading it with hidden cost. The employee discovers, by trial, that the door they thought was open has been welded from the outside. The choice shifts not because staying became better but because leaving became untenable.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effective when the industry has a small peer set with personal relationships at the top (so a quiet phone call can reach the relevant decision-makers), when the employee's role is specialised enough that the peer set IS the realistic market, when the collusion can be conducted via deniable back channels (golf, alumni networks, board overlaps) rather than written agreement, and when the employee lacks the legal sophistication or evidence to allege antitrust violation. Fails when the labour market is deep and geographically dispersed (the peer set isn't comprehensive enough to matter), when a single defector breaks ranks for self-interested reasons, when antitrust regulators or class-action lawyers get visibility (the US DOJ's Silicon Valley no-poach case showed the legal downside), or when a high-profile target's blocked moves themselves become evidence.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Monopsony / Collusive Restraint of an Alternative — drawing on the economics of cartel coordination (Stigler's theory of collusion; the no-poach economics literature) and Albert Hirschman's Exit/Voice/Loyalty framework. The tactic operates on the supply side of the exit market: by capturing the gatekeepers of the alternative, it converts what looks like a competitive multi-option environment into a closed loop the target cannot detect from inside.

**Decision Alteration.** The target's expected-value calculation for choosing Y is built on an assumption — that competing destinations exist and will entertain them on merit. The tactic invalidates that assumption silently. The target updates beliefs only after repeated failures, by which point sunk costs (time, burned bridges, signalling damage) make returning to a wholehearted X commitment the path of least resistance. The decision shift is achieved not by argument but by removing the alternative's option-value while leaving its surface appearance intact.

**Optimal Conditions.** Requires (a) a small, knowable set of gatekeepers controlling the alternative — peer-firm CEOs, recruiter networks, venture syndicates, accreditation bodies; (b) personal or reciprocal-favour relationships among those gatekeepers; (c) a culture of deniability where the coordination can be conducted off the record; (d) information asymmetry — the target cannot see why they're being rejected. Breaks down in deep, distributed markets with many independent gatekeepers, or where rejection patterns are auditable.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** The four firms' regional managing partners maintain a standing informal understanding — reinforced annually at an industry dinner — not to actively recruit from each other's senior ranks 'to protect industry stability.' When the associate partner discreetly signals availability, her CV reaches each firm but stops at the managing-partner desk for a quiet veto.

**Sample X:** An associate partner stays at her current Big Four advisory firm despite a known toxic practice leader

**Sample Y:** She lateral-moves to one of the other three Big Four firms in the same city for the same service line

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Her current firm does not respond with a counter-offer or a leadership change — the toxic practice leader remains. The X-side is materially unchanged.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Staying continues to cost her in stress, lost mentorship, and damaged engagement scores. The tactic does not lower this cost.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The promise of a fresh start at a peer firm — same comp band, same client base, healthier culture — becomes hollow. The benefit is technically still on paper but unattainable in practice.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each rejected interview costs reputational capital (the city is small, partners talk), delays her career timeline, and signals to her current firm that she's a flight risk without the leverage of an actual offer.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** The cleared-engineer market in a particular speciality is tiny — perhaps 400 people nationally across four primes — and the primes' chief engineering officers maintain a tacit non-aggression pact reinforced by their shared dependence on the same customer (a government agency that dislikes 'instability' in cleared workforces). The engineer's applications go unanswered or receive boilerplate rejections.

**Sample X:** A defence-contractor mid-career engineer remains at his current employer despite ethical concerns about a programme

**Sample Y:** He moves to one of the three other primes bidding on adjacent contracts

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — No ethical changes are made to the programme; his current role does not improve.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The moral cost of staying — which prompted the search — is unaddressed.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The expected upside of switching primes (cleaner programme, fresh team, comparable clearance use) evaporates because the gates are closed at all peer destinations.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Cleared specialists have narrow off-ramps; failed lateral attempts can damage clearance reciprocity, signal disloyalty, and waste the multi-year clearance-portability window.

### Versatility

**Level:** moderate

**Explanation.** Highly effective in concentrated markets with few gatekeepers and dense relational ties at the top (specialist labour markets, narrow industries, capital-controlled sectors). Loses force in deep, fragmented, or geographically distributed markets where no small clique can foreclose enough of the alternative to matter. Also loses force where rejection patterns are observable to regulators — antitrust regimes (US, EU, UK CMA) have specifically targeted no-poach and wage-fixing collusion. Versatility is therefore structural, not universal: the tactic needs an oligopsonistic shape on the alternative side to work.

### Core Strategy

### Application Examples

- Corporate: The 2005–2009 Silicon Valley no-poach pacts (Apple, Google, Adobe, Intel, Intuit, Pixar) where executives agreed not to cold-call each other's engineers, suppressing wages and exit mobility until a US DOJ antitrust case and a $415M class settlement broke the arrangement.
- Sports: Major-league owners running an informal collusion regime (as the 1985–1987 MLB free-agent collusion findings established) where teams quietly agree not to bid on each other's outgoing free agents to depress salaries.
- Academic: A small set of R1 universities in a sub-discipline tacitly declining to hire each other's junior faculty mid-tenure-clock, citing 'collegiality norms,' which traps unhappy assistant professors in their first appointment.
- Venture / capital: A founder who has alienated their lead investor finds that none of the other tier-one VC firms will take a follow-on meeting — the syndicate is too tightly networked, and one whispered 'difficult founder' note closes the round before it opens.
- Diplomacy: A defecting mid-rank intelligence officer discovers that allied services have all declined to receive them after a quiet bilateral request, leaving exile in a neutral third country as the only practical option.
- Family business / private wealth: A junior family-office principal seeking to leave for a peer single-family-office finds the principal-to-principal network has already been alerted by their parents, and the small Geneva–Singapore–New York circuit closes ranks.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both readings of the tactic — the specific HR/labour-market manoeuvre and the generic collusive-foreclosure pattern — converge on the same lever signature (B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑) and the same strategic logic: do not make staying better, make leaving fail. Within the C53 cluster (External Actors as Political Instruments) this sits next to THE FRICTION HIRE and THE 'BURNING BRIDGE' HIRE as a member of the 'manage talent through external coordination' sub-family — but where those tactics import outsiders to shape the inside, this one mobilises outsiders to seal the outside. The cluster lens highlights that the external actors here (peer-firm leaders) are not blame absorbers or legitimisers but gatekeepers of the alternative, which is a distinct political use of external relationships.

### Strategic Implications

Y Degradation tactics that operate through third-party collusion are unusually durable because the target diagnoses the situation wrongly — they read repeated rejections as personal failings, market conditions, or bad luck rather than coordinated suppression. This produces a learned-helplessness response that reinforces the X commitment without the actor ever having to defend the X-side terms. The strategic cost, however, is asymmetric and back-loaded: if the collusion is ever surfaced (a whistleblowing peer executive, an antitrust subpoena, a class-action discovery process), the legal, reputational, and regulatory exposure dwarfs any retention savings — the Silicon Valley case is the canonical cautionary tale. Practitioners drawn to this tactic systematically underweight the discovery risk because the upside (cheap retention) is visible weekly while the downside (litigation) is probabilistic and distant.

### Resolution Approach

Apply the specific lens when diagnosing a single suspected case — a particular employee's blocked moves, a particular industry's quiet pact — to map who the gatekeepers are, what reciprocal favours hold the pact together, and what evidence could surface it. Apply the generic lens when scanning an environment for the structural conditions under which the tactic is even possible (small peer sets, dense relational ties, deniable coordination channels) — those same conditions predict other Y-foreclosure tactics in capital, distribution, and credentialing markets, not just labour. The deepest practitioner takeaway: when an alternative looks freely available but never quite materialises in practice, suspect coordinated obstruction of the alternative before assuming personal failure or market weakness.
