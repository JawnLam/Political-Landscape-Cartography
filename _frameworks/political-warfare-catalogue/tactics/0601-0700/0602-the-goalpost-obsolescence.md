# 0602 — THE GOALPOST OBSOLESCENCE

> Setting performance objectives for a rival that are known to become irrelevant or counter-productive due to upcoming, unannounced strategic shifts, ensuring they waste effort and appear misaligned.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C46 — Setting Up to Fail (Impossible Mandates, Glass Cliffs & Burner Initiatives)

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Akerlof, Arkes & Blumer, Arkes, Blumer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description blends a concrete organizational move (assigning a rival performance objectives) with an abstract strategic-information principle (exploiting an asymmetry between what is publicly known about the future and what the rival doesn't yet know). The mechanism is recognizable in any setting where one actor can lock another onto a path that the actor privately knows is about to be devalued.

**Specific Interpretation.** Focuses on a manager or executive who, in formal performance-management cycles, assigns objectives, KPIs, or projects to an internal rival that the actor knows will be rendered moot by an unannounced reorganization, product-line cancellation, or strategy pivot.

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents the broader pattern of exploiting private knowledge of an imminent regime change to direct a rival's effort toward a soon-stranded asset, weaponizing the gap between current rules and pending rules so the rival looks misaligned at the very moment the new rules become public.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The mechanism — using superior knowledge of pending regime change to channel a rival's commitment onto a soon-obsolete target — generalizes across performance reviews, M&A integration, regulatory transition, military doctrine shifts, and academic funding pivots. The corporate goal-setting context is the most legible example, but the structural prerequisite is information asymmetry about an impending discontinuity, not anything specific to objectives-setting.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Senior leadership reads the rival as misaligned with the new strategic direction and reads the actor as forward-looking, advancing the actor's standing relative to the rival
- **Option Y:** Senior leadership reads the rival as a competent operator who executed against assigned objectives and remains credible despite the pivot
- **Context:** A senior manager who sits inside the strategy-planning circle assigns annual objectives, OKRs, or a flagship initiative to an internal peer or subordinate rival, fully aware that an unannounced restructuring, product sunset, or market-positioning shift will land mid-cycle and make those objectives either irrelevant or actively counter to the new direction

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

When the strategic shift becomes public, the actor — having quietly avoided committing to the soon-obsolete objectives and often having shadow-prepared work that aligns with the new direction — appears prescient by contrast. The rival's stranded effort becomes the visual foil against which the actor's apparent foresight is illuminated.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The tactic does not reduce the actor's actual cost of pursuing alignment with the new strategy. The actor still must do the work of positioning, networking, and producing aligned outputs. The lever's absence is part of the tactic's signature — it operates by degrading the rival's position, not by smoothing the actor's path.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The core operating lever. The rival's diligent execution against the assigned objectives produces deliverables that, when the strategic shift is announced, are no longer valuable to the organization — and in some cases are now liabilities (e.g., a product feature shipped just before the product is cancelled, a market entered just before exit is announced). The benefit of the rival's chosen path is engineered to evaporate.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The rival has burned a full review cycle of effort, headcount, budget, and political capital on objectives that now read as evidence of being out of touch with where the organization is going. The cost is not just the wasted work but the reputational cost of being the visible exemplar of yesterday's strategy at the moment everyone is performing fluency with tomorrow's.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic engineers a temporal mismatch: the rival is locked into a contract (the assigned objectives) whose value will be retroactively destroyed by an event the actor knows is coming. By the time the new direction is announced, the rival cannot un-spend the cycle — the cost of their path is fixed, while its benefit collapses to negative. The actor's standing rises by structural contrast rather than by any positive promotion of self.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires three conditions: (1) the actor has credible access to pending-strategy information the rival does not, (2) the actor has enough authority or proximity to shape the rival's objectives without raising suspicion, and (3) the organization's review culture penalizes mid-cycle pivoting more than it rewards good-faith execution against stated goals. Fails when leakage occurs, when the rival has independent strategic intelligence, or when the organization explicitly forgives stranded effort caused by leadership-level strategy changes.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Akerlof's Information Asymmetry compounded by Arkes & Blumer's Sunk Cost Effect. The actor exploits private knowledge of a pending regime change to induce the rival into a committed-effort position; once committed, the rival's escalation of commitment to the now-doomed path (driven by sunk-cost reasoning) deepens the trap. Schelling's analysis of strategic information advantage is a useful secondary frame — the actor's edge comes not from any direct move but from controlling what the rival believes about the stability of the current rules.

**Decision Alteration.** Alters not the rival's immediate choice but the future value of a choice the rival has already been induced to make. The rival rationally optimizes against the visible objective function, unaware that the function itself is about to be redefined. By the time the new function is revealed, the rival's allocation looks like a deliberate refusal to anticipate change — even though the change was unknowable to them.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in hierarchical organizations with (a) annual or quarterly formal commitment cycles that lock in objectives, (b) opaque or compartmentalized strategy-formation processes that produce material information asymmetry, and (c) evaluation cultures that judge outcomes against current rules rather than against the rules that existed when the work was committed.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** Two months before a major acquisition is publicly announced, the corporate-development executive — who is on the deal team — chairs the strategy review where a divisional president is pressed to commit to an aggressive regional-localization roadmap. The executive knows the acquired company's centralized global platform will replace localization investments organization-wide within six months.

**Sample X:** The corporate-development executive emerges from the post-merger integration looking like the leader who anticipated the new operating model

**Sample Y:** The divisional president who built a year-long localization roadmap looks like the leader who failed to read the room

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — When the acquisition is announced and the global-platform direction is set, the corporate-development executive's quiet refusal to commit to regional localization (and her shadow work on integration architecture) reads as strategic foresight, materially elevating her standing in the new operating model.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. The executive still bears the normal cost of executing the integration. The tactic operates through the rival's loss, not through any reduction in the executive's own effort.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The divisional president's localization deliverables — language packs, regional partnerships, country-specific features — instantly become redundant the moment the acquired global platform's roadmap is communicated, stripping the entire roadmap of its claimed business value.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The president has spent a full cycle of headcount, budget, and political capital on objectives that now read as evidence of strategic deafness. Reversing the work means writing off investment publicly; defending it means defending an obsolete strategy in the new leadership's first all-hands.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Within a military service's annual training-cycle planning, a senior intelligence officer with access to a pending joint-posture review presses a brigade commander to make manned reconnaissance modernization the brigade's marquee training objective for the year — knowing the joint posture, due in eight months, will reclassify manned reconnaissance as a legacy capability and shift resources to unmanned/space-based ISR.

**Sample X:** The senior intelligence officer who advocated drone-based ISR doctrine is seen as the doctrinal modernizer when the new joint posture is published

**Sample Y:** The brigade commander who spent the training year executing the assigned manned-reconnaissance modernization is seen as a doctrinal anachronism

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — When the joint posture is published, the intelligence officer's prior public advocacy for drone-based ISR — produced while the brigade commander was locked into the old doctrine — positions him as the doctrinal modernizer the service now needs.

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Not active. The intelligence officer still has to fight for resources and credibility under the new posture. Nothing about the tactic reduces his own workload or risk.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The brigade's training deliverables — qualified crews, exercise after-action reports, doctrinal contributions to manned reconnaissance — are recategorized overnight from career-building outputs into evidence of misallocation.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — The brigade commander faces the dual cost of a wasted training year and a promotion board that will read his command tour through the lens of a doctrine the service has just disowned. Defending the year's work compounds the damage.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable wherever three structural conditions co-occur: a formal commitment mechanism (objectives, contracts, doctrine, regulatory filings) that fixes a rival's path for a defined period, an opaque process that produces material asymmetry about pending changes to the rules, and an evaluation system that judges outcomes against current rather than original rules. Found in corporate performance management, military doctrine planning, academic grant cycles, regulated-industry investment planning, and political-party platform commitments. Less effective in flat, fast-pivoting organizations where commitment cycles are short and strategy is broadly known.

### Application Examples

- A government department head, on the budget-strategy committee, presses a peer department to commit fiscal-year priorities to a program area the head knows will be cut in the upcoming ministerial reshuffle
- A pharmaceutical R&D head, knowing the board has provisionally approved a therapeutic-area exit, allows a rival division leader to commit a full year's research budget to that area before the exit is announced
- A law firm managing partner steers a competing partner to chase business-development objectives in an industry vertical the firm's executive committee has already decided to deprioritize at the next compensation review
- An academic department chair lets a rival faculty member commit a sabbatical to a research program the chair knows the university's pending strategic plan will defund in favor of a new institutional priority
- A diplomat at a multilateral negotiation lets a rival delegation commit publicly to language that the diplomat's home capital has already decided to abandon in the next negotiating round, leaving the rival exposed when the position shifts
- A regulated-utility CEO permits an internal rival to make a major capex commitment under tariff assumptions the CEO knows the regulator will revise downward in the next rate case
- A political party's strategy director allows a rival politician to build a campaign issue platform around a policy the party leadership has privately decided to abandon at the upcoming convention

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Within this cluster of setting-up-to-fail tactics, the goalpost obsolescence has a distinctive temporal signature: it is the only family member where the rival's failure is engineered not by impossibility (impossible mandate), under-resourcing (unfunded mandate), illusion (ghost deliverable), or fragility (glass cliff), but by latency. The goals are achievable, resourced, real, and stable — they will simply be devalued by an event the rival cannot see coming. Where the impossible mandate damages the rival through visible inadequacy of means, this tactic damages the rival through invisible inadequacy of time horizon. The wound is delivered after the fact, when announcement-day reveals that diligent execution against last-month's rules now reads as failure to anticipate this-month's rules.

### Strategic Implications

The tactic teaches that under conditions of information asymmetry about pending regime change, the most damaging move is not to block a rival but to fund and bless their continued execution against the soon-to-be-obsolete rules. It also exposes a structural vulnerability in any organization that treats commitment to objectives as a virtue independent of strategic context — such cultures convert good-faith execution into political evidence against the executor when the strategy turns. For practitioners, recognition of this pattern reframes 'they assigned me a great project' as a question worth asking: who else, with information I lack, agreed to my taking it on, and why now?

### Resolution Approach

Defense against the specific form requires structural countermeasures more than vigilance: insisting on strategic-context briefings before committing to objectives, building mid-cycle review rights into objective contracts, and cultivating independent intelligence about pending strategic shifts. Defense against the generic form requires reading the meta-question — when an opportunity to commit to a defined path is being unusually facilitated by an actor with privileged information access, the facilitation itself is data. The actor's enthusiasm for your committing is a signal whose information content deserves at least as much scrutiny as the substantive merits of the commitment.
