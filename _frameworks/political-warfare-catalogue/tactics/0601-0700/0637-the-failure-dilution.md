# 0637 — THE FAILURE DILUTION

> Absorbing a failing project into a larger, successful program to mask its poor performance and protect the reputation of its executive sponsors.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C69 — KPI Gaming, Criteria-Shifting & Failure Dilution

**Cited principles:** Akerlof, Thaler, Spence

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic is described in concrete organizational-portfolio terms (a failing project absorbed into a larger successful program to protect executive sponsors), but the underlying move — concealing a losing position by aggregating it with a winner so the average metric still reads green — is a general accounting and signaling pattern that recurs across finance, government program management, M&A, and any context where performance is judged at the portfolio level.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads as a corporate-portfolio survival move: an executive sponsor whose pet initiative is about to fail folds it into a larger, healthier program so post-mortem reporting shows blended performance, killing the discrete failure signal before it can be attributed to a person.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads as a general performance-laundering pattern: whenever evaluators use aggregated metrics, a losing unit can be hidden inside a winning aggregate, exploiting the coarseness of the measurement to convert a visible, attributable failure into an invisible drag on a still-positive total.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The protect-the-sponsor frame is the recognizable corporate instance, but the operating mechanism — coarse signaling and aggregation bias — is what does the work, and that mechanism reappears identically in fund-of-funds disclosures, government portfolio reviews, conglomerate quarterly reporting, and academic research-center metrics. The named context illustrates the move; the move is not bound to it.

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Quietly merge the failing project into a larger successful program before its kill-or-continue review, reporting blended performance
- **Option Y:** Let the failing project go to a standalone post-mortem where its results, cost overruns, and sponsor accountability are visible as a discrete line item
- **Context:** A senior executive sponsor whose flagship initiative is missing its targets faces an upcoming portfolio review; the project sits inside a multi-program structure where reporting is rolled up at the program level, and the executive controls the program-boundary definitions

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The dilution does not improve the failing project's actual or perceived merits — it doesn't make the project itself look better, only less visible. The tactic deliberately avoids any defense of the project on its own terms, because any such defense would re-isolate it as a line item. The absence of B(X)↑ is diagnostic: this is a concealment move, not a rehabilitation move.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Core lever. By absorbing the project into a successful program, the political cost to the sponsor of continuing it collapses — there is no discrete failure to be accountable for, no line item to defend, no kill-decision to be forced into. The sponsor pays no reputational price for a result that is now invisible inside a still-green aggregate.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Central lever. A clean post-mortem on the standalone project would have produced organizational learning, sponsor accountability, capital reallocation, and a clear kill decision — all benefits that dilution destroys by eliminating the discrete object to be reviewed. Y's payoff is gutted: you cannot post-mortem what no longer exists as a separable thing.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Once the projects are merged, anyone who wants the discrete failure visible must now perform forensic disaggregation — separating shared budgets, shared staff, shared milestones — which is expensive, politically aggressive, and easy to characterize as a witch-hunt. The cost of reaching Y is raised by the structural opacity dilution creates.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic deliberately omits any direct promotion of the failing project (B(X)↑ inactive) because doing so would invite the very scrutiny it seeks to evade. Instead, it lowers the cost of continuing/protecting the project (no discrete line to defend), destroys the benefit of accountability review (no discrete object to review), and raises the cost of disaggregation. This is the Resistance Triple signature — quiet facilitation plus full attack on the alternative without selling the preferred course on its merits.

**Effectiveness factors.** Effective when (a) reporting boundaries are sponsor-controlled or weakly governed, (b) the absorbing program has enough scale and slack to mathematically dilute the failure inside acceptable variance, (c) auditors evaluate at the program level rather than project level, and (d) the merger can be framed as a 'natural synergy' or 'consolidation for efficiency.' Fails when independent auditors hold pre-merger baselines, when the failure is large enough to drag the host program negative, or when a rival has incentive to pay the cost of forensic disaggregation.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Coarse Signaling (Spence) combined with Mental Accounting (Thaler) and Information Asymmetry (Akerlof's 'lemons' mechanism). When evaluators receive aggregated signals, sellers/insiders can pool low-quality items with high-quality items so the average obscures the floor; mental accounting then locks the evaluator into judging the bundle rather than its components.

**Decision Alteration.** Shifts the evaluator's decision from the question 'is this unit performing?' to 'is the aggregate performing?' — questions with different answers. The failing unit's signal is averaged against the winner's signal until the blended metric falls within the evaluator's acceptance range, and the evaluator's cognitive frame is anchored on the bundle as the unit of analysis. The decision to kill or hold accountable simply never gets cued, because the object that would cue it no longer appears in the evaluator's view.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when evaluation occurs at the aggregate level, when measurement infrastructure is built around the aggregate boundary (so disaggregation is non-trivial), when the absorbing aggregate has enough scale that one bad unit doesn't tip it negative, and when those with disaggregation power share interest in the aggregate's success (auditors paid by the audited, boards aligned with management, regulators captured).

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** An asset manager runs eight funds; two are deeply underwater. Rather than wind them down — which would trigger investor redemptions and a marketing disaster — management quietly absorbs them into the firm's flagship fund just before the annual prospectus is finalized, so the only public number is a healthy blended return

**Sample X:** Fund manager reports a blended quarterly return for the entire fund family

**Sample Y:** Fund manager discloses standalone returns for each fund, exposing two losing funds

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active in this configuration. The failing funds' underlying performance is unchanged; the tactic does not improve them, it only changes what gets reported.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Eliminates the redemption-trigger cost of continuing to hold the losing positions, because investors no longer see them as separable objects they could redeem from.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Destroys the benefit of capital reallocation that standalone disclosure would have produced — investors cannot pull from funds that no longer exist as discrete vehicles.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Forces any investor or analyst seeking discrete performance to reconstruct it from incomplete post-merger filings, a costly and often defeated forensic exercise.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A major defense acquisition has one subsystem badly over budget and underperforming on tests. Before the upcoming Selected Acquisition Report, the program executive officer restructures the WBS so the failing subsystem is absorbed into the platform's overall integration budget, and the failure becomes a line in 'integration risk' inside an otherwise-green program assessment

**Sample X:** A defense program office rolls a failing weapons subprogram into the broader platform program-of-record

**Sample Y:** Congress receives the failing subprogram as a discrete budget line and cancels it

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Not active. No claim is made that the failing subsystem has improved — the tactic specifically avoids defending it, since any defense would re-attract Congressional attention.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Removes the political cost to the PEO and prime contractor of defending a discrete failing line item in front of appropriators and the GAO.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Eliminates Congress's ability to perform clean cancellation, because there is no longer a clean object to cancel — killing the subsystem now requires killing or restructuring the larger program.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Raises the cost of oversight by forcing GAO or HASC staff into a multi-month forensic decomposition of the integrated budget to surface the failure as a separable item.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies in any context with three structural features: (a) the failing object can be administratively merged with a successful object, (b) evaluators read aggregated rather than disaggregated metrics, and (c) the actor benefiting from concealment controls or strongly influences the merger boundary. These conditions are present in corporate program management, fund administration, government acquisition, academic research-center reporting, university department mergers, NGO grant rollups, hospital service-line consolidation, and conglomerate segment reporting. Less effective in contexts with mandatory disaggregated disclosure (SEC fund-level reporting), independent auditors holding pre-merger baselines, or when the failing unit is too large to be hidden.

### Core Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A division president absorbs a failing product line into the company's strongest segment so the next 10-K's segment reporting blends the loser into the winner's revenue base.
- Government: An agency rolls a failed IT modernization contract into a larger enterprise transformation portfolio so the inspector general's audit finds 'mixed results' rather than 'failed acquisition.'
- Academic: A university research center facing a poor external review absorbs a failing lab into a successful interdisciplinary institute, so the next NSF site visit evaluates the umbrella entity's collective output.
- NGO/philanthropy: A foundation merges a failing grant program into its flagship initiative before the donor's five-year impact review, replacing two separate evaluations with one blended report.
- Healthcare: A hospital system folds an underperforming specialty service line into a high-volume department before the joint-commission performance review, so quality metrics report at the larger department level.
- M&A/diligence: An acquirer about to close a deal moves a failing subsidiary into the target's larger consolidated segment before the purchase-price allocation, so post-close earn-out calculations are based on blended segment performance the seller can no longer challenge.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific and generic analyses converge tightly on the Resistance Triple — both readings produce the same lever signature because the tactic's defining feature is the deliberate absence of B(X)↑. The actor never argues that the failing project is good; arguing would re-isolate it. This silence is not weakness, it is the signature: failure dilution is an evasion tactic, not a defense tactic, and that distinguishes it from sibling tactics in the same cluster like 'NOBLE FAILURE' NARRATIVE (which actively reframes failure as virtue, activating B(X)↑) or 'DEFINITIONAL RETREAT' (which redefines what success means, also activating B(X)↑). Dilution does not redefine success — it changes the unit of measurement so the failure becomes invisible inside a still-true measurement of a now-different object.

### Strategic Implications

Practitioners reading this tactic should recognize three things. First, the move requires control over reporting boundaries — the tactic is unavailable to anyone without administrative authority over the WBS, the cost-center map, the segment definition, or the program structure. This is why it is an executive tactic, not an individual contributor tactic. Second, the move trades latent risk for visible risk: the failing project still exists and still drags performance, but the drag is now structurally hidden, which means the eventual reveal (when it comes) is larger, sharper, and harder to contain than a clean post-mortem would have been. Third, the move corrodes the measurement infrastructure itself — once an organization tolerates one dilution, evaluators learn to discount aggregate metrics, which raises the cost of legitimate aggregated reporting for everyone. This is the long-run cost the tactic externalizes.

### Resolution Approach

Read through the specific lens when the question is operational — who can do this, when, and how to detect it inside a particular organization (look for late-stage WBS or segment-boundary changes ahead of review dates, especially ones that move underperformers into winners). Read through the generic lens when the question is structural — how to design measurement systems that resist dilution (pre-register evaluation units, lock baselines, require independent disaggregation, evaluate units below the aggregation level where dilution is happening). The defense against the tactic lives in the generic lens; the detection of the tactic lives in the specific lens.
