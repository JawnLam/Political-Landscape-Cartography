# 0091 — LEGACY DEMOLITION PLANNING

> Systematically dismantling the symbols, processes, and physical artifacts associated with the previous regime to erase organizational memory and reduce the desire to revert to old ways.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C75 — Historical Revisionism, Founder-Myth, Artifacts & Institutional Amnesia

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The tactic names a specific operational program (an actual demolition plan targeting buildings, logos, rituals, templates, intranet pages) but its underlying logic — extinguishing reversion to the old order by destroying its material and symbolic substrate — is a generic principle of regime consolidation that long predates corporate practice.

**Specific Interpretation.** Reads as a post-transformation or post-acquisition integration program in which new leadership inventories and systematically removes the predecessor regime's offices, branded artifacts, signature processes, and ceremonial rituals to suppress employee nostalgia and prevent informal reversion.

**Generic Interpretation.** Reads as the universal principle that collective memory is hosted in material and ritual substrates, and that destroying those substrates is more effective at extinguishing a prior order than arguing against it. From iconoclasm to monument toppling to renaming streets, victors have always known that material erasure outperforms rhetorical persuasion.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The corporate-integration packaging is contingent; the operating principle — destroying the artifacts that anchor practice and memory — is the load-bearing element. The mechanism survives translation to political revolutions, religious reformations, colonial regimes, and rebranded nation-states without losing its shape. The artifacts change; the logic of removing them does not.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Employees adopt the new regime's processes, tools, and identity markers as the default
- **Option Y:** Employees informally revert to legacy processes, tools, and identity markers when unsupervised
- **Context:** Post-transformation integration in a company where new leadership has formally adopted new branding, processes, and operating models, but the old physical campus, templates, rituals, and informal practices remain accessible and continue to be used by long-tenured staff out of habit and preference

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not make the new way more attractive on its own merits. It does not promote the new vision, sell its upside, or improve the employee experience of the new processes. Its entire signature is negative-space — it operates by subtraction, not addition. This absence is diagnostic: legacy demolition is the move of a regime that either cannot or will not win the affirmative case for change, so it removes the competing option instead.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

When the old templates, intranet pages, file servers, office layouts, and meeting cadences are dismantled, the new way becomes the path of least resistance by default. Employees who would have defaulted to the old workflow no longer have a working old workflow to default to. The new system wins not by being easier in absolute terms, but by being the only system that still exists.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

The old way loses its sensory and ceremonial reinforcement. Without the old logo on the lobby wall, the annual founder's-day picnic, the named conference rooms, or the legacy quarterly review template, the prior regime becomes increasingly abstract — a memory rather than a felt practice. Nostalgia decays when there is nothing material left to be nostalgic over.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Reverting to old practice becomes materially expensive: the templates must be recreated from memory, the legacy systems must be reinstantiated, the rituals must be re-organized without institutional support. What was once free (just keep doing what you did) now requires active reconstruction effort, often performed clandestinely against an official direction that has removed the alternative.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The configuration's diagnostic feature is the absence of B(X)↑. Legacy demolition is not a persuasion campaign — it does not sell the new way. It wins by making the old way progressively impossible to live inside while leaving the new way as the residual default. The C(X)↓ comes not from improving X but from removing the friction of Y; the B(Y)↓ comes from severing memory from its anchors; the C(Y)↑ comes from making reversion an act of reconstruction rather than mere continuation.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires formal authority to dispose of physical and digital assets, a defensible business pretext for each removal (lease consolidation, brand refresh, system migration, ESG sustainability mandate), and timing that spreads the demolition across enough discrete projects that no single removal triggers organized resistance. Fails when employees identify the pattern early and begin private archiving — once people start mirroring the old intranet to personal drives and photographing the building, demolition has slipped from elimination to mere inconvenience. Also fails when demolition is too rapid: visible scorched-earth removal triggers grief responses that ironically intensify attachment to what was destroyed.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Halbwachs's Collective Memory and Connerton's How Modernity Forgets — collective memory is not stored in heads but in material substrates (places, objects, bodily practices, ceremonies). Reinforced by environmental-cue habit theory (Wood & Neal) and Gibson's affordances: behavior is triggered by contextual cues, and removing the cues degrades the behavior more reliably than instructing against it.

**Decision Alteration.** Rather than persuading the actor to choose X over Y, the tactic dismantles the perceptual and material infrastructure that made Y available as a choice in the first place. The actor does not consciously decide against Y; Y simply ceases to be cued by the environment, ceases to be supported by working systems, and ceases to be remembered as a coherent option. Choice is altered by environmental amputation rather than argument.

**Optimal Conditions.** Works best when (a) the actor controls the physical and informational environment of the targets, (b) the prior order's identity is heavily anchored in tangible artifacts rather than abstract doctrine, (c) the removal can be staged across discrete legitimate-looking projects, and (d) there is no parallel preservation effort (museum, archive, alumni network) able to externalize and protect the memory the demolition is trying to extinguish.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A new revolutionary government takes power and undertakes systematic removal of the prior regime's statues, currency, flag, national holidays, street names, military uniforms, and official architectural style, replacing or simply demolishing each in turn over a five-year program

**Sample X:** Subjects of a new political regime accept the new national identity as their lived default

**Sample Y:** Subjects continue to identify with the displaced prior order and treat it as the legitimate one

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The demolition program does not, in itself, make the new regime more attractive. It does not deliver bread, peace, or prosperity. Any affirmative case for the new order is carried by other tactics; this one operates purely by subtraction.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — When the old currency, calendar, and signage are gone, using the new ones becomes the path of least resistance. Citizens do not have to choose the new — they have nothing else functional to use.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The prior regime's identity, drained of its material anchors, fades from a felt presence into an abstract memory. A child born after the demolition has never seen the old flag fly over a real building.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Loyalty to the old order increasingly requires active reconstruction — clandestine displays, private rituals, dangerous nostalgia — rather than passive continuation of what was already there.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A reforming religious authority, having declared certain images, relics, side-altars, and devotional practices unauthorized, undertakes a program of physical removal — whitewashing frescoes, removing statues, dismantling shrines, suppressing the prior liturgical books — across all houses of worship under its jurisdiction

**Sample X:** Worshippers practice religion in the reformed mode without unconscious recourse to prior forms

**Sample Y:** Worshippers continue to practice the prior liturgical and devotional forms alongside or instead of the reformed mode

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Iconoclastic demolition does not, by itself, deepen the spiritual appeal of the reformed mode. The theological case for reform is carried elsewhere; the demolition simply ensures the old forms have nowhere to live.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — With the side-altars dismantled and the prior books gone, worshippers default to the reformed liturgy because it is the only one available in the physical space.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The old devotional life loses its sensory richness — the candles, the statues, the smell of incense at a particular shrine — and degrades into a thinner, more abstract recollection.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Continuing the old practice now requires private hidden altars, smuggled books, and clandestine gatherings — recoded from devout continuity into culpable defiance.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable in any context where (a) one party controls the material and ceremonial environment of others, and (b) the prior order's identity is materially anchored. Documented across political revolutions (Jacobin de-Christianization, Soviet church demolitions, post-Saddam de-Baathification of public space), religious reformations (Byzantine iconoclasm, English Reformation altar dismantling), colonial regimes (renaming, replacing indigenous monuments), corporate post-merger integrations (campus consolidation, brand retirement), and family-business successions (renovating the founder's office). Versatility narrows when the prior order is primarily textual or doctrinal rather than material — book-burning is a different and more difficult tactic than building-demolition because text can be recopied from memory.

### Application Examples

- A post-merger integration team systematically retiring the acquired company's intranet, email domain, logo, conference-room names, and annual employee awards within twelve months of close, framing each retirement as a 'systems consolidation' project rather than a cultural erasure
- A revolutionary government renaming streets, removing statues of the prior monarch, retiring the old currency, and replacing the national anthem in a coordinated multi-year program
- An incoming university president closing the legacy faculty club, demolishing the building, and converting the site to a generic student commons — eliminating the physical venue where the prior administration's loyalists gathered and organized
- A military reformer disbanding the prior elite regiments, retiring their colors and battle honors, demolishing their dedicated barracks, and absorbing personnel into newly numbered units with no inherited identity
- A new megachurch pastor removing the founder's portrait from the lobby, retiring the original hymnal, renovating the sanctuary architecture, and discontinuing the annual founder's-day service
- A colonial administration replacing indigenous place-names with metropolitan ones on official maps, demolishing pre-colonial public buildings, and prohibiting traditional festivals in formal civic space
- A family-business heir, upon taking over from the founder, gut-renovating the founder's office, retiring the company's heritage product line, and replacing the lobby display of historic photographs with abstract art

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Legacy demolition is the operational counterpart to HISTORICAL REVISIONISM: where revisionism rewrites the narrative of the past, demolition removes the material substrate that would otherwise contradict the revised narrative. Revisionism tells employees that the old way was a failure; demolition ensures there is nothing left for them to look at that might disagree. The two tactics are most powerful when deployed together — narrative and material erasure reinforce each other, because each closes off one of the two channels through which collective memory normally survives. Distinguishes from AMNESIA ENGINEERING (its sibling in this cluster) in that amnesia engineering targets the cognitive and informational layer (records, knowledge holders, exit interviews) while demolition targets the physical and ceremonial layer (buildings, logos, rituals, templates). A complete erasure program runs all three in coordination.

### Strategic Implications

The diagnostic signature of legacy demolition is its absence of affirmative promotion — it is the tactic of a regime that chooses subtraction over persuasion. Practitioners should recognize three implications. First, the move is irreversible in ways persuasion is not: once a building is torn down or a template purged from systems, it cannot be undeleted by changing one's mind. This makes it useful when commitment-signaling is part of the goal, dangerous when the new regime might itself be displaced. Second, demolition without parallel affirmative tactics produces a hollow regime — employees default to the new way because nothing else exists, not because they believe in it, which leaves the organization brittle under stress. Third, the tactic's effectiveness depends critically on speed-throttling: too slow and resistance organizes around each remaining artifact; too fast and the visible scorched-earth quality triggers grief responses that intensify attachment to what was lost. The artisanal version distributes demolition across many discrete legitimate-looking projects (lease consolidation, brand refresh, system migration, ESG mandate) so that no single removal is the one that crystallizes opposition.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when planning or counter-planning an actual integration program with named artifacts, owners, and timelines — the lens forces the operational granularity (which templates, which buildings, which rituals, in what sequence) that abstract analysis elides. Use the generic lens when teaching the principle, recognizing the tactic across domains (someone running it on a state, a church, a family firm, or a unit), or designing the counter-tactic, which is always parallel external preservation — alumni networks, independent archives, private museums, photographed records — that externalize memory beyond the regime's reach to destroy.
