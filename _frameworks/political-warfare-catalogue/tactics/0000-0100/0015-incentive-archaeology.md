# 0015 — INCENTIVE ARCHAEOLOGY

> Uncovering the real (often hidden) incentive structures that drive behavior - not what the employee handbook says, but what actually gets rewarded or punished in practice.

## Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Akerlof's information asymmetry (the 'lemons' problem applied internally: front-line actors hold private knowledge about which behaviors actually pay, while decision-makers and newcomers operate on a degraded public signal — the official policy). Adjacent: Schelling's focal-point analysis, since the formal rules are a salient but false coordination point. / Festinger's cognitive dissonance combined with Kahneman/Tversky's availability and salience effects: surfacing the gap between espoused rules and observed reward patterns generates an unresolvable contradiction in the decision-maker's mental model, and the most concrete recent failures (vivid, available) carry disproportionate force in driving systemic redesign. Adjacent: Argyris & Schön's distinction between 'espoused theory' and 'theory-in-use' — the diagnostic vocabulary closest to what the tactic operationalizes.

**Decision Alteration.** Reveals the gap between stated and actual reward systems, enabling decisions based on real consequences rather than official rhetoric — converts private knowledge into public legibility so that downstream choices can be made against the true payoff matrix / This is a diagnostic tactic that precedes persuasion. The tactic itself isn't influencing front-line employees — it is influencing a decision-maker. It works by manufacturing a stark contrast between a leader's flawed mental model (based on formal rules) and the operational reality the workforce already knows. The exposure of this 'theory-in-use' creates cognitive dissonance and a powerful impetus for the leader to either change the system or publicly defend its current incoherence.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in organizations with significant misalignment between espoused values and actual practices, or where newcomers lack institutional knowledge but have the political standing to act on it once it is supplied / Most effective in organizations where there is a significant and costly gap between stated goals and actual outcomes, where leadership is insulated from ground-truth but receptive to data-driven arguments, and where the decision-maker has the authority to redesign incentives without a coalition fight

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A high-performing senior associate at a professional services firm, three years from partner consideration, deciding how to allocate the next 18 months of discretionary effort between behaviors the handbook celebrates and behaviors the last five partner classes actually exhibited

**Sample X:** Focus on metrics that actually drive promotions (like revenue generation or executive visibility)

**Sample Y:** Follow official performance criteria (like collaboration scores and 360-review ratings)

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Surfacing the actual partner-track behavior pattern (rainmaking, executive-sponsor cultivation, visible deal closes) reveals which behaviors X is composed of and makes their career payoff legible — the rewarded path becomes a named, observable thing rather than an inference

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Clarifies which effort is wasted, eliminating the time and emotional cost of pursuing performance criteria that don't actually count — the associate stops spreading effort across both systems and can commit to the one that pays

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Exposes that the official collaboration/360 criteria correlate weakly with promotion outcomes, draining the perceived career value of investing in them as anything beyond a hygiene floor

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Makes the opportunity cost of following only the official guidelines salient — every hour spent optimizing for the espoused criteria is an hour not spent on the actually-rewarded ones, and the archaeology converts this from a vague feeling into a quantified gap

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** An engineering director allocating Q3 capacity between two competing initiatives, who has just been shown by a peer that every architectural project promoted in the last six years lacked executive sponsorship and that none of them survived past the next reorg

**Sample X:** Invest the engineering team's quarter in a politically favored platform consolidation with executive sponsorship

**Sample Y:** Pursue a technically superior but politically unsponsored architectural refactor

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Demonstrates that political alignment, not technical merit, is the variable historically associated with project survival and the director's own career mobility — the sponsored path's benefits become not just real but the operative ones

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Reduces career and team-morale risk by aligning effort with the structures that actually protect work from being shelved — chosen work survives the next reorg, so it does not generate the hidden cost of being silently killed mid-stream

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Reveals that technical superiority, in this organization's revealed preferences, does not drive resource protection or recognition — the benefit the director thought Y carried (durable impact via merit) is shown to be ephemeral

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Exposes the hidden political costs of bucking informal power structures — peer engineers historically punished for sponsorless excellence become a named, cautionary dataset rather than a vague worry

### Representative Scenario 3

**Context:** An incoming Chief Revenue Officer presents findings to a CEO after a 60-day diagnostic showing that the top three deals celebrated at last year's sales kickoff carried negative gross margins and that the deal-engineering team has been quietly subsidizing them with implementation labor written off as 'investment'

**Sample X:** For a CEO to overhaul the sales compensation plan to reward gross profit margin and customer lifetime value

**Sample Y:** For the CEO to maintain the current plan that rewards top-line revenue and headline contract value

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Highlights that the new structure (X) will directly increase bottom-line profitability — the organization's true goal — by aligning rep behavior with the metric the board actually evaluates the CEO against

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The tactic does not make the process of overhauling the plan any easier. Compensation redesigns still require rep negotiation, finance modeling, and quarterly disruption — none of which the diagnostic alleviates. Its work is on the framing of why the cost is worth absorbing, not on reducing the cost itself

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Reveals that the perceived benefit of the current plan — high revenue — is a 'vanity metric' obscuring a lack of actual profit, so the case for keeping it loses the headline-growth rationale that previously protected it

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Makes the hidden costs of the current system salient: 'We are actively losing money and burning out our operations team to support these unprofitable deals' — costs that were absorbed across departments now get assembled into a single visible bill

### Representative Scenario 4

**Context:** A consultant shows a hospital administrator that the relentless focus on cutting overtime is linked to a documented rise in medication errors, a 19% turnover spike in the ICU, and three pending litigation matters whose discovery files reference staffing pressure

**Sample X:** For a hospital administrator to change nurse scheduling to prioritize patient outcomes and staff sustainability

**Sample Y:** For the administrator to continue scheduling nurses primarily to minimize overtime cost-line variance

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Demonstrates how the proposed scheduling change (X) will improve patient safety scores and staff retention — outcomes core to the hospital's accreditation and reimbursement model, not just its mission statement

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Does not reduce the complexity of creating a new schedule or the immediate budget hit from added headcount. The tactic operates on framing the comparison, not on subsidizing the redesign — the administrator still has to defend a larger nursing line item

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Diminishes the perceived benefit of the current system (Y) by showing that the money 'saved' on overtime is dwarfed by litigation reserves, recruiter fees, and reputation cost from publicly reported errors

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Amplifies the hidden costs of the status quo: 'The current scheduling practice is creating a high-risk environment and violates our duty of care to patients' — converting an abstract operational decision into a fiduciary and ethical exposure the administrator now owns personally

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applicable to any organizational or social system where formal rules differ from informal practices — corporate cultures, academic tenure committees, government promotion ladders, military fitness reports, religious denominational politics, and family business succession. The precondition is only that an espoused/operative gap exists and that someone can observe and assemble evidence of both / This diagnostic-to-persuasion method can be applied to any complex system where formal incentives may have created unintended and counterproductive real-world behaviors. Less effective where incentive structures are short-cycle and externally measured (e.g., trading desks, athletic leagues with public statistics) because the operative incentives are already visible and the archaeology has nothing buried to dig up

### Application Examples

- Corporate: A consultant uses internal-promotion data to show a board that the 'promote from within' policy is functionally rewarding political loyalty to a single senior executive over cross-functional competence — the board's stated meritocracy is a Potemkin facade for one VP's patronage network
- Product / engineering: A product manager demonstrates that the celebrated 'features shipped' metric is incentivizing engineers to create technical debt at a rate that has cut release velocity by 40% over two years — the headline KPI is eating the underlying capacity it was meant to measure
- HR / culture: An HR analyst reveals that while the company handbook promotes work-life balance, regression analysis of the last three promotion cycles shows after-hours email response time is the single strongest predictor of advancement — exceeding tenure, performance review scores, and project leadership combined
- Government / regulatory: A government auditor shows that a procurement policy designed to save costs is creating so much bureaucratic friction that the average contracting officer spends 70% of their time on documentation that delays projects at a price exceeding the cost it was meant to control
- Academic: A junior dean assembles tenure-decision data across three departments and demonstrates that publication count in two specific journals — neither named in the official criteria — predicts tenure outcomes far better than the published rubric of teaching, service, and 'impact'
- Sales / commercial: A new VP of Sales shows the CEO that the President's Club selection criteria, ostensibly based on quota attainment, have for five straight years preferentially selected reps from a specific region whose deals carry the company's worst retention rates — the celebration ritual is rewarding the wrong customer behavior
- Religious / nonprofit: A diocesan business manager shows the bishop that priests who consistently meet capital-campaign targets are more likely to receive prestigious parish assignments than those with stronger pastoral metrics — the formal vocation criteria are quietly displaced by fundraising performance
- Military: A staff officer demonstrates that officer evaluation reports, while formally emphasizing operational effectiveness, statistically privilege 'visibility moments' (briefings to general officers, joint-task-force assignments) over the troop-level competencies the doctrine elevates — the 'up-or-out' system is selecting for narrators over operators
