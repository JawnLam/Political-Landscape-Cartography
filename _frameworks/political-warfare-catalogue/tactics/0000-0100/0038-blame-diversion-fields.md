# 0038 — BLAME DIVERSION FIELDS

> Creating complex, cross-functional processes where accountability is so diffused that no single entity can be held responsible when things fail, protecting the orchestrators.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C26 — Scapegoating, Blame Architecture, Expendable Allies & Ghost Sponsorship

**Cited principles:** Coase, Darley & Latané, Darley, Latané

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The description names a concrete organizational design move ('complex, cross-functional processes') but the underlying mechanism — using structural complexity to defeat individual accountability attribution — is a general-purpose technique observable in regulatory architecture, military chains of command, financial structuring, and bureaucratic delegation.

**Specific Interpretation.** Analyzes a deliberate process-design choice during organizational construction or reorganization: whether to build cross-functional, multi-owner processes that diffuse accountability versus single-owner processes that concentrate it

**Generic Interpretation.** Analyzes the broader strategy of using structural complexity as an accountability shield — any system where decision authority can be distributed across enough nodes that no single node can be plausibly held responsible for outcomes

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** The cross-functional-process framing is one important instance, but the underlying mechanism — exploiting the cognitive and political difficulty of attributing responsibility within a sufficiently complex system — appears identically in military command-and-control failures, financial-engineering structures designed to defeat regulatory enforcement, and government delegation chains. The reusable principle is structural-complexity-as-shield, not any particular form of process.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Accept that 'the process' or 'the system' is responsible when failure occurs, with no single individual or function held accountable
- **Option Y:** Identify and hold a specific individual or function accountable for the failure
- **Context:** Post-mortem on a failed initiative inside an organization whose process was deliberately designed with cross-functional ownership, distributed approval gates, and committee-based decision rights

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The orchestrators rarely promote the diffusion as a benefit — doing so would expose the design choice. The benefit accrues silently as protection; it is not argued for.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Concluding 'the process failed' is procedurally trivial. No investigation is required to identify a specific culprit; no individual must be confronted, demoted, or fired. The diffuse-attribution conclusion is the path of least administrative resistance.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

When the structural design genuinely distributed authority across many actors, holding any single person accountable appears arbitrary and unjust. The benefit of accountability — restored confidence, learning, deterrence — is corroded because the assignment looks like scapegoating rather than justice.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

To name a specific accountable party requires building a case against the structural reality that no one had end-to-end ownership. Anyone insisting on individual accountability becomes the person trying to scapegoat within a system designed to make scapegoating look unfair — a politically costly position.

### Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The tactic is designed to absorb accountability pressure without ever advertising itself as such. Orchestrators do not promote diffusion as a virtue (no B(X)↑); they simply make the diffuse-blame conclusion easy (C(X)↓), undermine the legitimacy of individual accountability calls (B(Y)↓), and raise the political cost of insisting on them (C(Y)↑). The signature is exactly Resistance Triple: opposition-focused, never promotion-focused.

**Effectiveness factors.** Most effective when the diffusion was designed into the process before failure occurred (retroactive diffusion is recognizable as cover-up) and when no external party with strong investigative authority enters the picture. Fails when a determined regulator, prosecutor, or activist investor refuses to accept structural explanations and demands individual accountability — the tactic provides no defense against actors who reject the legitimacy of diffuse attribution.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Diffusion of Responsibility (Darley & Latané) operating in reverse — at the receiving end of accountability rather than the originating end of action. Combined with Coase's transaction-cost insight: when the cost of investigating and attributing responsibility exceeds the value of doing so, the attribution doesn't happen. Reinforced by Fundamental Attribution Error working in the actor's favor: complex systems make 'situation' explanations easier than 'character' ones.

**Decision Alteration.** Shifts the accountability frame from 'who is responsible' to 'what is responsible.' Forces the would-be enforcer to make a high-effort argument that any individual node in the network bears asymmetric responsibility — an argument that may be technically true but is politically and cognitively expensive to sustain.

**Optimal Conditions.** Complex environments with genuine cross-functional dependencies (so the diffusion looks natural rather than contrived); receivers with finite investigative capacity (regulators, boards, journalists who can't or won't trace deeply); cultures that valorize 'systems thinking' (which provides the rhetorical cover for diffusion); and time pressure on accountability decisions (deeper investigation defers attribution).

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** Financial services firm post-mortem on a compliance failure that triggered a regulatory inquiry; the underlying decision passed through risk, legal, compliance, and the business line, each with sign-off authority but none with end-to-end ownership

**Sample X:** Conclude that the regulatory violation resulted from 'gaps in inter-departmental coordination' with no individual accountable

**Sample Y:** Identify which specific executive's decision or inaction caused the violation

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The firm does not promote 'no one is accountable' as the desired outcome — to do so would invite escalated regulatory attention. The protective benefit is realized silently.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The conclusion 'process gaps' satisfies the procedural requirement for a post-mortem and produces a remediation plan (better coordination, additional reviews) — administrative closure without anyone being fired.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Naming a specific executive when four functions all signed off appears like inside-the-firm score-settling rather than honest accountability — the credibility of the attribution is corroded by the structural reality.

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Whoever pushes for individual accountability must argue against the documentary record that multiple functions had reviewed and approved — a position that looks vindictive and may damage the would-be accuser's standing with peer functions.

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A consumer-tech company conducting post-launch review of a flagship product that missed its quarter; the launch process required formal approval from four different VPs at three different gates

**Sample X:** Attribute the product-launch failure to 'cross-functional alignment issues' across PM, engineering, marketing, and sales

**Sample Y:** Hold the product lead accountable as the named owner of the launch

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — The CEO does not openly favor diffuse attribution; doing so would signal a leadership-accountability gap.

- C(X)↓ ✓ — The 'alignment issues' framing produces a clean narrative for the all-hands and the analyst call: structural improvements rather than personnel changes.

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — If the product lead is fired while engineering and marketing also missed commitments, the move appears scapegoat-shaped and damages the CEO's credibility with the remaining VPs (who notice that they too signed off).

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Holding the product lead accountable requires explaining why the other three VPs are not equally accountable — a discussion the CEO does not want to have publicly or with the board.

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Operates wherever a system's structure can be made complex enough to defeat attribution: corporate matrixed organizations, government inter-agency processes, military combined-arms operations, multi-tranche financial structures, healthcare team-based diagnosis, algorithmic decision systems ('the model decided'), and supply chains spanning multiple vendors. Less effective in small organizations where everyone knows who actually decided, or in jurisdictions where regulators have explicit authority to assign individual responsibility regardless of process design (e.g., Sarbanes-Oxley CEO certifications).

### Core Strategy

**#14 Resistance Triple** · `C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: Matrixed organizations where every consequential decision requires sign-off from 4+ VPs across functions, making post-failure attribution structurally difficult.
- Financial engineering: Multi-layer SPV and holding-company structures used by financial-services firms to ensure no single legal entity is the locus of regulatory liability.
- Government: Multi-agency program execution where DoD, State, and CIA each had partial roles, making accountability for a failed operation impossible to assign in a hearing.
- Military: Combined-arms doctrine creating ambiguity about which command authority bears responsibility for civilian-harm incidents.
- Healthcare: Team-based clinical-decision protocols where attending, resident, consulting specialists, and nursing all sign off — making malpractice attribution to any single physician difficult.
- Algorithmic systems: 'The model recommended' framings used by financial institutions and platform companies to defeat accountability for outcomes produced by systems whose builders and operators are deliberately separated.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific reading shows the tactic as a process-design choice with downstream consequences; the generic reading shows it as a general accountability-shield architecture. Distinguish carefully from SCAPEGOAT ARCHITECTURE (cluster sibling): scapegoating redirects blame to a chosen target and preserves the principle of individual accountability; blame diversion fields defeat the principle of individual accountability itself. The two tactics are sometimes used in combination — a diffused field protects most actors while a designated scapegoat absorbs the residual pressure when diffusion alone is insufficient.

### Strategic Implications

Detection requires asking 'who could have, with their authority alone, prevented this outcome?' If the honest answer is 'no one,' the structure was designed to produce that answer. Practitioners building accountability systems should look for the Resistance Triple signature: structures whose orchestrators do not advertise the protective function but whose post-failure dynamics consistently produce structural-blame conclusions. The absence of B(X)↑ (no one is promoting the diffusion) is the diagnostic tell.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when analyzing a particular organization's process architecture for accountability properties. Use the generic lens when analyzing a broader pattern — an industry's regulatory-shield design, a government's habitual use of multi-agency program execution, or a class of legal structures.
