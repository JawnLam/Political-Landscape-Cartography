# 0005 — SUCCESSION LANDMINES

> Unresolved leadership tensions, passed-over internal candidates harboring resentment, and potential sabotage risks during transition.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** ORIGINAL — —

**Cited principles:** Cialdini, Schelling, Williamson, Pfeffer

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** This describes both a specific organizational dynamic during succession transitions AND a broader principle of how resentment creates leverage through sabotage threats

**Specific Interpretation.** Focuses on concrete succession decisions where passed-over candidates pose specific risks to transition success

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents the general pattern of aggrieved parties leveraging accumulated resentment and inside knowledge to create credible sabotage threats

**Primary Classification.** specific

**Reasoning.** While the underlying mechanism applies broadly, the succession context is so specific and detailed that it primarily operates as a concrete tactical scenario rather than a general principle

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Appease, neutralize, or carefully manage the passed-over candidates (offer roles, concessions, severance packages, or other considerations)
- **Option Y:** Ignore or confront the resentful candidates and proceed with the transition without addressing their grievances or accommodation demands
- **Context:** A leadership transition period where internal candidates have been rejected in favor of another, creating a high-risk political environment for the incoming leader and the organization during vulnerable succession phase

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The tactic does not enhance the inherent benefits of appeasing the candidates; accommodation isn't portrayed as inherently valuable, just as risk mitigation and damage control. Concessions to a passed-over rival carry no positive halo — they read internally as protection money, not as a strategic investment, so there is no upside narrative for the tactic to amplify.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

There is no evidence that the cost of appeasing resentful candidates (e.g., severance, creating new roles, political capital expenditure) is reduced — accommodation still requires real resources, may appear as organizational weakness, and can embolden similar leverage plays by other aggrieved parties. The tactic's signature is that it makes the comparison so lopsided that the genuine cost of X becomes acceptable, not that X itself becomes cheaper.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The potential for sabotage and unresolved tensions directly undermines any perceived benefits of ignoring the issue. What would otherwise look like decisive leadership ('move on, don't reward sour grapes') gets pre-degraded by the landmine framing — the supposed benefits of toughness (cost discipline, signal of resolve, faster transition) are recast as naïve or short-sighted. The 'landmines' metaphor strips inaction of its claim to virtue.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Load-bearing lever. The description explicitly invokes 'resentment' and 'potential sabotage risks' from parties who possess Williamsonian asset-specific knowledge (vendor relationships, undocumented system dependencies, customer trust, technical workarounds) that the incoming leader cannot quickly replicate. Each piece of inside access becomes a Schellingian credible commitment to disrupt — the passed-over candidate doesn't need to threaten anything explicitly; their continued resentful presence is itself the threat, with the cost of ignoring them scaling with their tenure-depth and network reach.

### Strategy

**#6 Y Degradation** · `B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** This tactic functions by making the alternative to appeasement seem incredibly risky and costly. The 'landmines' inflate the perceived costs of ignoring the passed-over candidates while simultaneously negating any benefits of taking a hardline stance. Creates pressure to accommodate passed-over candidates by highlighting the hidden dangers and sabotage costs of proceeding without addressing their resentment during vulnerable transition period. The Y-degradation pattern is the signature: the tactic does nothing to make accommodation look attractive on its own merits — it works entirely by making the alternative look reckless.

**Effectiveness factors.** The effectiveness depends on the perceived power and influence of the passed-over candidates, their history of disruptive behavior, the organization's dependence on them, the incoming leader's ability to manage political threats, the credibility of sabotage threats (Schelling: a threat is only as good as the ability and willingness to execute it), the passed-over candidates' access to sensitive information (Williamson's asset-specific knowledge), and the organization's tolerance for disruption during succession. Fails when the incoming leader can credibly demonstrate that the passed-over candidates' supposed indispensability is overstated — typically by quietly building parallel capability before the transition is announced, or by accepting a one-time disruption cost as the price of refusing to be hostage to future leverage plays.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Pfeffer's analysis of organizational power as access to critical resources and information, combined with Schelling's credible commitment / credible threat theory and Williamson's transaction-cost framework around asset-specific knowledge and opportunism. The resentful insider's leverage is not abstract grievance — it is the credible ability to withhold, leak, or sabotage assets that the organization has not generalized away from them. Cialdini's scarcity principle reinforces the dynamic: the passed-over candidate's cooperation becomes valuable precisely as it threatens to become unavailable.

**Decision Alteration.** Forces accommodation by making the costs of ignoring aggrieved parties prohibitively high through disruption risks. The decision frame shifts from 'should we reward bad behavior?' (a moral framing the tactic would lose) to 'what is the operational cost of an unmanaged insider during a vulnerable window?' (a risk framing the tactic wins). The aggrieved party's resentment is converted from a personnel problem into a structural risk.

**Optimal Conditions.** When resentful parties have inside knowledge, positional access, and credible ability to cause damage during vulnerable transitions; when the organization's operating capacity depends on tacit, undocumented knowledge that has not been codified; when the incoming leader lacks the institutional time to build parallel capability before the threat ripens; and when third parties (boards, customers, regulators) are watching the transition and will read disruption as evidence of an incoming leader's weakness.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A new VP of Engineering restructures away the architectural authority of three long-tenured staff engineers in favor of a centralized platform team; those three engineers hold the only living knowledge of how the legacy billing system actually fails under load

**Sample X:** Give concessions to disgruntled senior engineers during a platform reorganization

**Sample Y:** Implement the reorganization without addressing the engineers' grievances

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Concessions aren't inherently beneficial here — keeping aggrieved engineers around carries its own ongoing cost in influence and morale. The tactic doesn't claim the appeasement is good, only that the alternative is worse

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Framing the concessions as 'transition stabilization' rather than 'capitulation to internal politics' lowers the political cost of making them — the VP can present accommodation as a pragmatic operational choice rather than a defeat

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The supposed benefits of a clean break — clear authority lines, signal that internal politicking doesn't pay — are pre-degraded by surfacing the operational risks of proceeding without the engineers' continued cooperation during the billing system's quarterly close

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Each engineer's asset-specific knowledge (Williamson) creates a credible threat: not loud sabotage, but quiet non-cooperation during the next production incident — leaving the new platform team to discover failure modes in real time, with customer impact

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A newly installed agency head launches a flagship regulatory initiative without involving the long-serving general counsel, who had expected to lead it; the general counsel controls the relationships with the regulated industry and the procedural memory of the last three failed attempts at similar reform

**Sample X:** Include the excluded committee chair in shaping the new policy initiative

**Sample Y:** Proceed with the policy initiative without their consultation

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Inclusion isn't promoted for its intrinsic value — bringing in the general counsel is not framed as a wise consultative move, only as the cost of avoiding obstruction

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Framing inclusion as 'leveraging institutional expertise' makes accommodation politically defensible and reduces the visible cost of incorporating the bypassed party — the agency head doesn't have to admit they need the general counsel, only that they value continuity

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — The headline benefit of bold independent action (decisive new leadership, fresh agenda) is undermined by the predictable narrative of 'inexperienced new head ignores institutional knowledge' that the general counsel can credibly seed without saying a word, simply by being visibly excluded

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Resistance can be quiet but devastating — slow-walked legal opinions, procedural objections at exactly the wrong moment, off-record industry signaling that the initiative is on shaky ground. Pfeffer's point: organizational power often manifests as the ability to delay and complicate rather than to block outright

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Applies across any organizational change where some parties feel wronged AND have asset-specific knowledge or positional leverage to disrupt — from corporate restructuring to policy initiatives to project assignments to coalitional politics. Less effective in contexts where the aggrieved party's knowledge has been codified or where their network has been deliberately diversified ahead of the transition — both of which neutralize the leverage at its source. The tactic generalizes wherever there is a gap between formal authority (which the incoming party now holds) and operational capability (which the aggrieved party still controls).

### Core Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

### Application Examples

- Corporate: Passed-over promotion candidates with deep customer relationships threatening to take key accounts to competitors unless given retention packages or alternative leadership roles
- Government: Career civil servants displaced by political appointees using procedural knowledge and regulatory relationships to slow-walk the new appointee's priorities, until accommodation is offered through advisory roles or face-saving redesignations
- Academic: A department's senior tenured faculty, passed over for the chair appointment, using their committee positions and grant-relationship networks to make the new chair's reform agenda costly in faculty meetings, until course load and resource concessions are extracted
- Family business: A founder-generation lieutenant displaced by an heir's professional COO hire leveraging long-standing supplier relationships and undocumented operating procedures to extract a paid advisory role and continued equity treatment
- Religious organization: A senior cleric bypassed for the bishopric using their network of parish loyalties and traditionalist alliances to make the new bishop's pastoral letter politically costly, until ceremonial roles and seminary appointments are restored
- Military: A senior officer passed over for theater command using their relationships with allied counterparts and inside knowledge of operational compromises to create reputational risk for the new commander, until a face-saving advisory or coalition liaison role is created

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The tactic works both as a specific succession risk management scenario and as a general leverage mechanism — resentful parties with inside access create asymmetric bargaining power through credible disruption threats. The succession context provides the clearest example of this dynamic in action, but the strategies differ meaningfully between the two readings: in the specific succession context, the lever configuration is pure Y Degradation (Strategy 6) because accommodation costs are not lowered, only the alternative is made unbearable; in the generic resentment-leverage pattern, the rhetorical work of reframing accommodation as 'pragmatic stabilization' does reduce C(X), pushing the configuration to Cost Contrast (Strategy 8). The same underlying tactic operates through slightly different lever combinations depending on whether the practitioner can deploy face-saving framing for the accommodation itself.

### Strategic Implications

Understanding both views reveals how organizational memory and relationship networks become weapons during transitions, with succession being the most vulnerable application point. For the practitioner-actor (the aggrieved party): the leverage is real only as long as the asset-specific knowledge stays asset-specific — visible threats accelerate the organization's incentive to codify, document, and parallelize that knowledge, so the tactic must be played quietly with implied threats rather than explicit ones. For the practitioner-target (the new leader): the only durable defense is built before the transition, not after — quietly diversifying critical relationships, documenting tacit knowledge, and pre-distributing access points so that no single aggrieved party holds enough leverage to extract concessions. For the practitioner-evaluator (boards, peers): the tactic is most reliably detected by watching the asymmetry of who 'must be accommodated' in a transition — when the answer reveals a single individual with unreplicated knowledge, the organization has been under-investing in knowledge codification for years.

### Resolution Approach

Classify as specific when analyzing particular succession scenarios and their unique political dynamics; recognize the generic pattern when identifying similar resentment-based leverage across other organizational contexts. The specific lens sharpens attention to the operational moves a passed-over candidate can make in the vulnerable window — selective non-cooperation, relationship signaling, procedural slow-walking — and the counter-moves available to the incoming leader. The generic lens clarifies the structural preconditions (asset-specific knowledge, undiversified relationships, vulnerable transition windows) that make the tactic viable in the first place, which is the more actionable knowledge for someone designing succession processes before the leverage matures.

### Tactical Synthesis

The specific succession scenario demonstrates the maximum intensity version of the generic resentment-leverage pattern, making it both a concrete tactical situation and a paradigmatic example of the broader influence mechanism. What unites the two readings is the analytical signature: the tactic does not promote accommodation as virtuous — it makes ignoring the aggrieved party look operationally reckless. The carrots are absent by design; the work is all done on the stick side.
