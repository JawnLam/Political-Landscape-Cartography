# 0033 — CULTIVATING DEPENDENCY

> Restructuring workflows and resource access so that critical functions become entirely dependent on a specific new system or control group, creating a chokehold that can be leveraged for compliance.

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** This describes both a specific organizational restructuring dynamic (workflows, resource access, control groups) AND a general strategic principle (dependency creation for leverage applicable across domains)

**Specific Interpretation.** Focuses on concrete organizational scenarios where workflows and resource access are restructured to create mandatory dependencies on specific systems or control groups, typically under the guise of centralization, standardization, or efficiency.

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents a broadly applicable strategy of creating structural dependencies to establish control and compel compliance — the deliberate engineering of chokepoints in any resource-exchange relationship so that the target's access to essential inputs is mediated by the actor.

**Primary Classification.** specific

**Reasoning.** The explicit organizational context (workflows, resource access, control groups) provides specific situational anchoring that shapes how the tactic is executed: it is operationalized through process redesign, IT consolidation, and committee architecture. The underlying dependency-creation principle has universal applicability, but the original framing is anchored in the moment when an internal actor uses organizational restructuring authority to redraw who needs whom.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Comply with the new system or control group requirements to maintain access to critical functions
- **Option Y:** Resist the dependency relationship or attempt to work around the new system
- **Context:** Organizational restructuring where critical workflows and resource access are redesigned to create mandatory dependencies on specific systems or control groups

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

New systems are pitched with legitimate-sounding benefits — efficiency gains, standardization, audit-readiness, improved coordination, single source of truth — so that compliance reads as endorsing modernization rather than capitulating to a control move. The benefit lever is real but modest; it operates as cover narrative more than as the load-bearing inducement.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Once dependencies are embedded, compliance becomes the path of least resistance: requests flow through the new system in a single click, while every off-platform request now requires escalation, justification memos, or special handling. The actor systematically removes friction from the sanctioned path so that even users who dislike the change find it easier to comply than to route around it.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Working around the system loses utility as legacy channels are decommissioned, integrations are severed, and parallel workflows are deprecated. The benefit of 'doing it the old way' — once preservation of autonomy and known reliability — evaporates when the old way no longer connects to anyone or anything downstream.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Resistance threatens access to essential resources: a department that refuses the new procurement system cannot procure; a manager who bypasses the centralized HR platform cannot promote or hire. Non-compliance is converted from a stylistic preference into an operational impossibility, and the cost is paid not by the resister's principles but by their team's ability to do work.

### Strategy

**#15 Full Spectrum** · `B(X)↑ + C(X)↓ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** Creates comprehensive utility manipulation by making the new dependency appear beneficial while systematically eliminating alternatives and making resistance both difficult and costly. The Full Spectrum classification is earned here because the tactic genuinely operates all four levers in sequence: the benefit narrative legitimizes the move, the friction redesign makes compliance trivial, the deprecation of legacy paths drains the alternative of utility, and the chokepoint architecture makes resistance operationally fatal.

**Effectiveness factors.** Success depends on (a) genuine control over critical workflows and resources, (b) ability to embed dependencies gradually so that no single change triggers coordinated resistance, (c) timing during organizational changes (post-merger, post-CEO arrival, post-audit finding) when restructuring seems normal rather than political, and (d) maintaining plausible efficiency or improvement justifications so that the move reads as good housekeeping rather than power consolidation. Fails when the dependency is imposed too fast (triggering visible resistance before lock-in), when the central system underperforms (giving resisters proof of concept for alternatives), or when an external authority — regulator, board audit, new CEO — re-examines the consolidation and breaks it apart.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

Both perspectives reveal this as a sophisticated structural control strategy that exploits resource dependency to create sustainable leverage over decision-making. The two readings converge on the diagnosis — the tactic operates by making the target structurally bound rather than psychologically convinced — but disagree on whether the C(X)↓ lever is genuinely active. The primary reading (Strategy 15, Full Spectrum) holds that compliance is made objectively easier through process redesign; the structural-power reading (Strategy 13, Y-Focused Triple) holds that compliance retains its costs and only the alternative is degraded. Both can be true at different points in the tactic's lifecycle: C(X)↓ operates strongly in the early adoption phase (when carrots are needed to onboard users) and fades once dependency is established (when sticks alone suffice).

### Strategic Implications

The dual perspective shows this operates both as a specific organizational restructuring tactic and as a fundamental strategy for establishing structural power through dependency relationships. The practitioner-actor takeaway: the tactic's durability comes from its invisibility — the dependency is built one efficiency justification at a time, so that by the time the target notices the chokepoint, it has become 'how we do things here.' Crude execution — visibly accumulating power, openly punishing resisters — converts the tactic from structural to political and exposes it to counter-coalitions. The practitioner-target takeaway: defenses must be deployed early, when consolidation is still proposed as efficiency. Once the dependency is operational, breaking it requires either an external authority (regulator, new CEO, board audit) or accepting a period of operational degradation while alternatives are rebuilt. The practitioner-evaluator takeaway: dependency tactics are most reliably diagnosed by watching what happens to the alternative paths, not the preferred one — the signature is not 'how attractive is the new system' but 'what happened to the old options.'

### Resolution Approach

Classify as specific when analyzing concrete organizational workflow restructuring where the operational moves (system rollouts, committee architecture, integration severance) are the load-bearing detail. Classify as generic when examining the broader strategic pattern of dependency creation for control — geopolitics, vendor lock-in, abusive relationships — where the structural mechanism transcends any single domain. The two strategy classifications (15 vs 13) reflect a real analytical disagreement about whether the tactic facilitates the preferred path or only obstructs the alternative; the honest answer is 'it does both, weighted differently across the lifecycle,' and practitioners should classify based on which phase of the dependency cycle they are observing.

## Dependency Cultivation Dynamics

**Structural Power Creation.** Establishes control through architecture rather than direct coercion, making resistance structurally difficult rather than politically costly. The actor's power is invisible because it is built into the topology of who needs whom.

**Chokepoint Strategy.** Identifies and controls critical bottlenecks in essential processes to maximize leverage with minimal control points — Williamson's asset specificity logic applied to organizational design, where the actor positions themselves as the indispensable specialized link in the chain.

**Gradual Implementation.** Often deployed incrementally to avoid triggering resistance, with dependencies deepening over time. Each step is individually defensible (this integration, that consolidation), and the campaign exists only in the aggregate — invisible to anyone who looks at moves one at a time.

**Alternative Elimination.** Systematically reduces or eliminates alternative pathways to essential resources or functions. The signature is what disappears, not what appears: legacy systems decommissioned, parallel vendors not renewed, off-platform integrations severed.

**Compliance Automation.** Creates self-enforcing compliance through structural necessity rather than active monitoring — Samuelson & Zeckhauser's status quo bias does the policing once the dependency is established, because switching now feels effortful even when it is objectively warranted.

**Lock In Effects.** Generates switching costs and network effects that make dependency increasingly difficult to escape over time — Williamson's transaction cost analysis predicts that asset specificity compounds, so the actor's leverage grows as more of the target's infrastructure is tuned to the dependency.
