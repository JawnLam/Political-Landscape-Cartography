# 0019 — PROXY WARS

> Subtly encouraging conflict between opposing internal factions to deplete their political resources and attention, allowing the main agenda to proceed with less scrutiny.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** ORIGINAL — —

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Olson, Edelman

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** Uses specific organizational terminology ('internal factions,' 'main agenda') but represents a fundamental divide-and-conquer strategy that applies across many domains beyond organizational politics

**Specific Interpretation.** Focuses on encouraging conflict between organizational departments, teams, or stakeholder groups to advance specific change initiatives

**Generic Interpretation.** Represents a broader strategic principle of creating proxy conflicts to distract opponents while pursuing your own objectives across competitive contexts

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** While framed in organizational terms, the core mechanism of using proxy conflicts for strategic advantage is a fundamental principle that extends across international relations, competitive business, politics, and negotiation contexts

---

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Allow the main agenda to proceed without organized opposition
- **Option Y:** Face unified resistance from coordinated opposing factions
- **Context:** Organizational transformation where multiple stakeholder groups might unite against proposed changes, requiring strategic fragmentation of opposition

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

The orchestrator's main agenda gains an indirect benefit boost — not because its merits grow, but because the fragmented attention environment means it advances under reduced scrutiny. Each faction, locked in a near-term resource fight, codes its own victory in the proxy contest as more valuable than the diffuse, harder-to-attribute benefit of joint resistance, allowing the main agenda to look like the path of least objection.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — Not active

The proxy conflicts themselves remain costly in terms of time and resources, and the orchestrator does not reduce the substantive cost of executing the main agenda — they merely shift attention away from it. The absence of C(X)↓ is diagnostic: this is a misdirection tactic, not a facilitation tactic. A move that genuinely lowered the cost of executing the agenda (e.g., budget pre-approval, fast-track committee structures) would be a different play.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — **Active** · intensity 2 (Moderate)

Organized resistance loses perceived payoff once factions are oriented toward each other. The collective-action benefit of opposing the main agenda — which Olson notes is already chronically undervalued by individual actors — becomes even more abstract relative to the concrete, immediate prize of beating the rival faction. United scrutiny stops looking like the rational play and starts looking like a benefit nobody around the table is willing to pay to organize.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Coordination becomes prohibitively expensive once trust between factions has been seeded with rivalry. Political resources and attention are depleted through infighting; any leader who tries to broker the cross-faction alliance must spend their own credibility to mend manufactured grievances before even reaching the substantive opposition agenda. Worse, attempting unification can be reframed by rivals as 'siding with the enemy,' converting coalition-building into within-faction reputational risk.

### Strategy

**#13 Y-Focused Triple** · `B(X)↑ + B(Y)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** This tactic operates by fragmenting opposition unity through strategic conflict encouragement, making proxy conflicts seem more valuable than unified resistance while systematically undermining both the attractiveness and viability of coordinated opposition. The C(X)↓ lever is conspicuously absent — the orchestrator never has to make their own preferred path cheaper, because the cost of resisting it has been redirected into intra-opposition combat.

**Effectiveness factors.** Success depends on existing tensions between factions (pre-existing rivalries are catalysts the tactic exploits rather than manufactures from scratch), the ability to subtly instigate without detection, availability of legitimate-looking points of contention so the fight reads as organic, and maintaining plausible deniability while opponents exhaust resources on internal conflicts. Fails when a respected cross-faction broker explicitly diagnoses the pattern in public, when the manufactured contention turns out to be too small for either faction to bother fighting over, or when external pressure (regulator, board, customer) forces consolidation despite the rivalry.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Olson's collective action problem combined with Tajfel/Turner in-group/out-group dynamics, sharpened by Schelling's coordination logic — the rival faction is a more salient and tractable focal point of conflict than the diffuse main agenda, so attention rationally collapses onto the proximate adversary even when the strategic interest favors coalition. Edelman's symbolic politics is the secondary mechanism: the proxy fight functions as theater that absorbs the political energy that would otherwise locate the real opponent.

**Decision Alteration.** This is a 'divide and conquer' strategy that manipulates opponent decision-making by reframing their primary threat. Instead of focusing on the orchestrator's agenda, factions are induced to see each other as the more immediate danger to their interests. The tactic exploits the fact that humans, per Tajfel, default to coding salient out-groups as threats and that collective action against diffuse harm, per Olson, is chronically underprovided. Finite resources — attention, time, political capital — get redirected to manufactured proximate conflicts and never reach the orchestrator's actual move.

**Optimal Conditions.** Most effective in environments with pre-existing rivalries (the tactic accelerates a fire rather than starting one from cold), low trust between groups, limited resources that can be contested, and when the orchestrator can control information distribution or resource allocation to fuel conflict while maintaining plausible deniability. Also requires an absence of cross-faction trusted brokers — figures with standing in both camps who could surface the pattern and re-direct attention.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** CEO wants to push through controversial merger (the 'main agenda'). To prevent powerful Sales and Marketing VPs from uniting against it, CEO announces only one department can receive coveted budget increase, forcing competition

**Sample X:** Two rival departments (Sales and Marketing) fight each other over limited budget increase

**Sample Y:** Departments unite and scrutinize broader, unpopular company-wide change

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Increases perceived benefit of factional conflict by making budget prize seem essential for winning department's success and competitive advantage; the merger advances under reduced scrutiny because the salient win-condition has been narrowed to 'beat the other VP for the budget'

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The conflict itself remains costly in terms of time and resources; the CEO does not subsidize the budget fight or the merger's execution — they merely redirect what the VPs spend their political capital on

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Diminishes perceived benefit of uniting against merger by making immediate, tangible budget fight seem more important than abstract, future threat of merger — collective-action payoff is structurally less salient than the concrete proximate prize

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any VP who proposes joint resistance must first concede the budget fight or be seen as 'soft' by their own department; the in-group cost of coalition-building rises sharply once the rivalry frame has set

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** Non-family CEO, whose contract is up for renewal, subtly leaks damaging information about each heir to the other, igniting personal rivalry to prevent united front against him

**Sample X:** Two rival heirs engage in public power struggle for family business control

**Sample Y:** Heirs cooperate and challenge authority of long-serving (non-family) CEO

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Frames infighting as the only path for one heir to definitively win control over the other, creating a highly motivating proximate prize that dwarfs the diffuse benefit of jointly negotiating the CEO's renewal terms

- C(X)↓ ✗ — The family conflict remains emotionally and politically expensive for all involved; the CEO neither funds nor cushions the fight — he merely supplies the spark

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Reduces perceived benefit of cooperation by sowing deep personal distrust, making a united front against the CEO seem fragile and contingent on a peace the heirs no longer believe each other capable of

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Amplifies cost of not engaging in the rivalry by making forbearance seem like a sign of weakness; in family-firm dynamics, the heir who proposes truce can be read as conceding succession, raising the per-unit cost of cooperation prohibitively

### Representative Scenario 3

**Context:** Dominant platform player in a fragmented developer ecosystem quietly signals favorable treatment to mid-tier developers willing to publicly criticize their direct competitor's tooling, ensuring no industry coalition forms to demand platform-wide reforms

**Sample X:** Gain market dominance despite competitor alliances

**Sample Y:** Face coordinated competitive response from industry players

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✓ — Each mid-tier developer codes 'preferred status with the platform' as a higher-value prize than the diffuse, hard-to-attribute benefit of an industry coalition negotiating better terms collectively

- C(X)↓ ✗ — Competitive battles between developers remain expensive in terms of engineering attention and reputational risk; the platform does not subsidize that combat, only directs it

- B(Y)↓ ✓ — Coordinated industry response loses appeal when companies are focused on relative position within the ecosystem rather than absolute terms with the platform — the per-firm payoff of an industry coalition gets discounted heavily against the immediate cost of antagonizing rivals

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Any developer who tries to broker an industry coalition becomes visible to the platform as a coordination threat and risks losing preferred-partner status — a cost the orchestrator never has to name explicitly for it to operate

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Timeless political strategy applicable in any situation with multiple power centers and finite collective-action capacity — from international relations (fueling regional conflicts to weaken neighbors), to corporate politics (department-vs-department resource wars), to family dynamics (sibling rivalries), to competitive markets (platform-vs-developer fragmentation), to legislative coalitions (whipping rival blocs against each other), to labor relations (inter-union disputes). The structural precondition is the same everywhere: two or more potential coalition partners, at least one underlying tension to exploit, and an orchestrator with enough information control or resource allocation power to amplify the rivalry without being publicly attributed. Less effective in tight, high-trust groups where a respected broker can quickly diagnose the pattern, or in contexts with hard external coordination forcing functions (regulators, customers, courts) that compel consolidation despite manufactured rivalry.

### Application Examples

- International relations: A major power discreetly arms two warring neighboring countries on opposite sides of a border dispute, keeping both weakened and dependent, while preserving the major power's regional hegemony and freedom to operate in adjacent theaters without coordinated regional resistance.
- Corporate strategy: A division head pits two ambitious subordinates against each other for a single VP promotion, ensuring both spend the next 18 months trying to outshine each other rather than questioning the division head's controversial restructuring plan that would consolidate their authority above both candidates.
- Political legislatures: A party leader publicly praises two intra-party factions for 'healthy debate' on a tertiary policy fight, letting endless caucus arguments absorb the rank-and-file's attention while the leader's signature legislative package advances through committee with minimal floor scrutiny.
- Regulatory capture: An industry incumbent encourages fragmentation among smaller competitors by selectively supporting one trade association over another on a minor technical standard, preventing the unified industry voice that would otherwise oppose the broader regulatory framework the incumbent has co-drafted.
- Academic politics: A dean fosters a turf war between two adjacent departments over a shared introductory course, ensuring both faculties exhaust their political capital on enrollment-count disputes rather than uniting against the dean's faculty-line-reduction plan affecting the whole college.
- Labor relations: A management negotiator quietly encourages procedural disputes between a craft union and an industrial union sharing the same plant, preventing the joint bargaining table that would otherwise force a uniform wage floor across both bargaining units.
- Religious institutions: A senior bishop facing a reform movement amplifies a long-running liturgical dispute between two reform-aligned parishes, ensuring the reformist clergy spend their synod time on liturgical procedure rather than the structural governance changes the bishop is quietly resisting.

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The tactic consistently operates by exploiting finite attention and the chronic underprovision of collective action (Olson) — redirecting opponent energy toward manufactured proximate conflicts rather than the diffuse, harder-to-coordinate external resistance, systematically fragmenting opposition unity through competitive reframing. The signature is structural: the orchestrator never has to lower the cost of their own move (C(X)↓ stays inactive), because the opposition's coordination cost has been raised so high that the path of least resistance for the agenda is just to keep moving.

### Strategic Implications

Understanding both applications reveals how proxy conflicts serve as force multipliers — a small investment in conflict instigation (a leaked detail, a contested scarce resource, a public asymmetric reward) generates disproportionate resource depletion among opponents while providing plausible deniability and maintaining focus on 'legitimate' competitive issues. For the practitioner-actor, the discipline is restraint in visible intervention: every nudge must be defensible in isolation, because the campaign exists only in the aggregate. For the practitioner-target, the durable defense is appointing a credible cross-faction broker early — someone with standing in both camps who can name the pattern before it consolidates — and treating any sudden offering of a contested scarce prize as a diagnostic signal worth interrogating before accepting the frame. For the practitioner-evaluator (boards, observers, journalists), watching where the orchestrator's attention is NOT — what they are not defending, not arguing, not contesting — is the most reliable detection method, because the tactic's effectiveness depends on the orchestrator's main agenda advancing under reduced scrutiny.

### Resolution Approach

Classify as generic when analyzing the reusable divide-and-conquer principle across competitive contexts; classify as specific when focusing on particular organizational political scenarios or internal faction dynamics. The two readings converge on Strategy 13 (Y-Focused Triple) and on the same diagnostic signature — the absent C(X)↓ lever, which marks this as a misdirection play rather than a facilitation play. What the specific lens adds is the named organizational players and the cadence of conflict-seeding inside a transformation; what the generic lens adds is the structural recognition that any system with multiple power centers, finite collective-action capacity, and an orchestrator with information-flow control is a candidate environment, regardless of domain.
