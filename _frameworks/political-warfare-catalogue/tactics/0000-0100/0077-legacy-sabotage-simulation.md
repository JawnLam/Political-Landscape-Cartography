# 0077 — LEGACY SABOTAGE SIMULATION

> Running predictive wargames to anticipate how defenders of the old system might undermine the new one, and pre-emptively building countermeasures into the transformation plan.

**Type:** `hybrid` · **Strategy:** **#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑` · **Cluster:** C79 — Intelligence Gathering & Loyalty Mapping

**Cited principles:** Schelling, Janis

---

## Classification Rationale

**Ambiguity Source.** The label 'legacy sabotage' anchors the tactic in a particular organizational situation (transformation under threat from incumbents), but the underlying method — adversarial wargaming to anticipate and pre-empt attacks — is a generic strategic discipline drawn from military red-teaming and scenario planning.

**Specific Interpretation.** Inside a transformation program, leaders convene structured simulations in which trusted insiders role-play the saboteurs (mid-managers losing scope, function heads losing headcount, veteran experts whose tacit knowledge is being displaced) and rehearse the obstruction moves they would plausibly make. The transformation plan is then hardened against those specific moves before rollout.

**Generic Interpretation.** The general pattern is the deliberate pre-mortem: simulate the most credible adversarial response to your plan before you commit to it, treat the simulation outputs as design constraints, and bake counter-moves into the plan itself. This is red-team thinking applied prospectively to any contested initiative.

**Primary Classification.** generic

**Reasoning.** Although the title frames it as a transformation-defense move, the mechanism — Klein's pre-mortem combined with Schelling-style anticipation of strategic response — is reusable in any domain where a planned change will be opposed by parties with the means and motive to obstruct. The transformation context is the most vivid instance, not the boundary of the tactic.

## Specific Analysis

### Choice Architecture

- **Option X:** Launch the transformation with sabotage countermeasures pre-built into the sequencing, governance, and communications plan
- **Option Y:** Launch the transformation on its native logic and respond reactively as legacy defenders begin to obstruct
- **Context:** A PE-backed or board-mandated transformation in a mature company where the old operating model still has powerful constituencies — function heads, plant managers, long-tenured engineers, key customer-facing veterans — whose status and rents the transformation will erode

### Active Levers

#### B(X)↑ — Amplify benefit of X — Not active

The simulation does not make the transformation itself more attractive on its merits — the value proposition of the new operating model is unchanged. The tactic operates on the execution probability, not on the prize.

#### C(X)↓ — Reduce cost of X — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

This is the dominant lever. By rehearsing the saboteurs' playbook before rollout — the budget ambushes, the malicious-compliance edge cases, the customer-escalation theatrics, the leaks to the trade press, the quiet talent flight — the transformation team buys down its execution risk: it pre-stages the counter-narrative, it sequences decisions so the obstructionist's strongest move arrives after the team's defenses are already in place, and it eliminates surprise as the saboteur's most valuable resource.

#### B(Y)↓ — Diminish benefit of Y — Not active

The tactic does not operate by making the old operating model look less appealing or by attacking the legitimacy of the legacy. It is silent on Y's merits; it simply assumes Y's defenders will fight and prepares for that fight.

#### C(Y)↑ — Amplify cost of Y — **Active** · intensity 3 (Intense)

Pre-built countermeasures raise the political cost of sabotage itself: when an anticipated obstruction move is met with a calm, pre-scripted response (the documented decision rationale, the alternative supplier already qualified, the redundant signoff path already approved), the saboteur is exposed as predictable and incurs reputational damage. The first failed sabotage attempt makes the second one look reckless rather than principled.

### Strategy

**#8 Cost Contrast** · `C(X)↓ + C(Y)↑`

**Rationale.** The simulation does not change the underlying attractiveness of the transformation or the legacy — it changes the relative cost of executing each. By stripping surprise from the saboteur's arsenal and pre-positioning defenses, it makes the transformation cheaper to run and makes the saboteur's available moves more expensive to attempt. It is a pure asymmetric-cost play.

**Effectiveness factors.** Requires (1) brutally honest role-players — usually insiders who genuinely understand the legacy's grievances, not consultants improvising villains; (2) leadership willingness to redesign the plan in response to the simulation, not just check the exercise off; (3) operational security around the simulation itself, since leaks signal distrust and can themselves provoke the sabotage being modeled; (4) a sequencing window in which countermeasures can still be built in before rollout commits. Fails when the exercise becomes performative or when the team confuses naming the threat with neutralizing it.

## Generic Analysis

### Influence Mechanism

**Psychological Principle.** Klein's Pre-Mortem (prospective hindsight) combined with Schelling's anticipation of strategic response and Janis's groupthink-immunization. The pre-mortem flips the frame from 'plan and defend it' to 'assume it failed — explain how,' which empirically surfaces failure modes that ordinary planning suppresses. Schelling's contribution is the insistence that one's plan must be designed against the rational best response of an opponent who is also strategizing.

**Decision Alteration.** Does not directly alter any single target's choice; instead, alters the deployer's own plan such that the targets' best obstruction moves are anticipated and absorbed at lower cost than reactive defense would incur. The decision being altered is the deployer's prior commitment to a brittle plan — the simulation forces the plan to evolve before it is exposed.

**Optimal Conditions.** High-stakes, contested initiatives where opposition is rational, organized, and has discretion in how it resists. Most powerful when the simulation can be conducted in secret, when the planning team has the authority to incorporate findings, and when the obstruction repertoire of opponents is large enough that pre-mortem yields non-obvious moves. Less useful when opposition is irrational, when surprise is already lost, or when the plan cannot be modified at the simulation stage.

### Representative Scenario 1

**Context:** A health-system CMO preparing to roll out a single evidence-based protocol across formerly autonomous medical groups, knowing that influential department chairs will resist on grounds of clinical judgment and academic freedom

**Sample X:** Launch a clinical-pathway standardization across a multi-hospital system with pre-built responses to physician-led obstruction

**Sample Y:** Launch the standardization on its clinical merits and address physician pushback as it surfaces

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Does not change the clinical evidence base supporting the protocol

- C(X)↓ ✓ — By rehearsing the chairs' objections in advance — 'this protocol doesn't account for my patient population,' 'the evidence is from a different setting,' 'this is cookbook medicine' — the CMO pre-stages the data, the carve-outs, and the physician-champion endorsements that defuse each objection before it can crystallize into organized resistance

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Does not attack the status of clinical autonomy as a value

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — When a chair raises an anticipated objection and the CMO has the published response ready, the chair is exposed as having raised a known and answered concern, raising the reputational cost of further obstruction within the medical staff

### Representative Scenario 2

**Context:** A military service introducing a new combined-arms maneuver doctrine, using its opposing-force units to play the adversary's general staff and probe the doctrine's seams in simulated campaigns before fielding

**Sample X:** Deploy a new infantry doctrine after rehearsing the enemy's most credible counter-doctrine in force-on-force exercises

**Sample Y:** Deploy the new doctrine and adapt to the enemy's counter-moves in contact

**Template levers:**

- B(X)↑ ✗ — Does not increase the doctrine's intrinsic battlefield effects

- C(X)↓ ✓ — Each simulated defeat in the wargame surfaces a doctrinal seam that can be closed before the doctrine meets a real adversary, dramatically lowering the cost of fielding it for the first time

- B(Y)↓ ✗ — Does not diminish the appeal of the doctrine being replaced

- C(Y)↑ ✓ — Forces the adversary to either accept that their best counter-move is now anticipated, or to commit to a worse counter-move whose downside is itself wargamed

### Versatility

**Level:** high

**Explanation.** Works wherever (a) a deployer is preparing a contested initiative, (b) opponents have discretion in how they resist, and (c) there is a planning window before commitment. Strong fit in military doctrine, corporate transformations, regulated-product launches, election campaigns, security architecture (purple-teaming), and policy rollouts. Less powerful when opposition is genuinely irrational or random, when surprise has already been lost to leaks, or when the plan is locked and cannot absorb the simulation's findings — in those cases the exercise produces insight without the authority to act on it.

### Application Examples

- Corporate transformation: A new CEO's first 100-day plan is wargamed by a small inner circle role-playing the displaced COO, the regional sales barons, and the CFO, surfacing the budget freezes and procurement slow-walks that would otherwise have derailed Q2
- Military: NATO's Cold War exercises that scripted Warsaw Pact countermoves to NATO doctrine refinements, allowing doctrinal seams to be closed in peacetime
- Cybersecurity: A purple-team exercise where the blue team's incident-response plan is stress-tested by a red team explicitly licensed to use the most credible attacker techniques before the plan is canonized
- Election campaigns: A presidential campaign's October surprise drill, in which staffers role-play opposition research drops and rehearse 24-hour response protocols for each plausible attack before the events themselves occur
- Regulatory affairs: A pharmaceutical sponsor pre-mortems an FDA advisory committee meeting by having internal scientists role-play the most skeptical committee members and rehearse responses to their likely lines of questioning
- Policy rollout: A finance ministry preparing a tax reform convenes a closed exercise in which trusted economists role-play the affected industries' lobbyists and surface the carve-out demands and parliamentary procedural blocks the ministry will face, allowing the bill text to be hardened before introduction

## Integrated Insights

### Pattern Recognition

The specific and generic readings converge on the same lever signature — pure Cost Contrast (8) — which is itself the diagnostic insight: this tactic does not try to win the argument or sweeten the prize, it merely lowers the cost of executing the plan and raises the cost of obstructing it. Every variant of this tactic across domains operates on the cost dimension of both choices, never on the benefit dimension. That single-axis discipline is what distinguishes it from adjacent tactics like crisis manufacturing (which inflates the cost of inaction) or coalition-building (which boosts the benefit of joining).

### Strategic Implications

The practitioner takeaway: the value of a pre-mortem is captured at the moment the plan is redesigned in response to it, not at the moment the simulation is held. Most legacy-sabotage simulations fail not because they fail to surface the right threats, but because the planning team treats them as a confidence-building exercise rather than a design-constraint exercise. The discipline is to commit, before the simulation, that any obstruction move surfaced will be either neutralized in the plan or explicitly accepted as residual risk with named ownership. Without that pre-commitment, the simulation produces insight without authority and is worse than not running it, because it gives the team false comfort that the threats have been 'considered.' A second implication: this tactic compounds with intelligence-gathering tactics in its cluster — the better the loyalty mapping and the better the read on who has motive to sabotage, the more credible the simulated saboteurs become and the more useful their pre-mortem moves are.

### Resolution Approach

Use the specific lens when working a named transformation and the saboteurs are identifiable individuals whose grievances and capabilities can be modeled concretely; the specific frame disciplines the team to name the human, not the archetype. Use the generic lens when designing the planning process itself or when teaching the discipline to a new team; the generic frame makes the method portable to the next contested initiative without dragging the transformation context along.
